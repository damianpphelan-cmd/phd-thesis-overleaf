# The thesis style guide

The register: an applied paper in the Journal of Public Economics or
Economics of Education Review (supervisor's instruction, 16 Aug 2026),
written in Damian's own sentence-level voice (his 2020/2021 dissertations
and 2022 upgrade report). Structure follows the economics-paper register;
sentences follow his voice. British English. Precision and honesty are
content, not style: every number derived by macro, every limitation
stated. The rules govern how things are said, never whether.

Rules carry stable IDs (B1, C2, D4...) so assessments can cite them.
Provenance: B* from the supervisor (16 Aug 2026); C*/D* largely from
Damian (24 Aug 2026); E7-E12 from Damian (25 Aug 2026); the voice notes
from his writing samples (25 Aug 2026).

---

## A. The one-sentence version

State what was done and what was found, one point per paragraph, one idea
per sentence, in plain declarative English, for a reader who knows
nothing about the project — and stop.

---

## B. Structure rules (paper level)

- **B1. Five sections, few subsections.** Introduction, Data, Measurement
  (or Empirical Specification), Results, Conclusion.
- **B2. Zero `\cref` to sections.** `\cref` only to tables, figures,
  equations and appendix sections. If a sentence must point to another
  section, the material is in the wrong place: move it or cut it.
  Signposting ("this section describes... there are seven parts") is a
  symptom, not a cure.
- **B3. State each thing once.** An important concept appears at most
  three times: previewed in the introduction, established in the body,
  restated in the conclusion. Everything else appears once. A paragraph
  that restates what an earlier paragraph established is cut.
- **B4. No history, no diary.** What was done and why — never what was
  tried and dropped, what an earlier draft said, or what comes later.
  Rationales yes; the biography of the analysis no. (Banned strings in
  Appendix G.)
- **B5. Length is a constraint.** 35 pages per chapter including body
  tables and figures; the appendix is not bloated to compensate.
- **B6. The linear story.** Every section serves it: detailed data on a
  subset of schools → a gold-standard measure built from it → for the
  other schools, a prediction from what is observable → evidence the
  prediction reproduces the gold standard where both exist → facts about
  the measures.

## C. Paragraph rules

- **C1. One point per paragraph, stated in its first sentence.** The
  most-violated rule after the reviewer rounds; check it first.
- **C2. Patient sequencing.** A paragraph walks A to B to C in order and
  does not loop back; each sentence advances one step.
- **C3. The stranger test.** Could a reader who knows nothing about the
  project read the paragraph once and say what was done and what was
  found? If not, simplify or cut.
- **C4. No process narration.** No decision histories, no "we first tried
  X", no rejected alternatives — unless a failed attempt IS the finding,
  in which case state the finding, not the story. One sentence of
  motivation per method. Grainy operational detail (thresholds, file
  mechanics, tuning history) goes to an appendix or nowhere.
- **C5. Tables carry the detail, prose carries the argument.** A number
  appears in prose only when it carries the argument; the rest live in
  tables.
- **C6. No paragraph ends on a clipped fragment** ("It is not." / "That
  check has no power here."). Finish the sentence.

## D. Sentence rules

- **D1. One idea per sentence; most sentences under about 25 words;**
  never more than two subordinate clauses. A sentence that needs a second
  reading is split or shortened.
- **D2. Subject early, verb close behind it.** No delayed openings ("It
  is worth noting that...", "It should be stressed that...", "In terms
  of..."). This overrides the occasional "It should be noted" in the
  writing samples.
- **D3. Plain connectives carry the logic.** Sentences open with
  "However,", "Therefore,", "Consequently,", "For example,", "As a
  result" — not with dashes, semicolon splices, or participle phrases. A
  contrast may end with "though". Dashes are not a habit.
- **D4. A number lives inside a sentence that states its meaning** ("a
  man who has completed tertiary education will live on average eight
  years longer") — never a bare parenthetical statistic bolted into a
  claim.
- **D5. A number and its caveat get separate sentences.** Never nest the
  caveat mid-clause.
- **D6. Active voice** unless the actor is genuinely irrelevant. Third
  person, or authorial "I" used sparingly and consistently.
- **D7. No rhetorical questions, no aphorisms, no italicised emphasis
  for effect.**

## E. Word and phrase rules

- **E1. Technical words keep their technical meaning.** "Convergence"
  only in its econometric sense; for agreement between measures write
  "correlation" or "agreement". "Validate" says what was tested against
  what; "robust" says to what; "significant" says at what level;
  "identify" takes an effect, not a pattern; a model "predicts", a
  correlation does not.
- **E2. Plain words over grand ones.** "Use" not "utilise", "show" not
  "demonstrate", "because" not "in consequence of the fact that".
  Technical terms are fine; ornamental abstraction is not.
- **E3. Explain, then name.** A term is defined in ordinary words at
  first use, in line: "referred to as X", "meaning Y".
- **E4. One name per thing, announced once.** "From this point on the
  term academies will be used to refer to both academies and free
  schools" — then the name never varies. No elegant variation: the
  warmth measure is not also "the instrument", "the reading", "the
  signal" and "the leg" in one section.
- **E5. At most one live metaphor per concept, defined at first use,**
  reused consistently, never stacked.
- **E6. Concrete subjects.** Not "the picture", "the story", "the
  pattern" doing argumentative work — name the coefficients, the
  correlations. "The results suggest" is fine; "the chapter argues",
  "the table reveals", "the data tell us" are not.
- **E7. The antithesis tic.** "Not X but Y" / "X, not Y" as a sentence
  shape: one per chapter at most, never twice in a section.
- **E8. No self-praise of candour.** Never "reported honestly", "and
  honestly so", "an unusually honest...". State the adverse result; the
  honesty is the content, not a label.
- **E9. Rule-of-three.** Triadic parallel lists must not be the default
  rhythm; break the pattern.
- **E10. No importance adverbs.** "Notably", "crucially", "importantly",
  "strikingly", "genuinely", "clearly" assert what the evidence should
  show; delete them.
- **E11. No inflated copulas.** "Serves as", "stands as", "represents",
  "underscores", "highlights", "speaks to" → "is", "shows", "supports".
- **E12. No meta-announcements with "follow"/"remain"/"emerge".** Not
  "Two further limitations follow." / "One caveat remains." Damian's own
  form announces a list plainly and then walks it: "There are two further
  limitations. The first is... The second is...".

## G. Grep-able banned strings

For the mechanical screen. Case-insensitive; flag every hit for review
(a few have legitimate technical uses — judge in context).

B4 (diary): "an earlier version", "previously", "was withdrawn",
"superseded", "is not yet", "will be", "as we shall see",
"documented below", "documented above"

D2 (delayed openers): "It is worth noting", "It should be noted",
"It should be stressed", "In terms of"

E2 (grand words): "utilise", "demonstrate", "in consequence of"

E8 (candour): "honestly", "candid", "unusually honest"

E10 (importance adverbs): "notably", "crucially", "importantly",
"strikingly", "genuinely"

E11 (copulas): "serves as", "stands as", "underscores", "highlights",
"speaks to", "is a testament"

E6 (containers): "the picture", "the story", "tells us", "the chapter
argues", "the table reveals"
