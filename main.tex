\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

This thesis asks whether the organisational culture of a school---the relational
and behavioural environment its pupils experience day to day---can be measured
credibly at scale, and whether it predicts how much pupils learn.

The first paper develops the measurement framework. Drawing on 304 headteacher
interviews and 103 structured researcher visits to English secondary schools, it
separates culture into two dimensions: warmth, the relational quality of the
school environment, and strictness, its behavioural structure. Gold-standard
scores are constructed for 102 schools observed directly, and Ridge models
calibrated on those schools extend measurement first to the interview sample and
then, using text from Ofsted reports and behaviour policies, to the national
population of approximately 3,330 schools. The central finding is an asymmetry.
Strictness is recoverable from public text: LLM-scored Ofsted strictness
correlates at $r = 0.418$ with directly observed strictness. Warmth is not,
in any public source---Ofsted reports, behaviour policies or school websites---and
the null survives a more capable scoring model. What schools say about their
warmth is systematically unrelated to what an observer sees.

The second paper uses these scores to estimate the association between culture
and pupil progress. For the 2023--24 cohort, both warmth and strictness predict
Progress~8 with near-equal magnitude ($\hat{\beta}_W = 0.127$,
$\hat{\beta}_S = 0.120$), conditioning on prior attainment, pupil composition,
school type and pre-COVID Ofsted grade. Adding directly observed teaching quality
leaves both coefficients essentially unchanged, indicating that culture and
instruction operate through largely distinct mechanisms. Substituting
headteacher self-reports for observed scores reduces the warmth coefficient by
63--106~per~cent---the measurement asymmetry of the first paper reappearing as a
predictive one.
