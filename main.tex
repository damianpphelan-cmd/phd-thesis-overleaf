\subsection*{Part B: Declaration of contribution from other sources}

\paragraph{Data sources, if not collected yourself.}
Chapters~2 and~3 use published Ofsted inspection reports, school
behaviour policy documents and school websites (all publicly
available), the Department for Education's Get Information About
Schools register, and published school performance data. The
gold-standard school visit and headteacher interview data were
collected by the candidate with a team of research assistants. Chapter~4 uses the Longitudinal Education
Outcomes dataset, accessed through the ONS Secure Research Service,
and published lifetime-returns estimates from Britton, Ogden, Ornadel
and Waltmann (2026).

\paragraph{Software code, instrumentation development, etc.}
In Chapters~2 and~3, large language models are used as measurement
instruments to score school documents; this methodological use,
including the exact prompts and validation against the gold standard,
is documented in full in those chapters. AI assistance with the
analysis code is declared under AI below.

\paragraph{AI.}
Generative AI (Claude, Anthropic) was used throughout the preparation
of this thesis. It assisted with revising prose, writing analysis code
in Python and Stata, preparing tables and figures, and checking the
internal consistency of the numbers reported. All of this work was
directed by the candidate, and every analytical decision,
interpretation and piece of final wording was reviewed and approved by
the candidate. For Chapter~4, the analysis was carried out by the
candidate and co-authors inside the ONS Secure Research Service, where
AI tools are not available. AI assistance for that chapter was
therefore confined to revising the prose and preparing the tables and
figures for the thesis.

\paragraph{Other.}
Not applicable beyond the contributions declared above.

\paragraph{Publications.}
All three papers are unpublished working papers. Chapter~4 is joint work
with Arun Advani and Elaine Drayton; the candidate's contribution is
stated in the Research Paper Declaration Form.

\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

This thesis measures two features of English secondary schools: their
culture, and whether they offer a high-return subject.

The first paper measures culture. A research team that I led visited
\NTierOne{} schools. Each visit combined a day of observation with a
headteacher interview, covering how warm and how strict the school is. Every school's inspection report, behaviour policy and
website was then scored by a prediction model and by a language model
applying a mark scheme. Scores from inspection reports agree modestly with
the visit scores. Warmth is predicted from a report's ordinary wording.
Strictness is found only by a language model applying
a mark scheme that says which statements about behaviour to look for. What
a headteacher says about the school agrees only weakly with what observers
find, and a school's own documents agree with the head rather than the
observers.

The second paper relates the visit scores to Progress~8. A standard
deviation of warmth is associated with \PrimarySpecWSD{} of a standard
deviation of Progress~8, and a standard deviation of strictness with
\PrimarySpecSSD{}. Headteachers' own ratings of strictness predict progress
as well as the observers' scores do. Their ratings of warmth do not predict
progress. The inspection-report scores exist for nearly every school in
England, and the same models estimated with them give the same pattern.

The third paper, joint with Arun Advani and Elaine Drayton, shows that a
school offers A-level economics only if it employs a suitably qualified
teacher, and that during the period we study fewer than
half of state schools did. When a school starts offering the subject,
take-up rises by 5.7 percentage points, and taking it raises earnings at
age 28 by around 14\%. The extra tax raised from those earnings
exceeds the cost of employing the teacher.
