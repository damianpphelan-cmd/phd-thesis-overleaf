\begin{chapterabstract}
School culture is widely held to matter for what pupils learn, but it is
rarely measured. This paper constructs measures of two dimensions of the
culture of English secondary schools, warmth and strictness, and asks how far
each can be recovered from data that exist for every school. For \NTierOne{}
schools a research visit and a headteacher interview yield a gold standard in
two parts, an enacted score from observation and an espoused score from the
head's own account; inspection reports, behaviour policies and websites are
scored for the national population by language-model instruments, and
prediction models fitted where both exist carry the gold standard to the
rest. Three findings follow. Strictness is legible in public text and warmth
is not: inspection-report strictness is correlated with observed strictness at
$r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$ and a text-only model predicts it out of sample at
$\RidgeBStrictness$, while no text source is correlated with observed warmth.
Espoused and enacted culture diverge, and more for warmth: headteachers rate
their schools \GapWarmth{} points warmer on a ten-point scale than observers
record and \GapStrictness{} points stricter, and every public source tracks the
espoused warmth measure rather than the enacted one. And across roughly 85 versions of eleven
language-model instruments, agreement with reference labels was unrelated to
correlation with the observed measure.
\end{chapterabstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:p1_intro}

School culture is widely held to matter for what pupils learn, but it is
rarely measured. This paper constructs measures of two dimensions of the
culture of English secondary schools, warmth and strictness, for the full
population of around 3,330 state secondary schools, and asks how far each
dimension can be recovered from data that exist for every school.

Warmth is the relational quality of a school: whether pupils are known,
cared for and supported by the adults around them. Strictness is its
behavioural structure: whether expectations of conduct are clear, enforced
and met. The two are the axes of the parenting typology of
\citet{baumrind1971} and \citet{maccoby1983}, in which the combination of
high responsiveness with high demandingness, the authoritative style,
produces the best child outcomes; the same two-dimensional structure has been
applied to schools by \citet{pellerin2005} and \citet{gregory2010}, who find
that pupils in schools combining support with structure report less
victimisation and more engagement. Warmth and strictness are treated here as
distinct dimensions rather than the ends of one axis, because a school can be
warm without being orderly and orderly without being warm, and the empirical
question of whether the two co-vary is left to the data. The difficulty is
that neither is recorded in any administrative dataset. What is
recorded, for every school, is text: an inspection report written by an
external observer, a behaviour policy written by the school, and a website
written by the school for parents. What is recorded for a smaller set of
schools, because I collected it, is direct observation and a structured
interview with the headteacher.

The design follows from that asymmetry. For \NTierOne{} schools I have a
research visit, comprising structured lesson and out-of-lesson observations,
and a headteacher interview; from these I construct a gold-standard measure of
each dimension. Because the interview and the visit disagree substantially
about the same school, the gold standard is two measures, not one: an
\emph{enacted} score, from what an observer recorded, and an \emph{espoused}
score, from what the headteacher reported. For a further \NTierTwo{} schools I have
the interview alone, and for the remaining national population I have only
the public text. For those schools I estimate the gold standard from what is
observable, using prediction models fitted on the schools where both the
predictors and the gold standard exist, and I report how well the predictions
reproduce the gold standard out of sample.

Three findings follow. First, the two dimensions are recoverable to very
different degrees. Strictness scored from inspection-report text is
correlated with observed strictness at $r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$, and a
text-only prediction model retains a leave-one-out correlation of
$\RidgeBStrictness$ with the gold standard. Warmth is not recoverable from
any public source: no text-derived warmth score is correlated with observed
warmth, and the text-only model has no out-of-sample skill (leave-one-out
$R^2$ of $-0.02$). Second, the enacted and espoused measures diverge in a
systematic and dimension-specific way. Headteachers rate their schools nearly
two points warmer on a ten-point scale than observers record and about one
point stricter; the interview and
the visit are correlated at $\GoldWarmthSplit$ for warmth and
$\GoldStrictnessSplit$ for strictness. Every public source, including a
survey of parents, tracks the espoused warmth measure and not the enacted one.
Third, the language-model instruments used to score the text carry a
measurement result of their own: an instrument's agreement with its reference
labels and its correlation with the observed measure are unrelated, and the
best-agreeing version of an instrument was usually the worse measure.

The paper contributes a measurement framework and a set of school-level
scores, a national-scale demonstration of what school culture is and is not
legible from public text, and a set of findings about language-model
measurement that apply beyond this setting. The scores are the explanatory
variables of the following chapter.

The paper sits between three literatures. The school-climate literature
measures culture through pupil, teacher and parent surveys
\citep{hoy2001,cohen2009,thapa2013}; these capture perceptions, are open to
social desirability, and are costly at national scale, and administrative
proxies such as exclusion rates \citep{machin2020} conflate culture with
intake. Structured classroom observation \citep{hamre2007} measures practice
directly but has not, to my knowledge, been combined with a leadership
interview and public text to separate what a school does from what it says.
And the text-as-data literature in economics \citep{gentzkow2019} supplies
the tools for reading the public record at scale; this paper applies large
language models rather than dictionary or topic methods, and reports what
that choice costs and buys in measurement terms. Relative to all three, the
contribution is a measure of enacted culture with a national text-based
extension whose validity is tested against direct observation rather than
assumed.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:p1_data} describes the data.
Section~\ref{sec:p1_measurement} constructs the gold standard, the text-based
instruments and the prediction models, and reports how well the predictions
reproduce the gold standard. Section~\ref{sec:p1_results} reports what the
measures show. Section~\ref{sec:p1_conclusion} concludes.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:p1_data}

The paper uses three kinds of data on English state secondary schools: a
research visit and a headteacher interview conducted for this study, three
corpora of public text, and the Department for Education's school-level
administrative panel. \Cref{tab:tier_summary} sets out coverage.

\textbf{Visits and interviews.} Between November 2023 and April 2025 I
interviewed the headteacher, or where unavailable the senior leader
responsible for behaviour and culture, at 304 schools, and visited
\NTierOne{} of them. Schools were recruited by direct approach and by
referral from participating schools; no quota was applied. Three-quarters of
interviews and half of visits took place in the 2024/25 academic year.

A visit comprised structured observation of lessons and of unstructured time,
each by two researchers rating independently on a fixed schedule. The lesson
schedule has nine items bearing on warmth and strictness (for example the
frequency with which the teacher uses pupils' names; the level of misbehaviour
tolerated before a first sanction) and nine on teaching practice; the
out-of-lesson schedule has eight items covering transitions, breaks, corridors
and the dining hall. All items are rated 1 to 5 and averaged across
observations. The interview followed a fixed schedule of eleven questions and
nine statements on which the headteacher rated their own school on a five-point
agreement scale (for example ``Sanction systems are applied consistently across
the school''; ``Pupils in this school feel that their teachers care about
them''), and recorded whether ten named behaviour systems were in place. The
full schedules are reproduced in \cref{app:2A:interview_guide,app:2A:visit_protocol}.
Inter-rater agreement on the observation items is reported with the measures in
Section~\ref{sec:p1_measurement}.

\textbf{Public text.} Three corpora cover the national population. Ofsted
inspection reports were obtained for 3,288 of the 3,312 open schools
(99.3 per cent); the remainder opened after 2023 and had not been inspected.
Behaviour policy documents were obtained for 3,363 schools, one document per
school, selected by a rule that prefers the current whole-school behaviour
policy over annexes and predecessor versions (\cref{app:2A:crawl}). School
websites were crawled for 3,370 schools, taking the home, values and welcome
pages; 3,284 crawls contain scorable prose. Where a crawl returned no prose,
the school is flagged rather than dropped.

\textbf{Administrative data.} All schools are matched on their unique
reference number to a school-year panel built from the Department for
Education's published tables for 2015/16 to 2024/25, carrying pupil
composition, prior attainment, Progress~8 and its components, workforce and
finance. This paper uses the panel only to describe the samples; the
following chapter uses it for outcomes and controls.

\textbf{Representativeness.} \Cref{tab:2A:representativeness} compares the
visited and interviewed schools with the national population. Both samples
resemble the population on school type, phase, admissions, size, deprivation
and urban location, and both over-represent London (45 per cent of visited
schools against 15 per cent nationally). Both also lean toward stronger
schools: 25 per cent of visited schools held an Outstanding inspection grade
as at August 2024 against 15 per cent nationally, and their mean Progress~8
in 2018/19 was $+0.17$ against $+0.04$. Estimates from the visited sample
therefore apply most directly to a population somewhat stronger than the
national average, and the restricted range of both culture and outcomes in
that sample works against finding associations rather than for it.

\Cref{tab:score_dist} reports descriptive statistics for the measures
constructed in Section~\ref{sec:p1_measurement}, for the visited schools.

\section{Measurement}
\label{sec:p1_measurement}

This section builds the measures in the order the design requires. It first
constructs the gold-standard scores from the visits and interviews and reports
their reliability. It then sets out how the public text is scored by
language-model instruments and how those instruments were tested. It ends
with the prediction models that carry the gold standard to schools that were
not visited, and the evidence on how well they do so.

\subsection{The gold standard}

Every observation item and interview statement is rated 1 to 5. Sub-scores
are item means; the dimension scores are sub-score means, rescaled to 0--10.
Two sets are constructed and kept apart. The \emph{enacted} scores use the
observations alone: enacted warmth averages in-lesson warmth (names, praise,
quality of interaction) and out-of-lesson warmth (interactions during
transitions and breaks); enacted strictness averages in-lesson strictness
(misbehaviour tolerated, disruption, response to sanction, respect) and
out-of-lesson strictness (sanction use, corridors, arrival, dining hall,
recreational space); enacted teaching averages the nine lesson items on
practice. The \emph{espoused} scores use the headteacher's statement ratings
alone: espoused warmth averages the three warmth statements (rewards applied
consistently; pupils feel safe; pupils feel their teachers care), espoused
strictness the four strictness statements (sanctions applied consistently;
behaviour in lessons; behaviour outside lessons; teachers feel respected). The
two remaining statements, on staff turnover and morale, form a staff-climate
score; the interview yields no espoused measure of teaching. Item definitions
and the mapping to sub-scores are in \cref{app:2A:guide}.

Two components of the interview are recorded but excluded from the scores. The
question-level ratings of how fully the headteacher answered each open
question measure articulacy rather than culture: they load on one general
factor that tracks the interviewer's rating of the head's confidence
($r = 0.34$ to $0.47$) and correlate with the observed scores at zero. A count
of eight named behaviour systems correlates with observed strictness at
$-0.01$, against $0.41$ for the statement battery, and is likewise excluded.
Both are retained in the data as variables in their own right.

Inter-rater agreement is substantial. Two researchers rated every lesson and
every out-of-lesson period independently; pairwise Pearson correlations across
the observation items range from $0.47$ to $0.81$, and within-one-point
agreement is close to 90 per cent throughout
(\cref{tab:irr_classroom,tab:irr_outside}). The interview items were rated by
the interviewer and an observer, with correlations of $0.61$ to $0.74$ and
within-one-point agreement above 90 per cent (\cref{tab:irr_interview}). Enacted warmth and strictness are
correlated at $r = \CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$ across the \NTierOne{}
visited schools, and the espoused pair at $\CorrWarmthStrictnessEspoused$
across the same schools.

The gold standard is two measures rather than one because the interview and
the visit disagree about the same school. Enacted and espoused warmth are
correlated at $r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ ($p = \GoldWarmthSplitP$) and enacted and
espoused strictness at $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ ($p = \GoldStrictnessSplitP$),
on the \NTierOne{} schools with both. Two measures sharing at most a sixth of
their variance are not fallible readings of one quantity, and a weighted
average of them estimates neither; the two are therefore carried separately
throughout, and their divergence is itself a result reported in
Section~\ref{sec:p1_results}.
Both are also correlated with an external administrative record where one
exists: the espoused staff-climate score is correlated with the school's
recorded teacher turnover at $r = -0.52$, and at $-0.48$ conditional on the
full control set.

\subsection{Scoring the public text}

For schools without a visit the gold standard must be predicted from what is
observable, and the richest observable is text. Each of the three corpora is
scored by a language-model instrument that reads one document about one
school and returns a 1--5 score for warmth and for strictness (and, for
inspection reports, teaching). All scoring uses gpt-4o-mini at zero
sampling temperature; every score records the prompt version, a hash of the
rubric text and the model version, and identical inputs are served from
cache.

Two architectures are used and the choice between them is empirical.
\emph{Prose} instruments give the model a written marking scheme and take its
band; the inspection-report and behaviour-policy instruments are of this
kind, and each scores its constructs in a single call on the full document.
\emph{Decomposed} instruments never ask for a band: the model answers a fixed
set of factual questions, each answer supported by a verbatim quotation
verified against the document, and the band is assigned by a fixed rule in
code. The website and interview-transcript instruments are of this kind. The
operative rubric and question set of every instrument is reproduced in
\cref{app:2A:prompts}.

Instruments were tuned against reference labels and then tested three ways.
The reference labels were produced by three independent instances of a
larger language model (Claude, Opus class), each given only the written rule
and the documents, blind to one another and to every model score, with the
majority label as the target; I wrote and revised the rules, ruled on the
boundary cases the raters could not, and labelled the development packs
myself. Agreement figures below are therefore model--model agreement, and the
final arbiter of every instrument is not agreement but the gold standard. My
own agreement with my labels of the same documents two weeks apart is
$\kappa_w = 0.77$, which bounds what any rater can achieve against them at
about $0.88$.

The first test is agreement out of sample: weighted kappa against the
reference labels on documents no version of the instrument was tuned on.
Roughly 85 archived versions across eleven instrument families were each
scored once on such documents; every in-sample figure had read high, and
\cref{tab:instruments_adopted} reports the out-of-sample figures for the
instruments retained. The second is validity: correlation with the enacted
gold standard on the visited schools, and, for one construct, with an
external register. The website instrument for the prominence of religious
faith recovers the official register of religious character with an area
under the ROC curve of 0.960, which establishes that the method reads what is
in the text; where a construct fails against its criterion, the failure is a
property of the text. The third is a check that no field available to the
model carries the inspection verdict: report bodies are stripped of every
stated grade before scoring.

\input{tables/tab_instruments_adopted}

The testing produced four results that governed which instruments were kept,
summarised in \cref{tab:instrument_findings}. Agreement with labels and
correlation with the gold standard are unrelated across versions, and the
best-agreeing version was usually the worse measure: the inspection-report
strictness instrument that agreed best with labels correlated with observed
strictness at $0.07$, the prose version at $0.43$. Instruments that ask the
model for facts outperform those that ask for judgements. Scoring related
constructs in separate calls can produce a correlation between them that the
gold standard does not show: enacted warmth and strictness are correlated at
$\CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$; the single-call prose instrument gives
$0.54$; the
separate-call instruments return $0.08$ to $0.22$. And the model is part of
the instrument: an unchanged rubric moved between models fell from
$\kappa_w = 0.63$ to $0.12$, so instruments are pinned to the model they were
tested with. Two limitations of the retained instruments are stated once here
and apply wherever their scores are used. The inspection-report warmth score
tracks the inspection grade at $-0.40$ from text that never states it, where
human raters of the same text track it at $-0.25$; it is carried as a
descriptive measure and never as a clean predictor. The website warmth
instrument leaves its bottom two bands nearly indistinguishable at the model's
limit.

\subsection{Predicting the gold standard}

For interviewed schools without a visit (Model~A) and for the national
population (Model~B), the enacted score of each dimension is predicted by
ridge regression fitted on the \RidgeTrainN{} visited schools with complete
predictors. Model~A's predictors are the espoused sub-scores, the systems
count and the two text scores for the dimension; Model~B's are the two text
scores alone. Predictions are evaluated by leave-one-out cross-validation on
the same schools (\cref{tab:interview_model_loo,tab:national_model_loo}); the
fitted coefficients are in \cref{app:2A:ridge_spec}.

Strictness is predictable and warmth is not. Model~A predicts enacted
strictness at a leave-one-out $r = \RidgeAStrictness$ and Model~B, from text
alone, at $\RidgeBStrictness$. For warmth Model~A reaches $\RidgeAWarmth$ and
Model~B has no out-of-sample skill: its leave-one-out $R^2$ is $-0.02$, no
better than assigning every school the sample mean, and its predictions
collapse toward a constant with a standard deviation under a tenth of the
gold standard's. Among the predictors, the espoused strictness score and the
inspection-report strictness score do roughly equal work ($0.24$ and $0.23$
per standard deviation); the systems count contributes nothing.

Because Model~B's warmth predictions carry no signal and Model~A's carry
little, neither model's predictions are used as regressors in the following
chapter. That chapter uses the enacted scores directly for the visited
schools and the raw inspection-report strictness score for the national
extension. The prediction models are best read as the measurement result they
are: publicly available text can rank English secondary schools on strictness
and cannot rank them on warmth.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:p1_results}

This section reports what the measures show. Three results stand out: the
enacted and espoused measures diverge, and they diverge more for warmth than
for strictness; every public source of text tracks the espoused warmth measure
and none tracks the enacted one; and behaviour policy text is, if anything,
inversely related to observed practice.

\textbf{Distributions.} \Cref{tab:score_dist} reports the measures for the
visited schools and \cref{fig:score_distributions} the distributions of the
espoused and inspection-report scores at scale. Enacted warmth across
the \NTierOne{} visited schools has mean 6.6 and standard deviation 1.0 on the
0--10 scale; enacted strictness has mean 7.0 and standard deviation 0.9.
Espoused scores across the \NInterviewed{} interviewed schools sit higher
(means 8.5 and 8.0) with standard deviations of 0.9 and 1.0. The two enacted
dimensions are correlated at $r = \CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$: warm schools
tend also to be orderly ones, which is
consistent with the authoritative-school framework and inconsistent with a
single warm-to-strict axis. Between-school variation is genuinely narrow in
the English system, and score compression partly reflects that: about
two-thirds of secondary schools are graded Good and a further sixth
Outstanding, and common inspection, curriculum and training pressures
compress the distribution of any culture measure.

\textbf{Enacted and espoused culture diverge.} On the \NTierOne{} schools
with both, headteachers rate their schools warmer than observers record by
$\GapWarmth$ points on the 0--10 scale and stricter by $\GapStrictness$ (paired
$p < 0.001$ for both). The two sources are correlated at $\GoldWarmthSplit$
for warmth and $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ for strictness. The gap is therefore
larger, and the agreement lower, for warmth: what a headteacher says about
relationships is a poorer guide to what a visitor finds than what they say
about behaviour. Within the interview, the items on which heads report
reliably are those about the consistency of practice (sanctions applied
consistently, $r = 0.39$ with observed strictness; rewards applied
consistently, $0.29$), not those about how people feel (pupils feel safe,
$0.16$; teachers care, $0.11$, neither significant). Headteachers report
accurately on whether their systems are applied and inaccurately on
relational climate.

\textbf{Public text tracks talk, not practice.} \Cref{tab:source_criterion}
sets each source's warmth and strictness score against the enacted and the
espoused criterion. The pattern is uniform. For strictness, the
inspection-report score is correlated with the observed measure at
$\CorrOfstedStrictness$ and with the espoused measure at
$\CorrOfstedStrictEspoused$; behaviour policies and websites carry no
strictness signal against either. For warmth, no source is correlated with
the observed measure, and the inspection report and the parent survey are
correlated with the espoused one ($0.29$ and $0.31$). The parent survey is
the fourth independent respondent group and shows the same crossover: parents'
reports of their children being happy and safe track what heads say about
warmth ($0.31$) more than what observers record ($0.16$), and track observed
strictness ($0.28$) better than observed warmth. \Cref{fig:crossover_warmth}
draws the warmth half of the table. What is written and reported about a
school's warmth, by inspectors, by parents and by the school itself, tracks
the school's account of itself and not the classroom.

\input{tables/tab_source_criterion}

\textbf{Behaviour policies describe aspiration.} The behaviour-policy warmth
score is uncorrelated with observed warmth ($-0.08$; $-0.02$ on the revised
instrument used in the following chapter) and negatively correlated with the
parent measure ($-0.04$, $p = 0.03$); the strictness score is uncorrelated
with observed strictness ($0.01$ and $-0.08$ respectively). One school in ten publishes
a policy authored by its trust rather than by the school. Written behaviour
policies are governance documents that state intent, and schools that state
the warmest intent are not, on these measures, the warmest schools. The same
result appears in the following chapter as a small negative association
between behaviour-policy warmth and pupil progress.

\textbf{What the text does and does not contain.} Two further results
qualify the inspection-report scores. The warmth score tracks the inspection
grade at $-0.40$ from report text stripped of every stated grade, where human
raters of the same text track it at $-0.25$: inspectors write about
relationships in a register that moves with their overall verdict, and the
model reads that register. The score is therefore a characterisation of the
report rather than a clean measure of the school. The strictness score also
tracks the verdict ($-0.49$) but, unlike warmth, it tracks observed practice
as well, and in the following chapter its association with pupil progress
survives conditioning on the grade. Finally, the one website construct with an
external criterion, the prominence of religious faith, is recovered from
website text with an area under the ROC curve of 0.960: the method reads what
is there, and warmth is not.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:p1_conclusion}

This paper constructs measures of warmth and strictness for English secondary
schools from direct observation, headteacher interview and public text, and
asks how far each dimension can be recovered from data that exist for every
school. Three findings summarise it.

Strictness is legible in public text and warmth is not. An inspection
report's strictness score is correlated with observed strictness at
$\CorrOfstedStrictness$, and a text-only model predicts it out of sample at
$\RidgeBStrictness$; no text source is correlated with observed warmth, and
the text-only warmth model has no out-of-sample skill. The asymmetry is a
fact about the sources, not the instruments: inspectors record conduct and
enforcement in detail and describe relationships in a uniform positive
register, schools' own documents describe intent, and websites are not written
about the thing observers see.

Espoused and enacted culture diverge, and more for warmth. Headteachers
over-state warmth relative to observation by nearly two points and agree with
observers only weakly on it, while their reports of behaviour systems being
applied consistently track observed strictness. Every public source, including
a survey of parents, tracks the espoused warmth measure and not the enacted
one. The gap is dimension-specific and appears in four independent kinds of
evidence, which suggests it is a feature of how schools present themselves
rather than an artefact of any one instrument.

The instruments carry a measurement result of their own. Across roughly
85 versions of eleven language-model instruments, agreement with reference
labels was unrelated to correlation with the observed measure, and the
best-agreeing version was usually the worse measure; instruments that ask for
facts outperformed those that ask for judgements; scoring related constructs
in separate calls produced correlations between them that the observations
do not show; and an unchanged rubric moved between models lost most of its
agreement. These results apply to any study that scores text with a language
model against human labels.

The measures have limits that the following chapter inherits. The visited
sample is \NTierOne{} schools, weighted toward London and toward stronger
schools; the
enacted scores come from one visit each; the inspection-report scores are read
from prose that carries the inspection verdict; and the reference labels for
the text instruments were produced by a language model rather than by human
raters, with the observed measures as the final check. Within those limits,
the enacted scores are a reliable, two-dimensional measure of school culture
for the visited schools, and the inspection-report strictness score extends
one of the two dimensions to the national population.

\section{Scoring Guide and Item Definitions}
\label{app:2A:guide}

This section documents how the individual proforma items combine into the nine
sub-scores and the two score sets. The items themselves---question
wording, response format, and the anchor description attached to each rating
level---are reproduced in full in \cref{app:2A:interview_guide} for the interview
and \cref{app:2A:visit_protocol} for the two observation forms.

Every item is recorded on a 1--5 scale. Two observation items admit a zero code
with a distinct meaning: a zero on \emph{response of pupils to sanctions} records
that no sanction was issued during the lesson, and a zero on \emph{effectiveness
of pupil discussion} records that no discussion was initiated. The first is not
a genuine low score---an orderly lesson requiring no sanction should not be
counted as one where sanctions were ignored---so it is replaced by
$6 - \text{disruption}$, which assigns a calm unsanctioned lesson a high value
and a disrupted unsanctioned lesson a low one. The second is treated as a
genuine negative, since a lesson containing no discussion did not achieve
effective discussion.

Observation item scores are averaged across lessons and across researchers to
give one value per school before the sub-scores are formed.
\cref{tab:p1_subscore_items} lists the constituent items of each sub-score.

\input{snippets/subscore_items}

Two items on the outside-of-lessons form---consistency of verbal feedback and
quality of differentiation across lessons---are recorded on the form as
cross-lesson averages of the corresponding lesson observation items, and so
enter no sub-score of their own. Of the ten yes/no systems in the interview, S3
uses the eight that represent behavioural or academic requirements; the two
reward systems (reward assemblies and reward points) are excluded, as their
presence indicates a warmer rather than a stricter regime. S3 is the count of
those eight, rescaled to 0--5. Every other sub-score is the mean of its items,
computed over the items present so that a missing item reduces the number of
contributing items rather than the school's score.

Sub-scores combine into two score sets grouped by source, observed behaviour and
stated philosophy kept apart:
%
\begin{align}
\text{Enacted:}\quad
  W^{\text{enac}} &= \overline{(W1, W2)}, &
  S^{\text{enac}} &= \overline{(S1, S2)}, &
  T^{\text{enac}} &= T1 \label{eq:enacted} \\
\text{Espoused:}\quad
  W^{\text{esp}}  &= W3, &
  S^{\text{esp}}  &= S4, &
  \mathrm{SC} &= \overline{(\text{S3-stmt}, \text{S4-stmt})} \label{eq:espoused}
\end{align}
%
There is no espoused teaching score: the two interview statements under the
teaching heading concern staff turnover and morale, and they form the staff
climate score SC instead. The systems count $S3$ enters no espoused score.

Schools with only one of the two instruments carry only the corresponding score
set: an interview without a visit yields espoused scores alone, a visit without
an interview yields enacted scores alone. No imputation is attempted across the
two, since the espoused and enacted scores are correlated at only
$r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ (warmth) and $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ (strictness), too
little for either to stand in for the other. All scores are finally rescaled
from 0--5 to 0--10.

The mapping in \cref{tab:p1_subscore_items} is generated from
\texttt{warm\_strict\_scorer.py} by \texttt{thesis/make\_instruments.py}, which
fails if the scorer uses an item this appendix does not describe.

\section{Behaviour Policy Crawl Methodology}
\label{app:2A:crawl}

Behaviour policies are not held in any central register, so each document had to
be located on the school's own website. The crawl proceeds in three stages, run
by \texttt{download\_behaviour\_policies.py} and
\texttt{select\_behaviour\_policy\_documents.py}.

\paragraph{URL discovery.} Starting from the school website recorded in GIAS,
the crawler tries a fixed list of conventional policy-page paths
(\texttt{/policies/}, \texttt{/key-information/policies},
\texttt{/statutory-information/policies} and around thirty further variants,
each in both capitalisation conventions), since English school sites cluster
heavily on a small number of content-management templates. Pages that are found
are parsed for links to documents with a \texttt{.pdf}, \texttt{.doc} or
\texttt{.docx} extension. Where the path list fails, the crawler falls back to
site-scoped web search---Brave Search, with Google Custom Search as a
secondary---restricted to the school's own domain and querying a set of title
phrases (``behaviour policy'', ``relationships and behaviour
policy'', ``positive behaviour policy'', ``behaviour for learning''
and similar, including the American spelling and the initialisms some trusts
use). The two spellings matter: a non-trivial number of English schools publish
under ``behavior''.

\paragraph{Retrieval.} Candidate documents are scored on link text, file name
and URL path, and the highest-scoring few per school are downloaded rather than
only the single best, so that the selection stage has something to choose
between. Requests are rate-limited and identify themselves as
\texttt{school-policy-downloader/1.0}; each file is stored with its SHA-256
digest so that repeat runs are idempotent and identical documents shared across
a multi-academy trust can be detected. The retrieval stage returned 8,277
candidate documents.

\paragraph{Policy identification.} The selection stage picks one document per
school and records why. Candidates are scored on weighted regular-expression
matches against both document metadata and the first portion of extracted text.
Positive patterns range from an exact ``behaviour policy'' title match
down to weaker signals such as ``discipline policy'' or ``conduct
policy''; negative patterns penalise the documents most easily confused with a
behaviour policy---anti-bullying policies, exclusions procedures, home--school
agreements, and primary-phase policies published by all-through trusts. The gap
between the best and second-best candidate determines a confidence label. Of
3,363 schools with at least one candidate, selection was high-confidence for
2,755, medium for 387 and low for 79, with a further 142 resolved by hand; 334
were flagged for manual review.

The residual risk this procedure cannot eliminate is that a school publishes a
current policy the crawl never reaches---behind a login, inside a newsletter, or
as a web page rather than a document. That failure is not random with respect to
school characteristics, which is why coverage is reported by tier in
\cref{app:2A:sample} rather than assumed uniform.

\section{Prediction Model Specification and Coefficients}
\label{app:2A:ridge_spec}

The interview-tier model (Model~A) predicts the enacted score of each
dimension from the vector $\mathbf{f}_s$ of interview sub-scores ($W3$, $S3$,
$S4$) and the two text scores for the dimension,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ridge_interview}
  \hat{y}_s = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}' \mathbf{f}_s ,
\end{equation}
%
and the national-tier model (Model~B) from the two text scores alone,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ridge_national}
  \hat{y}_s = \hat{\alpha} + \beta_O x_{O,s} + \beta_P x_{P,s} ,
\end{equation}
%
where $x_{O,s}$ is the inspection-report score and $x_{P,s}$ the
behaviour-policy score for school~$s$. Both are ridge regressions fitted on the
\RidgeTrainN{} visited schools with a complete predictor vector; the penalty
$\lambda$ is selected by leave-one-out cross-validation on the same schools,
and predictions are clipped to $[0, 10]$.
\Cref{tab:interview_model_coefs} and \cref{tab:national_model_coefs} report the
fitted coefficient vectors, in both standardised and original units.

\input{tables/tab_interview_model_coefs}

\input{tables/tab_national_model_coefs}

The national-tier model takes the language model's holistic score for each
document as a single number per source. The question that specification invites
is whether the model's reading contributes anything that a deterministic counter
could not recover from the same text.
\Cref{tab:ridge_spec_comparison} tests this directly, comparing two feature
representations under the identical estimation procedure: (A) the two holistic
scores used throughout the chapter, and (D) the keyword-rule scores computed by
the pattern matcher that runs alongside the language model on the same
documents, as a floor against which to judge whether the language model
contributes anything beyond term counting.

\input{tables/tab_ridge_spec_comparison}

The comparison rests on the identical \RidgeTrainN{}~schools in every cell, so
it is like for like.\footnote{The Ofsted keyword-rule scores are taken
from the national scoring run, the same file that supplies the holistic Ofsted
scores used elsewhere in the chapter.} The result is dimension-specific, and the
split runs along the same seam as the rest of the chapter.

On strictness, the language model's reading contributes something the counter
cannot recover. The holistic specification reaches $r = \RidgeBStrictness$ with
a cross-validated $R^2$ of $0.114$; the keyword baseline on the same documents
reaches $r = 0.094$ and an $R^2$ of $0.008$, barely distinguishable from
predicting the sample mean. Whatever the strictness score captures, it is not
the frequency of particular words. It is located in an act of reading rather
than in any countable property of the text, which is the opposite of the pattern
a feature-representation artefact would produce.

On warmth, neither specification recovers anything. Both select a penalty at or
near the top of the grid and shrink almost entirely to the training mean, and
both return a negative cross-validated $R^2$ ($-0.015$ for the holistic scores,
$-0.020$ for the keyword rules), meaning each predicts the enacted warmth score
worse than the sample mean does; the keyword specification's predictions are too
nearly constant for a correlation to be defined at all. This is not evidence
that the warmth score is empty---\cref{tab:source_criterion} shows it is
correlated with the espoused criterion---but it does establish that the part
of it that survives to the national tier carries no enacted-warmth signal for
either representation to find. The feature-representation test can only
discriminate between two readings of the text where there is something in the
text to read.
