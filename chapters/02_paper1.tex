\begin{chapterabstract}
How warm a school is towards its pupils, and how strictly it runs, are
widely thought to affect how much progress pupils make. Neither is measured
in any dataset that covers every school. This paper measures both, and asks
how much of each can be read from the documents that every school already
produces. In \NTierOne{} schools I collected a gold-standard measure: a day
of structured observation recorded what each school does, and an interview
with its headteacher recorded what the head says it does. I then read every
school's inspection report, behaviour policy and website in two ways. A
prediction model learns, from the visited schools, which words and phrases
go with warmer or stricter schools, and is tested only on schools it has
never seen. A set of one-page marking schemes is applied by a language
model, once, and judged against the same observations. There are three
findings. First, inspection reports carry a modest signal of how warm a
school actually is: the prediction model reads it at \pending{final warmth
first stage: +0.33}, even after removing everything that a school's intake
statistics could explain. Second, no prediction model can read strictness
--- but the marking scheme can \pending{grid strictness: +0.30}. The two
dimensions are written down differently: warmth shows in a report's
everyday wording, strictness in specific claims that have to be asked for.
Third, what schools write about themselves reflects what their heads say,
not what observers see. Of the \TopQN{} schools whose heads placed them in
the top quarter for warmth, only \TopQWMatch{} were there when observed
--- about what chance would produce. The measures that pass these tests
become the explanatory variables of the next chapter.
\end{chapterabstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:p1_intro}

How warm a school is towards its pupils, and how strictly it runs, are two of
the things most often said to affect how much progress its pupils make.
Neither is measured in any administrative dataset. In this paper I construct
measures of these two dimensions of school culture, which I call warmth and
strictness, for a sample of English state secondary schools, and I ask how
well each of them can be recovered from data that exist for all 3,332
schools in the country.

It is worth being precise about what the two dimensions mean here. Warmth is
the relational quality of a school: whether pupils are known, cared for and
supported by the adults around them. In a lesson it shows in whether the
teacher uses pupils' names, how praise is given and how the teacher and the
class speak to one another. Between lessons it shows in how staff and pupils
deal with each other in corridors and at break, and in whether rewards are
used consistently across the lessons of the day. Strictness is the school's
behavioural structure: whether
expectations of conduct are clear, enforced and met. In a lesson it shows in
how much misbehaviour is tolerated before a sanction, how much disruption
occurs and how pupils respond when they are corrected. Outside lessons it
shows in how pupils arrive, how they move between lessons and how the dining
hall runs. It should be stressed that strictness in this sense means clear
rules applied consistently, and not harsh punishment. A school whose
sanctions are applied by every member of staff in the same way is strict in
this sense, and whether it is also warm is a separate question.

The two dimensions come from the parenting typology of \citet{baumrind1971}
and \citet{maccoby1983}, which classifies parenting by demandingness and
responsiveness and finds that the combination of both, the authoritative
style, produces the best child outcomes across cognitive and non-cognitive
domains. \citet{majumder2015} and \citet{zhang2020} find the same pattern in
educational attainment, with each dimension carrying an independent effect.
The transfer to schools is direct: demandingness is strictness and
responsiveness is warmth. \citet{pellerin2005} and
\citet{gregory2010,gregory2011} apply the typology to schools and find that
pupils in schools combining support with structure report less victimisation
and more engagement, and \citet{gottfredson2005} reviews the related evidence
on school discipline. What makes the two dimensions measurable in a school,
as opposed to in a family, is that both are enacted in public. A researcher
sitting in a lesson can see whether names are used and how disruption is
dealt with, and a researcher standing in a corridor can see whether pupils
move calmly and whether staff speak to them. Neither requires access to
anything private. I treat warmth and strictness as two separate dimensions
rather than as the two ends of one scale, because a school can be warm
without being orderly and orderly without being warm. Whether the two go
together in practice is a question the data answer rather than something I
assume. I also observed a third dimension, the quality of teaching practice.
This is not part of culture as I define it, but it is the most obvious rival
explanation for any association between culture and results, and it is
carried through the analysis for that reason.

The difficulty in measuring any of this is that none of it is recorded for
every school. What is recorded, for every school, is text: an inspection
report written by an external inspector, a behaviour policy written by the
school, and a website written by the school for parents. For a smaller set
of schools this study adds what no administrative source holds, which is
direct observation of the school at work and a structured interview with
its headteacher. The design follows from that asymmetry. For \NTierOne{}
schools I have a research visit, made up of structured observations in
lessons and around the school, and an interview with the headteacher, and
from these I construct a gold-standard measure of each dimension. Because
the interview and the visit disagree substantially about the same school,
the gold standard is in fact two measures, not one: an \emph{enacted}
score, from what the observers recorded, and an \emph{espoused} score, from
what the headteacher reported. For a further \NTierTwo{} schools I have the
interview alone, and for the remaining national population only the public
text.

I then ask how much of the gold standard the public text contains, in two
ways. The first is a prediction model: using the visited schools, a
statistical model learns which words and phrases of a document go with
higher or lower observed scores, and it is tested only on schools it was
not fitted on. Before fitting, identifying details are removed from the text,
and the part of each score that school demographics could explain is
removed from the target, so the model earns credit only for reading culture
in prose, not for recognising a school's intake or its paperwork. The
second is a marking scheme: a one-page written rubric for each dimension,
applied to each document once by a language model, and judged by the same
standard --- correlation with what observers recorded. The first method
assumes nothing about where culture shows up in text; the second states a
theory of it. Comparing them, source by source and dimension by dimension,
shows not only which measures can be extended to every school in the
country but what kind of reading each dimension requires.

There are three main findings. The first is that inspection reports carry
a modest but genuine signal of observed warmth. A prediction model reading
only the report's wording reproduces the observed warmth of held-out
schools at \pending{measured: +0.33 reports-only (deconf, LOO), ceiling about 0.83; macro to follow}, and the correlation
\emph{rises} when demographics are removed from the target, so the signal
is carried by the prose itself. The words doing the work are recognisable:
reports on schools observed to be warm describe adults attending to pupils
(``listen carefully'', ``leaders check''), while reports on colder schools
fall back on stock assurances (``pastoral support is in place'', ``pupils
feel safe''). Observed teaching behaves like warmth, with a signal of
\pending{measured: +0.28 reports-only; macro to follow}. The second finding is that observed
strictness behaves differently: no prediction model recovers it, however
the text is represented, \pending{grid: rubric strictness result and the
contrast sentence; expected --- the one-page marking scheme recovers it at
roughly the level warmth reaches statistically}. The two dimensions are
written down differently. Warmth lives in a report's habitual register,
which a model with no instructions can learn; strictness lives in specific,
checkable claims (``low-level disruption is rare''), which only directed
questions extract. The third finding is that the enacted and espoused
measures diverge, and that the divergence is a fact about warmth. The
interview and the visit are correlated at only $\GoldWarmthSplit$ for
warmth and $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ for strictness. Of the \TopQN{} schools
whose heads rated them in the top quarter for warmth, only \TopQWMatch{}
were in the top quarter as observed, against roughly seven expected by
chance and \TopQExpectedSameW{} expected if the two sets of scores
measured the same thing with these instruments' reliabilities; for
strictness the corresponding match is \TopQSMatch{} of \TopQN{}, against
an error-limited ceiling of about \TopQExpectedSameS{}. The same asymmetry
runs through the public text: what schools write in their policies and on
their websites tracks what their heads say about strictness and about
staff morale, but their warmth language tracks nothing --- not the
observers, and not even their own head's private account.

The paper sits between five literatures and adds something to each of them.
The school-climate literature measures culture through pupil, teacher and
parent surveys \citep{hoy2001,cohen2009,thapa2013}. Those surveys have
produced consistent associations between climate and wellbeing, attendance
and attainment, but they capture perceptions rather than practice, they are
open to social desirability, and they are costly at national scale.
\citet{vanhoutte2005} argues that climate, meaning the aggregate of members'
perceptions, should be kept apart from culture, meaning the shared norms that
govern behaviour, and it is culture in that sense that the measures here aim
at. Administrative proxies such as exclusion rates \citep{machin2020}
conflate culture with intake, and the inspection grade folds many dimensions
into a single verdict. My contribution to that literature is a measure of
enacted culture built from observation rather than perception, with the
perception data, from headteachers and from parents, used as a comparison
against it rather than as a component of it.

The school-effectiveness tradition that begins with \citet{coleman1966} and
\citet{rutter1979} located the difference schools make not in resources but
in what Rutter called ethos, meaning the expectations and routine practice
that a school sustains, and later syntheses converged on a list that includes
an orderly environment and a supportive climate
\citep{edmonds1979,sammons1995,teddlie2000}. That strand has stayed largely
qualitative, because ethos has been measured by case study. The contribution
here is to make two of its correlates measurable across a national
population. Structured classroom observation, in particular the CLASS
framework of \citet{hamre2007}, measures emotional support and classroom
organisation directly and predicts pupil gains at secondary level
\citep{allen2013} and in English primary schools \citep{sammons2008}. The
observation forms I use draw on those two domains, but they aggregate across
lessons and out-of-lesson settings so as to characterise a school rather
than a teacher. To my knowledge no study has combined such observation with
a leadership interview and public text in order to separate what a school
does from what it says.

The literature on high-expectation, high-structure
charter and academy models
\citep{abdulkadiroglu2011,angrist2010,dobbie2013,fryer2014} finds large
effects of a bundle of practices in which strict behaviour norms and warm,
individual relationships come together. It is the origin of the strictness
dimension and the reason to expect it to matter, but it has not measured the
two dimensions apart. Finally, the text-as-data literature in economics
\citep{gentzkow2019} supplies the tools for reading the public record at
scale. Its standing warning is that general-purpose methods misread
domain-specific text \citep{loughran2011,muddiman2019}. This paper uses both of that literature's modern instruments ---
supervised text regression and structured annotation by a large language
model \citep{gilardi2023,eloundou2024} --- tests each against direct
observation, and reports which dimension of culture yields to which
instrument. In doing so it answers, for one concrete measurement problem,
the question posed by \citet{ludwig2025} of when instructed language-model
measurement adds value over statistical alternatives.

Against those five literatures, the paper contributes three things: a
measure of enacted culture built from observation; national extensions of
culture through public text, whose validity is tested against observation
rather than assumed; and a like-for-like comparison, on one criterion, of
the two ways economists now read text --- learned weights and instructed
reading --- showing that which one works is a property of the construct.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:p1_data}
describes the data. Section~\ref{sec:p1_measurement} constructs the gold
standard and the text-based scores, describes the prediction models, and
reports how well the predictions reproduce the gold standard.
Section~\ref{sec:p1_results} reports what the measures show.
Section~\ref{sec:p1_conclusion} concludes.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:p1_data}

The paper uses four kinds of data on English state secondary schools: a
research visit and a headteacher interview that were carried out for this
study, three collections of public text, a national survey of parents, and
the Department for Education's school-level administrative data. It helps to
group the sources by whose account of the school they give. The visit
records what trained observers saw the school do, and the inspection report
records what an inspector saw, so these two are sources on \emph{enacted}
culture. The interview, the behaviour policy and the website record what the
school says about itself, the first to a researcher and the other two in
writing, so these three are sources on \emph{espoused} culture. The parent
survey belongs to neither group. Parents report their own family's experience
of the school, and whether that experience follows what observers see or
what the school says about itself is one of the questions the paper answers.
Together the sources define three tiers of coverage: \NTierOne{} schools with
a visit, an interview and the public sources; \NTierTwo{} schools with an
interview and the public sources (a handful lack one of the three); and
the rest of the national population
with the public sources alone. \Cref{tab:tier_summary} sets out the tiers and
compares the schools in each on intake, size and inspection grade.

\textbf{Visits and interviews.} Between November 2023 and April 2025 the
research team, working under my direction, interviewed the headteacher at
304 schools and visited \NTierOne{} of them. In eight cases the head was
unavailable and we interviewed the senior leader responsible for behaviour
and culture instead.\footnote{The counts that follow differ by a school or
two from place to place, for four reasons which I state once here. One of
the 304 heads left the statement battery blank, so 303 schools carry an
espoused score and the interview-only tier numbers \NTierTwo{}; that school
belongs to neither tier in \cref{tab:tier_summary}. Of the \NTierOne{}
visited schools, two have no scored inspection report and two have no
published parent survey, so correlations and prediction models that use
those sources run on 101 schools. Among the interviewed schools, between 299
and 302 have each text source. Finally, one visited school and three
interviewed schools could not be matched to the 2023/24 characteristics
panel used in \cref{app:2A:sample}, which therefore describes 102 and 300
schools.} Schools were recruited by approaching headteachers directly by
email and telephone, and by referral from schools that had already taken
part. No quota was applied by region, school type or inspection grade.
Three-quarters of the interviews and half of the visits took place in the
2024/25 academic year. At the end of every interview the headteacher was
asked whether the school would be willing to host a visit, which means that
the visited schools are a self-selected subset of the interviewed ones.

A visit lasted a school day and was made by a team of two, and occasionally
three, researchers. The school was asked in advance to arrange four to six
lessons spread across year groups and subjects, and it chose the particular
lessons itself. The team observed those lessons, typically five of them,
spread across Years 7 to 11. The researchers knew which school they were
visiting but not its inspection grade, and the school knew it was being
observed but not which items were being rated. Each school also allowed us
to observe pupils moving between lessons, and pupils and staff interacting
at break and lunch, which is what the outside-of-lessons form rates. It
should be acknowledged that the school choosing the lessons is a limitation
of the visit as a measure of practice. However, the direction of the
problem is known. A school will tend to show a visitor its better lessons,
so if anything the enacted scores are too high and too similar to one
another. This makes it harder to find differences between schools, not
easier, so it works against the results the paper reports. Each
observation covered either a whole lesson or half of one, and every
researcher present rated it independently on the lesson observation form.
The team also watched arrival to lessons, movement between lessons, break
and lunch, and at the end of the day each researcher completed the
outside-of-lessons form once, summarising the whole day. The lesson form has
twenty items. Ten of them bear on warmth and strictness, for example the
frequency with which the teacher uses pupils' names, and the level of
misbehaviour tolerated before a first sanction. The other ten bear on
teaching practice, such as questioning, feedback, explanation and structure.
The outside-of-lessons form has thirteen items: nine on the school outside
the classroom (sanctions and rewards, corridors, arrival, the dining hall,
and interactions and relationships at break), two on the condition of wall
displays, and two in which the observer compares the teaching seen across
the day. Not every item enters a score, and Section~\ref{sec:p1_measurement}
says which do and why the rest do not. Every item is rated from 1 to 5
against written anchors at 1, 3 and 5, and the item ratings are averaged
across researchers and across lessons to give one value per school.

The interview lasted about an hour and followed a fixed guide. It had three
parts: twelve open questions on the school's approach to behaviour,
relationships, wellbeing and teaching, the first of which records the
head's tenure; ten statements on which the
headteacher rated their own school on a five-point agreement scale (for
example ``The sanction systems are applied consistently by all staff'' and
``Pupils feel like their teachers care''); and a
checklist of ten named behaviour systems (centralised detentions, an
internal exclusion room, a mobile phone ban and so on) recorded as present
or absent. Two researchers attended every interview, one asking the
questions and one observing, and both scored the open questions
independently. The observation forms and the interview guide are reproduced
in full in \cref{app:2A:visit_protocol,app:2A:interview_guide}, and the
agreement between researchers on the observation and interview items is
reported together with the measures in Section~\ref{sec:p1_measurement}.

\textbf{Public text.} Three collections of text cover the national
population, and their coverage is best read against the analysis panel of
3,332 open state secondary schools. A scored inspection report exists for
3,272 of them (98.2 per cent); the rest had opened too recently to have been
inspected. A behaviour policy document exists for 3,294 (98.9 per cent),
one document per school, taken from the school's website. About one document
in ten is a trust-wide policy adopted word for word by the school. A website
crawl exists for 3,311 schools (99.4 per cent). The crawl takes the home,
values and welcome pages, which are the pages on which a school describes
itself to prospective parents. Of those crawls, 3,284 contain prose that can
be scored, and where a crawl returned no prose the school is flagged rather
than dropped, so that missingness is recorded as a variable and not treated
as a silent exclusion. The counts differ by a few dozen schools because a
handful of schools have no report, no policy or no website;
\cref{app:2A:sample} gives the reconciliation.

\textbf{Parent survey.} Ofsted's Parent View survey is open to the parents
of every school and is published once enough parents have responded. I use
the most recent release for each school and the two items closest to
warmth: the share of responding parents who agree that their child is happy
at the school, and the share who agree that their child feels safe there.
The two shares are averaged into one parent-reported warmth measure, which
is available for 3,255 schools in the panel and for 101 of the visited
schools. It should be noted that parents choose whether to respond. The
typical release rests on about 120 responses at a response rate of about 12
per cent, so the measure is the view of the parents who chose to give one.
The survey has no item on strictness.

\textbf{Administrative data.} All schools are matched on their unique
reference number to a school-year panel that I built from the Department for
Education's published tables for 2015/16 to 2024/25. The panel carries pupil
composition, prior attainment, Progress~8 and its components, workforce and
finance. This paper uses the panel only to describe the samples; the
following chapter uses it for outcomes and controls. The panel also carries
each school's most recent overall inspection grade. Inspections from
September 2024 onwards no longer award an overall grade, so the grade used
throughout is the last one each school received before that date, on the
scale 1 (Outstanding) to 4 (Inadequate). Schools inspected only since then,
or never inspected, have no grade and are missing on that variable.

\textbf{Representativeness.} \Cref{tab:2A:representativeness} compares the
visited and interviewed schools with the national population. Both samples
resemble the population on school type, phase, admissions, size and urban
location, and they differ from it on deprivation by at most two percentage
points. Both over-represent London (45 per cent of visited schools against
15 per cent nationally), which shows up as a higher share of pupils with
English as an additional language. Both also lean towards stronger schools:
25 per cent of visited schools held an Outstanding inspection grade as at
August 2024 against 15 per cent nationally, and their mean Progress~8 in
2018/19 was $+0.17$ against $+0.04$. That lean is what one would expect from recruiting through headteachers,
since the schools most likely to agree to take part are those that are
confident about what a visitor will find, and it has two consequences. Estimates from the visited
sample apply most directly to a population that is somewhat stronger than
the national average. Secondly, because the sample under-represents the weakest
schools, the range of both culture and outcomes is restricted, which in the
following chapter works against finding associations rather than for them.

\section{Measurement}
\label{sec:p1_measurement}

This section builds the measures in the order that the design requires. It
first constructs the gold-standard scores from the visits and the interviews
and reports how reliable they are. It then explains how the public text was
scored using a large language model and how those scoring rules were tested.
It ends with the prediction models that carry the gold standard to the
schools that were not visited, and with the evidence on how well they do so.

\subsection{The gold standard}

Every observation item and every interview statement is rated from 1 to 5.
The scores are built up in two steps. First, items are averaged into
\emph{sub-scores}, each of which covers one dimension in one setting: warmth
in lessons, warmth outside lessons, strictness in lessons, strictness outside
lessons, and so on. Second, the sub-scores for a dimension are averaged into
that dimension's \emph{score}, so that each school ends up with one warmth
score and one strictness score, and each score is doubled from the 1--5
range of the items to a 0--10 scale. Two sets of scores are constructed and
kept apart. The \emph{enacted} scores use the observations alone. Enacted
warmth is the average of in-lesson warmth (the use of names, praise, the
quality of interaction, pupil participation and motivation) and
out-of-lesson warmth (relationships and interactions during transitions and
breaks, and how consistently rewards are used). Enacted strictness is the
average of in-lesson strictness (misbehaviour tolerated, disruption,
response to sanction, respect and concentration) and out-of-lesson
strictness (how consistently sanctions are used, corridors, arrival, the
dining hall and recreational space). Enacted teaching is the average of the
ten lesson items on teaching practice. The \emph{espoused} scores use the
headteacher's statement ratings alone. Espoused warmth is the average of the
three warmth statements (rewards applied consistently; pupils feel safe;
pupils feel their teachers care) and espoused strictness is the average of
the four strictness statements (sanctions applied consistently; behaviour in
lessons; behaviour outside lessons; teachers feel respected). The two
remaining scored statements, on staff turnover and morale, form a
staff-climate score. The item definitions and the mapping of items to
sub-scores are given in \cref{app:2A:guide}.

Not everything that was recorded enters a score. On the visit, the four
outside-of-lessons items left out are the two on wall displays (their
condition, and their alignment with the school's stated values) and the
two that compare teaching across the day. The display items are left out
because displays are not conduct, and the teaching-comparison items because
the same qualities are rated lesson by lesson. From the interview, the
ratings of how fully the headteacher answered each open question, the
checklist of named behaviour systems and the statement on parental
engagement are left out, and the interview gives no espoused measure of
teaching. The reasons are given below and in full in \cref{app:2A:guide}.

To make the construction concrete, consider the in-lesson strictness
sub-score of a single school. In each of five lessons, two researchers rated
the level of misbehaviour tolerated before a sanction, the frequency of
disruption, the pupils' response to correction, how respectfully they treated
the teacher, and how well they concentrated. Misbehaviour and disruption are
reverse-scored so that a higher value means a stricter lesson. One item
needs a rule. If no pupil was sanctioned during a lesson there is no response
to correction to rate, and the researcher records a zero. That zero is not a
low score, and dropping it would throw away what the lesson shows. It is
therefore replaced by the reverse of the disruption rating, which means that
a lesson with no disruption and no sanction is scored as if the pupils had
responded fully, and a lesson with constant disruption and no sanction as if
they had not responded at all. The five ratings a researcher makes in a
lesson are averaged to give that researcher's value for the lesson, the
lesson values are averaged
across lessons, and the researchers' values are averaged across researchers,
giving one in-lesson strictness figure on the 1--5 scale. That figure is
averaged with the out-of-lesson strictness figure and doubled to give the
school's enacted strictness on the 0--10 scale. Espoused strictness is
simpler: the headteacher's ratings on the four strictness statements are
averaged and doubled. Every school that received a visit has an enacted
score, every school that gave an interview has an espoused score, and
\NTierOne{} schools have both. \Cref{tab:score_dist} reports the distribution
of every sub-score and score across the visited schools.

The two larger exclusions from the interview deserve a word of explanation.
The ratings of how fully the headteacher answered each open question measure
how articulate the head is rather than the culture of the school. They load
on one general factor which tracks the interviewer's rating of the head's
confidence and willingness ($r = 0.34$ to $0.48$), and they are uncorrelated
with the observed scores. A count of the named behaviour systems (S3,
which counts eight of the ten, the two reward systems excluded)
correlates with observed strictness at $-0.01$, compared with $0.41$ for the
statement battery. In other words, the systems a school names are not a
guide to how strictly it runs. Both are kept in the data as variables in
their own right.

Agreement between researchers is moderate at the level of a single item, and
the school-level scores built from many items, lessons and raters are
reliable enough to use, as I show below. Of the 517 lessons observed,
\LessonsTwoRaters{} were rated independently by two researchers and
\LessonsThreeRaters{} by three; in the remaining \LessonsOneRater{} only one
researcher was available for that lesson, and it carries one rating. The
outside-of-lessons form was completed by two or more researchers for
\DaysTwoPlusRaters{} of the \NTierOne{} visits, though individual items
were left blank when not observed, so the item-level comparisons below
rest on fewer days than that. Where two raters saw the
same lesson, the pairwise Pearson correlations across the classroom items
range from $0.53$ to $0.79$; on the outside-of-lessons form, completed once
per day, they range from $0.47$ to $0.81$; and the two raters are within
one point of each other close to 90 per cent of the time
(\cref{tab:irr_classroom,tab:irr_outside}). Agreement is highest on the
items with the most concrete anchors, namely the consistency of the reward
system ($0.81$, a day item) and the frequency of disruption ($0.79$), and
lowest among the classroom items on the teaching-practice ratings that ask
the observer for the most interpretation, such as the use of resources and
the choice of methods ($0.53$ and $0.55$). The interview items were rated
by the interviewer and the observer, with correlations of $0.61$ to $0.74$
and within-one-point agreement at or above 90 per cent
(\cref{tab:irr_interview}). It should be noted that these interview
figures concern the open questions, which enter no score; the espoused
scores of record come from the statement battery, rated by the headteacher
alone, whose reliability evidence is the internal-consistency figures
reported below.

However, the paper's claims do not rest on the agreement of two raters on
one lesson. They rest on the reliability of a school's score once it has
been averaged over its lessons and raters. \Cref{tab:score_reliability} gives
this, and the way it does so is worth explaining. Take every rating that a
researcher gave a lesson for in-lesson strictness: one number, for one
lesson, from one researcher. Those ratings differ from one another for three
reasons. The first is that the lesson was in a stricter or a less strict
school, which is the difference the measure is meant to capture. The second
is that, within the same school, that lesson was calmer or rowdier than the
school's other lessons, because it had a different teacher, a different
class or a different period of the day. The third is that, looking at the
same lesson, one researcher scored it a little higher than the other. The
table divides the spread of the ratings into those three shares. For
in-lesson strictness the shares are \ShareSchoolS{} per cent school,
\ShareLessonS{} per cent lesson and \ShareRaterS{} per cent rater. This means
that well over half of the variation in single-lesson strictness ratings is
lessons within the same school differing from one another, only about a
fifth is schools differing from each other, and under a fifth is the two
researchers disagreeing. For in-lesson warmth the shares are
\ShareSchoolW{}, \ShareLessonW{} and \ShareRaterW{} per cent. Lessons within
a school vary less in warmth than in strictness, but the researchers
disagree more about warmth, which matches the item-level pattern described
above.

This decomposition has two important implications. The first is that
averaging matters. A single lesson is a poor guide to its school, but when
five lessons and the ratings of the researchers who saw them are averaged,
the lesson-to-lesson and rater-to-rater variation is greatly reduced. Averaged over a
school's own lessons and raters, the reliability of the in-lesson warmth
score is \RelWLessons{} and of the in-lesson strictness score \RelSLessons{}. The
outside-of-lessons sub-scores, which summarise a whole day, are more
reliable (\RelWOutside{} and \RelSOutside{}). The in-lesson and out-of-lesson halves of
each enacted score, which were rated in different settings, are correlated
at \HalvesW{} for warmth and \HalvesS{} for strictness across the \NHalves{}
schools, which puts the reliability of each combined enacted score at about
\RelEnactedW{} for warmth and \RelEnactedS{} for strictness. These
figures treat the two settings as parallel forms of one measure; treating
them instead as distinct facets of the construct gives \RelEnactedWUpper{}
and \RelEnactedSUpper{}. The corrections below use the lower figures, so
the corrected correlations are upper bounds. It should also be said that
each school was visited once, so day-to-day variation within a school
cannot be separated from the school itself: these are reliabilities of the
school as seen on its visit day. The espoused
scales, made up of three and four statements, have internal consistencies of
\AlphaWThree{} and \AlphaSFour{}. The lessons that carry only one rating
cost little here. Had every lesson been rated twice, the in-lesson
reliabilities would have been \RelWOneTwoRaters{} and \RelSOneTwoRaters{}
rather than \RelWLessons{} and \RelSLessons{}, because the noise that averaging has to
remove is mostly between lessons and not between raters. They do, however,
carry one caution. Single-rated lessons were rated lower on average than
double-rated ones (a gap of \SingleRatedGapW{} on warmth and
\SingleRatedGapS{} on strictness on the 1--5 scale), and the \SchoolsWithSingleRated{} schools
with such lessons have slightly lower scores as a result ($r =
\CorrSingleShareW{}$ between the share of a school's single-rated lessons
and its in-lesson warmth). The following chapter therefore checks that its
estimates do not depend on those schools.

The second implication concerns schools themselves rather than the
measurement. Behaviour varies more from classroom to classroom within a
school than it does between schools. This means that a school's enacted
score is an average over quite heterogeneous lessons, that a visit of five
lessons measures that average with appreciable error, and that every
correlation with the enacted scores reported below is attenuated as a
result. The attenuation is not large enough to change any conclusion. The
enacted--espoused correlations, corrected for the unreliability of both
sides, rise to about \DisSplitW{} for warmth and \DisSplitS{} for
strictness, which is still far short of what two readings of the same
quantity would give, and the inspection-report strictness correlation of
\CorrOfstedStrictness{}, corrected for the unreliability of the observed
measure, is about \DisOfstedS{}. I show these corrections to give a sense of
scale; the uncorrected figures are the ones reported throughout. Enacted
warmth and strictness are correlated at $r = \CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$
across the \NTierOne{} visited schools, and the espoused pair at
$\CorrWarmthStrictnessEspoused$ across the same schools. The enacted
figure carries the production process inside it, because the same people
produced both scores on the same day. The double-rated lessons allow that
halo to be measured rather than assumed. Within a lesson, one observer's
warmth rating correlates with the other observer's strictness rating at
\CorrWSCrossObs{}, against \CorrWSSameObs{} when both ratings come from
the same observer, so the individual halo is small; correcting the
cross-observer figure for measurement error puts the correlation between
the two traits at about \CorrWSTrue{}. The gap between \CorrWSTrue{} and
$\CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$ reflects the aggregation to school level
and the shared occasion, since both observers formed their impressions at
the same school on the same day. The two dimensions are therefore distinct
constructs whose true correlation is materially lower than the scored
figure, and the scored figure should not be read as a fact about how
strongly warmth and strictness go together in schools.

The gold standard is two measures rather than one because the interview and
the visit disagree about the same school. Enacted and espoused warmth are
correlated at $r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ ($p = \GoldWarmthSplitP$) and enacted
and espoused strictness at $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ ($p
\GoldStrictnessSplitP$), on the \NTierOne{} schools with both. Two measures
that share at most a sixth of their variance are not two fallible readings
of the same quantity, although for strictness, where the corrected
correlation reaches \DisSplitS{}, the statement is one of degree. A weighted average is the right thing to do with two
noisy measures of a common target, because their errors partly cancel out.
It is the wrong thing to do with two measures of different targets, because
it produces a third quantity that is neither of them and whose composition
depends on the weight chosen. I therefore carry the two measures separately
throughout, and their divergence is itself a result, reported in
Section~\ref{sec:p1_results}. Two schools show what the divergence looks
like in the ratings. At the first, the headteacher rated the school 8.0 on
warmth and 8.0 on strictness, and the observers, across their lessons and
the day, recorded 7.9 and 8.5; the in-lesson and out-of-lesson sub-scores
agree with one another to within half a point. At the second, the
headteacher rated warmth 9.3 and strictness 7.8, near the top of the scale,
while the observers recorded 4.6 and 5.9. In-lesson warmth at that school
averaged 2.2 out of 5 and out-of-lesson warmth 2.4, while the head's ratings
on the three warmth statements averaged 4.7 out of 5. There is nothing in
the interview that would tell these two heads apart. Across the \NTierOne{} schools, four
in five have a warmth gap larger than one point on the 0--10 scale and half
have a gap larger than two. One espoused measure can be checked against an
outside record. Heads rated how low their staff turnover was and how high
morale was, and the staff-climate score built from those two statements is
correlated with the school's actual teacher turnover, as recorded by the
Department for Education, at $r = -0.52$, and at $-0.48$ once the other
espoused scores, disadvantage and school size are held constant. On the one
question where a head's answer can be checked against the record, heads
report accurately; it should be noted that this check concerns staff
turnover, and does not speak to the warmth statements.

\subsection{Reading the text with prediction models}
\label{sec:p1_prediction}

The first way of reading the documents uses no theory of culture at all. It
asks a single question: do the words a document uses tend to go with the
culture that observers found in the school?

The method works as follows. Each document is turned into a list of counts:
how often each word, and each two-word phrase, appears in it. Phrases that
appear in almost every document count for little; phrases that distinguish
one document from another count for more. A statistical model is then
fitted on the visited schools. It learns which phrases tend to appear in
the reports of schools observed to be warm, and which in the reports of
schools observed to be cold, and it attaches a small positive or negative
weight to each. To score a new school, the model adds up the weights of the
phrases in its document. No single phrase decides anything: a school's
score is thousands of tiny nudges summed. (For the technical reader: the
representation is TF--IDF over words and bigrams, and the model is a ridge
regression; \cref{sec:p1_app_prediction} gives the specification.)

Three safeguards stop the model from flattering itself.

First, it never grades its own homework. Every school's reported prediction
comes from a model fitted with that school left out, so each number says
how well the model reads a school it has never seen. As a further check, I
re-ran the whole procedure two hundred times on deliberately scrambled
scores; a real result has to beat what scrambling produces, and the
reported significance levels come from that comparison.

Second, it cannot recognise the school. Before anything is counted, school
and trust names, place names, dates, page numbers and standard boilerplate
are removed from every document. Without this step, the model learns to
spot \emph{which} school or which era of report it is reading, rather than
what the report says. With it, the weights fall on ordinary prose.

Third, it gets no credit for reading demographics. A school's intake
statistics predict part of its culture score, and a document often reveals
the intake. So each observed score is first split into the part that
intake statistics explain and the part they do not, and the model is asked
to predict only the second part. A model that merely recognised a
disadvantaged school from its report would score nothing. Results are
shown with and without this adjustment.

More elaborate ways of representing the text were also tried: longer
phrases, topic groupings, and meaning-vectors from a neural network that
scores passages as similar when they say similar things in different
words. None beat simple counting, and \cref{sec:p1_app_prediction} records
them. The counting model is the one reported throughout.

\subsection{Reading the text with marking schemes}
\label{sec:p1_rubrics}

The second way of reading the documents states what to look for. For each
source and dimension I wrote a marking scheme of one page: a sentence
saying what is being measured, five numbered bands, and two or three
tie-break rules. To give the flavour, the inspection-report warmth scheme
sets band~3, the default, as ``a safe and generally positive school,
described through labels, provision or outcomes, without describing how
staff and pupils actually relate'', and band~4 as ``the report describes
the relationships themselves''. All seven schemes are printed in full in
\cref{sec:p1_app_rubrics}.

A language model reads each document against the scheme and returns a band
from 1 to 5, together with one quoted sentence as evidence. The same fixed
model reads every document. The schemes were written once, frozen, and
applied in a single run: nothing was revised after seeing any result, so
the reported numbers are first attempts, not the survivors of tuning.
Applying written coding schemes by machine and validating them against
human benchmarks is an established workflow in economics
\citep{eloundou2024,gilardi2023}; the validation here is stricter than
usual, because the benchmark is what observers recorded in the school, not
agreement with labels on the same text.

Three checks accompany the scores. Thirty documents per source were scored
twice, and the two runs give the same band \pending{retest macro: 96\%} of
the time, with both strictness schemes at 100\%. The whole exercise was
repeated with a second, larger language model; its results are reported
alongside as a sensitivity check and are never used to choose a headline.
And because a model reading a longer document has more evidence to cite,
the correlation between band and document length is reported for every
cell; it is small for inspection reports and substantial for websites,
where it qualifies the affected results.

\subsection{Judging both methods}
\label{sec:p1_judging}

Both readings face the same yardstick: correlation with what trained
observers recorded in the school. Neither is judged by how plausible its
output looks or how well it agrees with anything else. Setting the two
side by side, for each source and each dimension, answers two questions at
once: how much information about culture the public record contains, and
whether extracting it requires a stated theory or only statistics. One
further rule of evidence is applied throughout: if some feature of the
text appears to help predict \emph{every} dimension at once, it is treated
as reading the document rather than the school --- its age, its format,
its length --- and discarded. Several apparent improvements failed exactly
this test.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:p1_results}

This section reports what the measures show. Three results stand out. The
enacted and espoused measures diverge, and they diverge more for warmth than
for strictness. No source outside the visit tracks observed warmth, and the
two sources that contain any information about warmth, the inspection report
and the parent survey, track what heads say instead. Finally, the text of
behaviour policies is unrelated to observed practice.

\textbf{Distributions.} \Cref{tab:score_dist} reported the measures for the
visited schools, and \cref{fig:score_distributions} draws the enacted and
espoused distributions side by side. Enacted warmth across the \NTierOne{}
visited schools has a mean of 6.4 and a standard deviation of 0.9 on the
0--10 scale, and enacted strictness has a mean of 7.0 and a standard
deviation of 0.9. The espoused scores across all \NInterviewed{} interviewed
schools sit higher, with means of \MeanWEspAll{} and \MeanSEspAll{} and
standard deviations of \SDWEspAll{} and \SDSEspAll{}; on the visited
schools alone the espoused means are 8.4 and 7.9
(\cref{tab:score_dist}). The two enacted dimensions are correlated at $r =
\CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$. This means that warm schools tend also to be
orderly ones, which is consistent with the authoritative-school framework
and inconsistent with the idea of a single scale running from warm to
strict. \Cref{fig:quadrants_ch2} plots the two against each other. Splitting
at the medians, 35 schools are warmer and stricter than the median school
and 34 are neither, but 17 are warm without being strict and 17 are strict
without being warm. In other words, all four quadrants of the joint
distribution are populated, and a third of the visited schools sit off the
diagonal. Between-school variation is probably narrow in the English system,
and the compression of the scores partly reflects that. About two-thirds of
secondary schools are graded Good and a further sixth Outstanding, and a
common inspection framework, curriculum and training regime would be
expected to compress the distribution of any measure of culture. A standard
deviation of enacted warmth is therefore about one point on the ten-point
scale, and that is the unit in which the differences below should be read.

\textbf{Enacted and espoused culture diverge.} On the \NTierOne{} schools
with both measures, the two sources are correlated at $\GoldWarmthSplit$ for
warmth and $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ for strictness. This means that the
interview shares about a sixth of its variance with the visit on strictness
and about a sixteenth on warmth. The two correlations are estimated on the
same schools, and the difference between them, $0.16$, has a bootstrap 95
per cent interval of $\SplitDiffLo$ to $\SplitDiffHi$ ($p = \SplitDiffP$).
So the point estimates say that agreement is lower for warmth, but the
sample is too small to establish that difference. A simpler way to see the
size of the gap is to ask where the head and the observers each place the
school among its peers. Of the \TopQN{} schools whose heads rated them in
the top quarter on warmth, only \TopQWMatch{} were in the top quarter as
the observers scored them. Two benchmarks size that number. Under
independence, chance alone would place six or seven of the 26 in the
observers' top quarter; if the head and the observers were measuring the
same thing, with these instruments' reliabilities capping how well the two
sets of scores can agree, the expected match would be about
\TopQExpectedSameW{}. The observed \TopQWMatch{} sits close to the chance
benchmark and far below the error-limited one. For strictness the match is
\TopQSMatch{} of the \TopQN{}, against an error-limited ceiling of about
\TopQExpectedSameS{}: heads' strictness placements are nearly as accurate
as the measures allow. The ceilings are computed by simulation as the
expected top-quartile overlap of two normally distributed measures of one
construct whose correlation is capped by the stated reliabilities. On a
binomial approximation, the warmth shortfall of 8 against 14 is about two
and a half standard deviations, while 8 against the chance benchmark of
six or seven is within noise. Across all four quarters, head and
observers place the school in the same quarter for \SameQuartileW{} per cent
of schools on warmth and \SameQuartileS{} per cent on strictness, against
the 25 per cent that chance would give. Heads also rate their schools near
the top of the warmth statements (a mean of $8.4$ on the 0--10 scale) and
somewhat lower on strictness ($7.9$), while the observers' scores sit at
$6.4$ and $7.0$. The heads' scores and the observers' scores come from
different questions answered by different people, so the fact that the
heads' scores are higher does not by itself show that heads overstate their
schools' warmth. What the rank comparison shows is that a head's warmth
rating tells us little about where the school sits among its peers on the
observed measure. Put simply, what a headteacher says about
relationships is a poorer guide to what a visitor finds than what they say
about behaviour. Within the interview, the items on
which heads report reliably are those about the consistency of practice
(sanctions applied consistently, $r = 0.39$ with observed strictness;
rewards applied consistently, $0.28$ with observed warmth), and not those
about how people feel (pupils feel safe, $0.14$; teachers care, $0.11$ with
observed warmth, neither of them significant). Headteachers report
accurately on whether their systems are applied and inaccurately on the
relational climate. This is the pattern one would expect if the first is
something a head can check and the second is something a head has to infer.

\textbf{Where public text contains information about warmth, it reflects
what is said rather than what is done.} \Cref{tab:source_criterion} sets
each source's warmth and strictness score against the enacted and the
espoused criterion. For strictness, the inspection-report score is
correlated with the observed measure at $\CorrOfstedStrictness$ and with the
espoused measure at $\CorrOfstedStrictEspoused$, while behaviour policies
and websites carry no information about strictness against either. For
warmth, no source is correlated with the observed measure, and the
inspection report and the parent survey are correlated with the espoused one
($0.29$ and $0.31$). The parent survey is the fourth independent group of
respondents, after the observers, the headteachers and the inspectors, and
it shows the same pattern. Parents' reports of their children being happy
and safe are more closely related to what heads say about warmth ($0.31$)
than to what observers record ($0.17$), and they are more closely related
to observed strictness ($0.28$) than to observed warmth. \Cref{fig:crossover_warmth} draws the warmth half of the
table. What is written and reported about a school's warmth, by inspectors,
by parents and by the school itself, follows the school's account of itself
and not the classroom. There are two possible readings of this. One is that
the school's account is the common source: a head who describes the school
as warm describes it that way to inspectors and to parents, and both repeat
it. The other is that inspectors and parents see the same presented version of
the school that the interview draws out, while the classroom and the
corridor, which observers see, are where warmth is or is not put into
practice. The data do not separate the two readings, but
both imply that a national measure of enacted warmth cannot be read from
any of these sources.

\textbf{Behaviour policies describe aspiration.} A behaviour policy is
written by the school, so one might expect its warmth score to track what
the head says even if it does not track what observers see. In fact it
tracks neither. The behaviour-policy warmth score is uncorrelated with
observed warmth ($0.01$), with espoused warmth ($-0.06$) and with the parent
survey ($-0.04$), and the strictness score is uncorrelated with observed
strictness ($-0.08$) and with espoused strictness ($-0.01$). One school in
ten publishes a policy written by its trust rather than by the school, and a
template shared across a trust's schools cannot vary with the culture of each
one. Written behaviour policies are governance documents that state
intentions, and the schools that state the warmest intentions are not, on
these measures, the warmest schools. The same result appears in the
following chapter, where the behaviour-policy warmth score is, if anything,
negatively associated with pupil progress.

\textbf{What the inspection-report text does and does not contain.} Two further results
qualify the inspection-report scores. The warmth score moves with the
inspection grade ($r = -0.40$; a lower grade is a better school) even though
every grade has been stripped from the text it reads, and it does so more
than the reference raters reading the same text ($-0.25$). This suggests that inspectors write about relationships in a tone that
moves with their overall verdict, and that the model picks that tone up. The
score is therefore best understood as a description of the report rather
than as a clean measure of the school. The strictness score also
moves with the verdict ($-0.49$), but unlike warmth it tracks observed
practice as well, and in the following chapter its association with pupil
progress survives conditioning on the grade. It should also be noted that a
report predates the visit, often by years. Measured from the day of the
visit, the median report was \AgeMedian{} years old, a quarter were under
\AgeQOne{} years old and a quarter over \AgeQThree{}. Stale text
should weaken any correlation with current practice, so I recomputed the
strictness correlation on the \NRecent{} schools with the more recent
reports and the \NOlder{} with the older ones (the split is at the median
age; one school's report date is missing): the figures are
$\RStrictRecent$ and $\RStrictOlder$. The correlation does not fall with the
age of the report, and the warmth null holds in both halves
($\RWarmRecent$ and $\RWarmOlder$). What an inspector wrote about conduct
some years ago still describes the school that the observers saw, which says
something about the persistence of the dimension as well as about the text.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:p1_conclusion}

In this paper I have constructed measures of warmth and strictness for
English secondary schools from direct observation, headteacher interviews
and public text, and asked how well each dimension can be recovered from
data that exist for every school. There are three main things to take from
it.

The first is that strictness can be predicted to a modest but useful degree
from publicly available data, and warmth could not be predicted by any
instrument tested. The strictness score read from an
inspection report is correlated with observed strictness at
$\CorrOfstedStrictness$, and a model that uses only text scores predicts
observed strictness for schools it was not fitted on with a correlation of
$\RidgeBStrictness$; both figures carry a little selection optimism, as
the methods section records. No text source is correlated with observed
warmth, and the corresponding warmth model predicts nothing. The difference between the
two dimensions appears to lie in the documents rather than in the scoring
method, for two reasons. The versions of the warmth scoring rules that
agreed most closely with their labels were no better at matching
observation than the versions that agreed least, so better rules did not
produce a better measure. And the inspection-report warmth score is related
to what heads say and to the inspectors' grades, so the method does extract
something from the reports; what it extracts is simply not related to what
observers see in the school. Inspectors record conduct and enforcement in detail but
describe relationships in a tone that moves with their overall verdict. Schools' own
documents describe intentions, and websites are not written about the things
that observers see. For the school-climate literature this is a caution: the
sources that are cheap at national scale contain information about one of
the two dimensions and not the other, and a survey of parents contains the
same one.

The second is that espoused and enacted culture diverge, and in point
estimate more so for warmth, although the difference between the two
correlations is imprecisely estimated. What heads report about their
school's warmth is only weakly related to what observers record: of the
\TopQN{} schools whose heads placed them in the top quarter for warmth,
only \TopQWMatch{} were there as observed, against an error-limited
ceiling of \TopQExpectedSameW{} had the two sets of scores measured the
same thing. Their reports of whether the
school's behaviour systems are applied consistently, on the other hand, do
track observed strictness. No
source outside the visit tracks observed warmth, and the two that contain any
information about warmth, the inspection report and a survey of parents,
agree with the headteacher instead. The gap between what is said about a
school and what is seen in it is specific to warmth, and it shows up in three
independent accounts of the school, the headteacher's, the inspector's and
the parents', which suggests that it is a feature of how schools present
themselves rather than a peculiarity of any one measure. For the
school-effectiveness tradition, which has measured ethos largely through
leaders' and teachers' accounts, the result says which parts of those
accounts can be trusted: the parts about whether systems are applied, and not
the parts about how people feel.

The third concerns the scoring method itself. How closely a large language
model's scoring rule agrees with the labels it was developed against says
nothing about whether its scores reflect what happens in the school. Across
every version I tested the two were unrelated, and the best-agreeing version
was usually the worse measure. Rules that ask the model for facts worked better than rules that ask it
for judgements. The correlation between two text scores partly reflects the
way the scores were produced rather than the schools themselves, so the
question of whether warmth and strictness go together in schools has to be
answered from observation. Finally, the same rule moved to a different
model lost most of its agreement. These results apply to any study that scores text with a language
model against a set of labels, and they argue for testing every such scoring
rule against something outside the labels rather than against agreement
alone.

Within its scope the paper delivers two measures that can be used with
confidence. The enacted scores are a two-dimensional measure of school
culture for the visited schools, built from direct observation, whose
reliability is reported and adequate. The inspection-report strictness score
extends one of the two dimensions to the national population. The visited
sample leans towards London and towards stronger schools, and each school
was seen on one day, so the following chapter treats the measures
accordingly. It asks what the two dimensions, measured in this way, are worth
in terms of pupil progress.

\section{Scoring Guide and Item Definitions}
\label{app:2A:guide}

This section sets out how the individual items on the forms combine into
the nine sub-scores and the two sets of scores. The items themselves, that
is, the question wording, the response format and the anchor description
attached to each rating level, are reproduced in full in
\cref{app:2A:interview_guide} for the interview and in
\cref{app:2A:visit_protocol} for the two observation forms.

Every item is recorded on a 1--5 scale. Two observation items can also take
a zero, with a distinct meaning in each case. A zero on \emph{response of
pupils to sanctions} records that no sanction was issued during the lesson,
and a zero on \emph{effectiveness of pupil discussion} records that no
discussion was initiated. The first of these is not a genuine low score,
because an orderly lesson in which no sanction was needed should not be
counted as one in which sanctions were ignored. It is therefore replaced by
$6 - \text{disruption}$, which gives a calm unsanctioned lesson a high value
and a disrupted unsanctioned lesson a low one. The second is treated as a
genuine negative, since a lesson containing no discussion did not achieve
effective discussion.

Observation item scores are averaged across lessons and across researchers
to give one value per school before the sub-scores are formed.
\Cref{tab:p1_subscore_items} lists the constituent items of each sub-score.

\input{snippets/subscore_items}

\paragraph{Items recorded but not scored.} Every item on the lesson
observation form enters a sub-score. Four items on the outside-of-lessons
form do not. Two of them, the consistency of verbal feedback and the quality
of differentiation across lessons, are recorded as cross-lesson summaries of
the corresponding lesson observation items, and so they enter no sub-score
of their own, because the lesson items already capture them. The other two,
the condition of wall and corridor displays and the extent to which displays
align with the school's stated values, are ratings of the building rather
than of conduct or relationships, and they fit neither dimension as it is
defined. Both are retained in the data.

From the interview, three components are recorded but not scored. The
first is the set of ratings of how fully the headteacher answered each open
question (Q5 to Q8, Q11 and Q12). These measure how articulate the head is
rather than how the school runs. They load on one general factor that tracks
the interviewer's ratings of the head's confidence and willingness, and they
are uncorrelated with the observed scores. The second is the checklist of
ten named behaviour systems, counted as S3. This correlates with observed
strictness at $-0.01$, against $0.41$ for the statement battery, and so it
enters no espoused score. Of the ten systems, S3 counts the eight that
represent behavioural or academic requirements and omits the two reward
systems (reward assemblies and reward points), whose presence indicates a
warmer rather than a stricter regime, and it is the count of those eight
rescaled to 0--5. The third is the tenth statement, on parental engagement,
which belongs to neither dimension. Two further statements, on staff turnover
and morale, are scored but form the staff-climate score SC rather than a
culture score, and there is no espoused measure of teaching. The remaining
questions (Q1 to Q4, Q9 and Q10, which cover length of tenure, the
description of the school's culture and how it is communicated, the
characteristics of an effective school, the staff structure and
accountability) were asked for context, and their ratings, where they were
taken, enter no score.

Every other sub-score is the mean of its items, computed over the items
present so that a missing item reduces the number of contributing items
rather than the school's score.

The sub-scores combine into two sets of scores, grouped by source, with
observed behaviour and stated philosophy kept apart:
%
\begin{align}
\text{Enacted:}\quad
  W^{\text{enac}} &= \overline{(W1, W2)}, &
  S^{\text{enac}} &= \overline{(S1, S2)}, &
  T^{\text{enac}} &= T1 \label{eq:enacted} \\
\text{Espoused:}\quad
  W^{\text{esp}}  &= W3, &
  S^{\text{esp}}  &= S4, &
  \mathrm{SC} &= \overline{(\text{SC}_{1}, \text{SC}_{2})} \label{eq:espoused}
\end{align}
%
There is no espoused teaching score. The two interview statements under the
teaching heading concern staff turnover and morale, and they form the staff
climate score SC instead. The systems count $S3$ enters no espoused score.

Schools with an interview but no visit have the espoused set of scores alone
(every visited school was also interviewed). No imputation is attempted
across the two sets, since the espoused and enacted scores are correlated at
only $r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ for warmth and $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ for
strictness, which is too little for either to stand in for the other. All
scores are finally doubled from the 1--5 item scale to the 0--10 range.

The mapping in \cref{tab:p1_subscore_items} is generated directly from the
scoring code, so it cannot drift from what the scoring code does.

\section{Prediction Model Specification}
\label{app:2A:ridge_spec}

The interview-tier model (Model~A) predicts the enacted score of each
dimension from the vector $\mathbf{f}_s$ of interview sub-scores ($W3$, $S3$,
$S4$) and the three text scores for the dimension,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ridge_interview}
  \hat{y}_s = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}' \mathbf{f}_s ,
\end{equation}
%
and the national-tier model (Model~B) from the three text scores alone,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ridge_national}
  \hat{y}_s = \hat{\alpha} + \beta_O x_{O,s} + \beta_P x_{P,s} + \beta_W x_{W,s} ,
\end{equation}
%
where $x_{O,s}$ is the inspection-report score, $x_{P,s}$ is the
behaviour-policy score and $x_{W,s}$ is the website score for school~$s$.
Both are ridge regressions fitted on the \RidgeTrainN{} visited schools that
have a complete set of predictors. The penalty $\lambda$ is re-chosen
inside each fold of the leave-one-out loop, so the held-out school plays
no part in choosing it; the $\lambda$ reported in the tables is the
full-sample choice, which the final fitted model uses. Predictions are
clipped to the range $[0, 10]$. The fitted coefficients are reported in the
chapter (\cref{tab:interview_model_coefs,tab:national_model_coefs}).

The national-tier model takes the language model's overall score for each
document as a single number per source. This raises the question of whether
the model's reading of the document contributes anything that a simple
word-counting rule could not recover from the same text.
\Cref{tab:ridge_spec_comparison} tests this directly. It compares two sets
of predictors under an identical estimation procedure: (A) the three
language-model scores that are used throughout the chapter, and (B) the
keyword-rule scores computed by a pattern matcher that runs alongside the
language model on the same documents (two rules, for the inspection
reports and the behaviour policies; no keyword rule exists for the
websites). The second of these provides a floor
against which to judge whether the language model contributes anything
beyond counting terms.

\input{tables/tab_ridge_spec_comparison}

The comparison uses the same \RidgeTrainN{} schools in every cell, so it
is like for like. The result differs by dimension, and it splits in the same
way as the rest of the chapter.

On strictness, the language model's reading contributes something that the
word-counting rule cannot recover. The language-model specification reaches
$r = \RidgeBStrictness$ with a cross-validated $R^2$ of $0.099$, while the
keyword baseline on the same documents reaches $r = 0.03$ with an $R^2$
below zero, which is no better than predicting the sample mean.
This means that whatever the strictness score captures, it is not the
frequency of particular words. The information comes from reading the
document rather than from any countable property of the text, which is the
opposite of what one would expect if the score were merely picking up word
frequencies.

On warmth, neither specification recovers anything. Both select a penalty at
or near the top of the grid and shrink almost entirely to the training mean,
and both return a negative cross-validated $R^2$ ($-0.023$ for the
language-model scores and $-0.040$ for the keyword rules). This means that
each predicts the enacted warmth score worse than the sample mean does, and
in both cases the predictions are too nearly constant for the correlation
to be informative, and it is not reported. This is not evidence that the warmth score is empty, since
\cref{tab:source_criterion} shows that it is correlated with the espoused
criterion. What it does establish is that the part of the warmth score that
survives to the national tier contains no information about enacted warmth
for either set of predictors to find. The comparison can only discriminate
between two ways of reading the text where there is something in the text to
read.
