\begin{chapterabstract}
This chapter develops and validates a two-dimensional measurement framework for
school culture---warmth and strictness---in English secondary schools. Using
data from \NInterviewed{} headteacher interviews and \NTierOne{} structured school
visits, I construct
gold-standard scores for \NTierOne{} full-data schools and calibrate Ridge regression
models that extend measurement to \NInterviewed{} interview-tier schools and the full national
population of approximately 3,330 state secondary schools. The framework rests on a
three-tier prediction cascade: direct observation provides the benchmark; an
interview-tier Ridge model combines interview sub-scores with text features from
Ofsted reports and behaviour policies; and a national-tier model uses text features
alone. Interview and visit evidence are kept as two scores rather than averaged
into one: what a headteacher says the school is like and what a visit records
agree only weakly (warmth $r = \GoldWarmthSplit$, strictness
$r = \GoldStrictnessSplit$), which is too
little shared variance for a weighted average of the two to estimate anything
either one measures. Separating them shows the two dimensions behave
differently in public text. Strictness converges across independent kinds of
observer: Ofsted-derived strictness tracks both what a visit records
($r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$, $p < 0.001$) and what the head espouses
($r = \CorrOfstedStrictEspoused$, $p < 0.001$). Warmth is the construct that
dissociates: no public-text source tracks warmth as enacted. Only strictness
survives to the national tier: the
national-tier model attains a leave-one-out cross-validated
$r = \RidgeBStrictness$ for enacted strictness and
$r = \RidgeBWarmth$---no better than the sample mean---for enacted warmth. The
inter-rater reliability of the observational
instruments is high, with within-one-point agreement exceeding 90\% for all
interview items.
\end{chapterabstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:p1_intro}

The academic progress that pupils make in English secondary schools varies
enormously across institutions that appear, on many observable dimensions, to be
similar. A growing body of evidence points to school culture---the shared norms,
expectations, and relational patterns that govern daily life in a school---as an
important driver of this variation, but systematic measurement of culture has
remained elusive. Prior attempts to quantify school culture have relied on
survey-based instruments, administrative proxies such as exclusion rates, or
inspection judgements: each of these approaches captures some signal about
culture but is subject to important limitations. Survey responses reflect
perceptions rather than observed behaviour; administrative records conflate
culture with pupil composition; and inspection judgements aggregate many
different features of school quality into a single rating. The absence of a
reliable, scalable measurement framework has made it difficult to study the
relationship between school culture and pupil outcomes in a rigorous way.

This chapter develops a two-dimensional measurement framework for school
culture in English secondary schools, centred on \textit{warmth}---the degree
to which school staff convey genuine care, positive regard, and a sense of
belonging to pupils---and \textit{strictness}---the degree to which a school
holds pupils to high behavioural standards and enforces those standards
consistently. These two dimensions are conceptually motivated by the authoritative
schooling tradition in developmental psychology, which draws on the parenting
styles typology of \citet{maccoby1983}: an authoritative approach combines high
demandingness with high responsiveness and is consistently associated with the
most positive outcomes for children. The application of this framework to school
settings implies that the most effective schools are those that are simultaneously
high on strictness and warmth, maintaining an orderly and demanding environment
while expressing genuine care for pupils. The conceptual framework is developed
in \cref{sec:p1_cf}.

The empirical contribution of this chapter lies in the construction and
validation of warmth and strictness scores across three tiers of measurement
intensity, using a prediction cascade calibrated to a gold standard. For
\NTierOne{} full-data schools, an enacted score is constructed from direct
classroom and outside-of-lesson observations and an espoused score from a
structured headteacher interview. These schools also serve as the training
dataset for two calibration models. The first---a Ridge regression of gold
standard scores on interview-derived sub-scores and features extracted from
publicly available Ofsted inspection reports and behaviour policy
documents---is applied to generate predicted warmth and strictness scores for
all 303 schools in the interview tier. The second---a Ridge
regression of gold standard scores on the text-derived features
alone---generates predicted scores for the full national population of
approximately 3,330 open state secondary schools.
The validity of both models is assessed via leave-one-out cross-validation on
the \RidgeTrainN{} full-data schools. The data sources and tier
structure are described in \cref{sec:p1_data}; the gold standard scoring
protocol is detailed in \cref{sec:p1_gss}; the interview-tier calibration
model is described in \cref{sec:p1_enriched}; and the national-tier model is
described in \cref{sec:p1_text}.

Across Q2--Q12, questioner--observer Pearson correlations range from $r = 0.61$
to $r = 0.74$ (median $r = 0.70$), with within-one-point agreement exceeding 90\%
for every item, providing reassurance that interview-derived sub-scores are not
primarily artefacts of idiosyncratic researcher interpretation. The visit and
interview components of the gold standard capture empirically distinct aspects of
school culture: enacted and espoused warmth scores correlate at
$r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ and the corresponding strictness scores at
$r = \GoldStrictnessSplit$, with a systematic gap of $+0.51$ points on warmth in
the self-report direction but no detectable gap on strictness ($+0.07$ points,
$p = 0.53$). They are kept as two scores for that reason rather than averaged
into one. At the national scale, text-based
strictness is a valid proxy ($r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$, $p < 0.001$, against directly
observed strictness). Text-based warmth is not: across three independent
sources---Ofsted, behaviour policies, and school websites---the warmth signal
predicts what schools espouse and not what a visit records, collectively
establishing the espoused--enacted gap as a structural feature of the data
rather than a measurement artefact of any single source.

The remainder of this chapter is organised as follows. \Cref{sec:p1_lit}
reviews the related literature. \Cref{sec:p1_cf} presents the conceptual
framework. \Cref{sec:p1_data} describes the data. \Cref{sec:p1_gss} details
the gold standard score. \Cref{sec:p1_irr} presents inter-rater reliability
results. \Cref{sec:p1_enriched} describes the main and enriched analyses.
\Cref{sec:p1_text} describes the text-based scoring. \Cref{sec:p1_results}
presents findings, and \cref{sec:p1_conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:p1_lit}

This chapter draws on five strands of related work: the measurement of school
climate and culture; the authoritative schooling tradition; structured classroom
observation; research on behaviour management practices; and text-based
measurement in economics.

\paragraph{School climate and culture.}
The study of school climate has a long history in educational research
\citep{hoy2001,cohen2009,thapa2013}. The predominant measurement approach relies on survey instruments
administered to pupils, teachers, and sometimes parents, capturing dimensions
such as safety, teacher--pupil relationships, academic expectations, and
institutional support. While this literature has produced consistent findings
in some areas---school climate is positively associated with pupil wellbeing,
attendance, and broadly measured achievement---survey measures are subject to
several limitations: they reflect perceptions rather than observed behaviour;
they are susceptible to social desirability bias; and they are costly to
administer systematically at national scale. Administrative records such as
suspension and exclusion rates have been used as proxies for the strictness of
a school's disciplinary environment \citep{machin2020}, but these conflate
genuine culture with pupil composition. Ofsted inspection judgements provide a
widely used summary of school quality but aggregate many dimensions into a
single rating and are updated infrequently. The present chapter addresses these
limitations by combining direct observational data with headteacher self-report
and, for the national sample, text-based scoring of publicly available documents.

\paragraph{The authoritative school.}
The conceptual foundation for the warmth and strictness dimensions is the
parenting styles typology of \citet{maccoby1983}, which classifies parenting
on two orthogonal axes: demandingness (high expectations, clear rules,
consistent enforcement) and responsiveness (warmth, sensitivity, emotional
support). The \textit{authoritative} style---high on both dimensions---is
consistently associated with the most positive outcomes across a wide range of
cognitive and non-cognitive domains. \citet{majumder2015} finds that children
with authoritative parents are significantly more likely to complete higher
levels of education, with effects substantially larger than those of other
parenting styles relative to uninvolved parenting. \citet{zhang2020} find that
both high demandingness and high responsiveness have independent positive effects
on children's academic outcomes, with high responsiveness additionally promoting
the formation of cognitive and socio-emotional skills.

The application of this framework to schools is conceptually natural:
demandingness maps onto the strictness dimension and responsiveness maps onto
the warmth dimension. The hypothesis that schools combining high levels of both
dimensions---analogous to authoritative parents---generate the greatest progress
for their pupils is central to the thesis. \citet{gregory2010} develops the
authoritative school concept in the educational psychology literature; related
empirical evidence is reviewed in \citet{gregory2010,gottfredson2005}.

\paragraph{Structured classroom observation.}
The most closely related methodological precedent for the observation component
of the gold standard score is the Classroom Assessment Scoring System (CLASS),
developed by \citet{hamre2007}. The CLASS framework assesses teachers on three
domains: emotional support (teacher--pupil relationships, positive climate,
sensitivity), classroom organisation (behaviour management, productivity), and
instructional support (quality of feedback, concept development). \citet{allen2013}
find that secondary school teachers with higher CLASS scores generate
significantly greater pupil achievement gains. \citet{sammons2008} apply the
framework in English primary schools and find that teaching quality as measured
by CLASS has positive effects on pupil outcomes in reading and mathematics that
exceed the influence of family disadvantage as measured by eligibility for Free
School Meals.

The warmth and strictness observation protocols developed in this chapter draw
on the emotional support and classroom organisation domains of the CLASS
framework, adapted to characterise institution-level culture rather than
individual teacher practice. The key adaptation is that observations across
multiple classrooms and outside-of-lesson settings are combined to produce a
single measure of the school-level norm, rather than an assessment of any
individual teacher.

\paragraph{Behaviour management and school-level practices.}
Research on high-performing charter schools in the United States has identified
structured behaviour management as a key distinguishing feature of effective
schools. \citet{abdulkadiroglu2011} find that effective Boston charter schools
are more likely to use strict discipline and produce large test score gains for
middle and high school students. \citet{angrist2010} report substantial
achievement gains at KIPP Lynn, a school featuring a demanding behavioural code
and high expectations, with the largest gains for pupils with the lowest baseline
scores. \citet{dobbie2013} investigate 35 charter schools and find that an
index of five practices---including high expectations and frequent teacher
feedback---explains approximately half of the variation in school effectiveness,
while traditional resource inputs do not. \citet{fryer2014} implements this
bundle in low-performing traditional schools in Houston using a field experiment
and finds significant mathematics gains. In the English context,
\citet{machin2020} show that higher exclusion rates in the first wave of
academies reflect genuine disciplinary rigour rather than the strategic
manipulation of test scores. This body of evidence motivates the strictness
dimension and provides grounds for expecting a positive association between
strictness and pupil progress.

\paragraph{Text-based measurement.}
The text-scoring approach in \cref{sec:p1_text} builds on the literature
treating text as data in economics \citep{gentzkow2019}. A key challenge in
applying automated text scoring to domain-specific documents is ensuring that
the method is calibrated to the relevant linguistic context.
\citet{loughran2011} demonstrate that standard general-purpose sentiment
dictionaries misclassify a large proportion of words in financial documents.
\citet{muddiman2019} develop a manually validated domain-specific dictionary
for political text and show it outperforms standard approaches. The present
chapter addresses the domain-specificity challenge by using a large language
model prompted with detailed descriptions of the warmth and strictness
constructs and calibrated to the vocabulary of English school policy documents
and Ofsted inspection reports. The text-based scores are validated against the
direct-observational gold standard for the \NTierOne{} full-data
schools.

\section{Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:p1_cf}

A school's culture can be characterised along many dimensions. This chapter
focuses on two: warmth and strictness. Both have antecedents in existing research
on school effectiveness and school climate, but have not previously been
operationalised at scale using direct observational data combined with headteacher
self-report and text-derived indicators.

\textbf{Warmth} is defined here as the degree to which school staff convey
genuine care, personal interest, and a sense of belonging to students. A warm
school is one in which teachers know pupils as individuals, communicate positive
regard consistently, and make pastoral relationships a visible priority. At the
level of classroom instruction, warmth is reflected in the use of students'
names, the quality of teacher--pupil interactions, and the frequency and
genuineness of praise. At the school level, warmth is reflected in the
headteacher's expressed belief that relationships are foundational to learning,
in structural commitments to pupil wellbeing, and in the emotional atmosphere
observed in corridors and common areas.

\textbf{Strictness} is defined here as the degree to which a school holds pupils
to high behavioural and academic standards and enforces those standards
consistently. A strict school is one in which expectations for conduct are
clearly communicated, sanctions are applied in a predictable and graduated
manner, and there is a low tolerance for disruption in lessons and disorder in
public spaces. Strictness as used here is distinct from harshness or punitivism:
it refers to clarity and consistency of expectation, rather than to the severity
of sanction. A school that deploys a mobile phone ban, line-up systems, and
centralised detentions applied uniformly by all staff is a strict school in this
sense, regardless of whether it is also a warm one.

These two dimensions are conceptually orthogonal: they can co-exist
independently. A school can be simultaneously warm and strict, warm but not
strict, strict but not warm, or neither. The combination of high warmth and high
strictness has been described as the \textit{authoritative} model in the school
culture literature \citep{gregory2010}, drawing on Baumrind's typology of parenting
styles \citep{baumrind1971}, and is associated with higher pupil engagement and
attainment in a range of settings \citep{pellerin2005, gregory2011}. A central question addressed
in \cref{ch:paper2} is whether this association holds for English secondary
schools, conditional on a rich set of observable school and pupil
characteristics.

The framework presented in this chapter operationalises both dimensions using
multiple data sources at different levels of observational proximity. Classroom
observations provide the most direct evidence on warmth and strictness as
experienced by pupils during lessons. Outside-of-lesson observations extend this
to the school environment more broadly. Headteacher interviews capture the
philosophy and structural systems that the leadership team deploys to
sustain---or fail to sustain---warmth and strictness as school-wide norms.
Finally, text from publicly available Ofsted inspection reports and behaviour
policy documents extends the measurement framework to the full national
population of English secondary schools.

A third dimension, teaching practice, is also scored and reported throughout
this chapter. It is not treated as a component of school culture in the same
sense as warmth and strictness, but as a related construct of independent
substantive interest. It also serves as an external validity check: if the
measures proposed here are capturing what they claim to capture, teaching
practice scores should correlate positively with warmth and strictness scores
whilst remaining empirically distinguishable from both.

This framework sits within a long tradition of asking whether the school a child
attends makes a difference beyond what that child brings to it. The tradition
begins with a negative answer: \citet{coleman1966} found school resources to
explain little of the variation in attainment once family background was
accounted for. The effective schools literature that followed argued that this
was a failure of measurement rather than a fact about schools---that the
variables surveyed (expenditure, class size, facilities) were simply not the
variables that mattered \citep{edmonds1979}. \citet{rutter1979}, studying twelve
inner-London secondary schools, located the difference instead in what they
termed \textit{ethos}: the aggregate of expectations, incentives, and staff
practice that a school sustains as a routine. Subsequent syntheses converged on
a similar list of correlates---purposeful leadership, high expectations
consistently communicated, an orderly learning environment, and a positive,
supportive climate \citep{sammons1995, teddlie2000}. Warmth and strictness as
defined here are an attempt to make two of those correlates measurable at the
scale of a national population rather than a case-study sample, which is the
constraint that has kept the ethos strand largely qualitative.

The distinction between school \textit{climate} and school \textit{culture}
matters for what the measures in this chapter are claiming to capture.
\citet{vanhoutte2005} argues that the two are routinely conflated: climate
properly refers to how members of a school \emph{perceive} their environment,
and is measured by aggregating individual perceptions, whereas culture refers to
the shared norms and assumptions that govern behaviour, and is a property of the
organisation rather than of the individuals reporting on it. The measures
developed here are aimed at culture in that sense. Classroom and
outside-of-lesson observations record enacted norms directly rather than
perceptions of them; the headteacher interview elicits the system the leadership
has put in place rather than any individual's satisfaction with it; and the text
sources record what the school states institutionally. Where perception data are
used, as with Parent View in \cref{sec:p1_text_valid}, they are used as an
external comparison against the culture measures rather than as a component of
them.

The interview component carries an assumption that deserves stating plainly:
that the headteacher's account of school-wide expectations is informative about
the norms actually operating in the school. That assumption is defensible on the
grounds that behaviour policy in English secondary schools is unusually
centralised---sanction systems, phone policies, and uniform enforcement are set
at whole-school level and are the headteacher's direct responsibility---but it
is not self-evidently true, and headteachers have an obvious incentive to
describe their school favourably. This chapter does not rely on that assumption
being true. The three-tier structure exists precisely so that the interview
account can be checked against something else: for the \NTierOne{} gold-standard schools
the interview score can be compared directly with what was observed on the day,
and \cref{sec:p1_policy_aspiration} reports where the two diverge. The
espoused--enacted gap
that emerges from that comparison is treated as a substantive finding rather
than as measurement error to be minimised.

\section{Data Sources and the Three-Tier Structure}
\label{sec:p1_data}

The warmth and strictness measures are constructed from three distinct data
sources, applied in combination to three overlapping sets of schools. The
three-tier structure reflects the practical constraint that the most
resource-intensive data collection---direct observation---was feasible for only a
subset of schools.

\textbf{Tier~1: Full-data schools.} A total of \NTierOne{} secondary schools in England
received a full research visit comprising structured classroom observations and
outside-of-lesson observations, conducted alongside a semi-structured headteacher
interview. These schools constitute the full-data tier and provide the most
comprehensive basis for measurement. Scores for full-data schools are denoted
the \textit{gold standard} and serve as the benchmark against which the validity
of scores constructed from more limited data is assessed.

\textbf{Tier~2: Interview schools.} An additional 201 schools participated
in a headteacher interview but did not receive a school visit.\footnote{Interviews were
conducted with the headteacher---or, where the headteacher was unavailable, the
most senior member of staff responsible for school culture and behaviour---between
November~2023 and April~2025, spanning the academic years 2023/24 and 2024/25.
Just over three-quarters of interviews (77~per cent) were conducted from
September~2024 onwards. Schools were recruited through direct
outreach to headteachers, supplemented by referrals from participating
schools.} All Tier~2 schools also have publicly available Ofsted inspection
reports and behaviour policy documents. Warmth and strictness scores for Tier~2
schools are generated by applying the interview-tier calibration model
(\cref{sec:p1_enriched}), which combines interview-derived sub-scores with the
text-derived features from those documents.

\textbf{Tier~3: National schools.} All open state secondary schools in
England---approximately 3,330 schools, including all Tier~1 and
Tier~2 schools---have publicly available Ofsted inspection reports and behaviour
policy documents. For the 3,029 schools not in the interview tier,
warmth and strictness scores are generated by applying the national-tier
calibration model (\cref{sec:p1_text}), which uses only text-derived features
from these documents.

The three tiers are nested by data richness: Tier~1 schools have visit,
interview, and public-data sources; Tier~2 schools have interview and
public-data sources; Tier~3 schools have public-data sources only.
\cref{tab:tier_summary} summarises the distribution of schools across tiers,
together with key observable characteristics. The tiers are closely comparable
on free school meal eligibility, median size, and inspection grade. They differ
on one dimension: Tier~1 schools have a mean EAL share of 27.8~per~cent against
18.2~per~cent nationally, reflecting the London weighting of the visit sample.
This imbalance is examined in \cref{app:2A:sample} and recurs as an
interpretive caveat where the interview sample is used. The predictive accuracy of the
two calibration models is quantified via leave-one-out cross-validation on the
100 Tier~1 schools for which all three data sources are observed; results are
reported in \cref{sec:p1_enriched} and \cref{sec:p1_text_valid}.

\input{tables/tab_tier_summary}

In addition to the novel data collected for this study, all schools in Tier~3
are matched to the Department for Education's school-level administrative panel
dataset, which provides information on pupil demographics, prior attainment,
Progress~8 outcomes, workforce characteristics, and financial inputs. This panel
dataset is used exclusively as a source of control variables in the analysis of
\cref{ch:paper2}; it does not contribute to the warmth or strictness scores.

\textbf{Selection and representativeness.} Schools were not selected at random
for the visit or interview programmes. Recruitment relied on direct outreach to
headteachers via email and phone, supplemented by referrals from participating
schools; no quota sampling by region, school type, or Ofsted grade was applied.
\Cref{tab:2A:representativeness} and \cref{fig:2A:representativeness_region} in
\cref{app:2A:sample} compare key observable characteristics of the visited, interviewed,
and national populations; a detailed discussion is provided there. Both samples are
broadly representative on most structural characteristics---school type, phase, gender
composition, admissions policy, urban--rural classification, size, and deprivation---but
over-represent London and the South East relative to their national shares, with the
visited sample particularly concentrated in London (44.6\% of visited schools versus
15.0\% nationally). Where the samples differ systematically from the national
population, the estimates presented in \cref{ch:paper2} should be interpreted as
applying to a population similar in composition to the sample studied, rather
than to the full population of English state secondary schools.

\section{The Gold Standard Score}
\label{sec:p1_gss}

The gold standard score is constructed from three classes of evidence:
structured classroom observations, structured outside-of-lesson observations,
and headteacher interview responses. Nine sub-scores are computed from these
three sources and combined into two parallel sets of warmth, strictness, and
teaching practice scores: an \emph{enacted} set built from the observational
evidence and an \emph{espoused} set built from the interview. The two are
reported side by side and are not averaged into a single composite; the reasons
are set out in \cref{sec:p1_composite,sec:p1_convergence}. All sub-scores are
defined on a $[0, 5]$ scale before rescaling to $[0, 10]$ for reporting.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsection{Classroom Observations}
\label{sec:p1_obs_class}

Classroom observations were conducted using a standardised coding schedule
applied by trained researchers. Each lesson was observed in its entirety by two
researchers working independently. Ratings were made on seven items for the
warmth and strictness dimensions, and nine items for the teaching practice
dimension. All items were scored on a 1--5 scale unless noted. Where a school
received multiple lesson observations, item ratings were averaged across lessons
before computing sub-scores.

\textit{Warmth-relevant items.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Names}: frequency with which the teacher used students' names
  during the lesson (1~=~rarely or never; 5~=~consistently throughout).
  \item \textbf{Praise}: frequency and quality of praise for student effort and
  progress (1~=~rare or absent; 5~=~frequent and genuine).
  \item \textbf{Interactions}: quality of interpersonal exchanges between teacher
  and pupils (1~=~cold or dismissive; 5~=~warm and encouraging).
\end{itemize}

\textit{Strictness-relevant items.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Misbehaviour} (reverse scored): level of observed pupil
  misbehaviour (1~=~persistent and widespread; 5~=~none observed).
  \item \textbf{Disruption} (reverse scored): degree to which pupil conduct
  disrupted the flow of the lesson (1~=~severely disruptive; 5~=~fully orderly).
  \item \textbf{Response}: the teacher's response when misbehaviour or disruption
  occurred, rated on effectiveness and consistency (1~=~no response or
  ineffective; 5~=~prompt and consistent).
  \item \textbf{Respectfully}: degree to which pupils behaved respectfully
  towards the teacher (1~=~openly disrespectful; 5~=~consistently respectful).
\end{itemize}

A special case arises for the Response item when no misbehaviour or disruption
was observed during a lesson (i.e., the raw score was zero, indicating that the
observer had no occasion to rate a teacher response). A score of zero cannot
straightforwardly be treated as missing: in a calm lesson, the absence of any
sanctioning event reflects well on the school's culture, while in a disruptive
lesson without any teacher response it reflects poorly. The Response score is
therefore imputed from the Disruption score according to:
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:response_impute}
  \text{Response} = 6 - \text{Disruption}
\end{equation}
%
such that an orderly lesson (Disruption~=~1) receives an imputed Response of~5,
and a disruptive lesson in which no response was forthcoming (Disruption~=~5)
receives an imputed Response of~1. Where the Disruption score is itself missing,
the Response item is excluded from the sub-score average.

\textit{Teaching practice items} (all scored 1--5): Questioning; Verbal
feedback; Discussion; Differentiation; Explanation; Outcomes; Methods;
Structure; Resources. The Discussion item is an exception to the general
coding convention: a score of zero (no pupil discussion) is treated as a genuine
negative rather than missing, reflecting the judgement that the complete absence
of discussion during a lesson is itself an informative observation about teaching
quality.

\textbf{Sub-score~W1} (teaching warmth) is the unweighted mean of Names,
Praise, and Interactions. \textbf{Sub-score~S1} (in-lesson strictness) is the
unweighted mean of Misbehaviour (reverse scored), Disruption (reverse scored),
Response (with imputation as described above), and Respectfully.
\textbf{Sub-score~T1} (observed teaching practice) is the unweighted mean of
the nine teaching items.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsection{Outside-of-Lesson Observations}
\label{sec:p1_obs_out}

A standardised coding schedule was also applied during observation of
outside-of-lesson environments, including corridor transitions, the canteen,
arrival, and recreational spaces. Ratings were made on eight items.

\textit{Strictness-relevant items} (all scored 1--5):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sanctions}: whether sanctions were observed being applied in
  out-of-class settings, and with what consistency.
  \item \textbf{Corridors}: orderliness of pupil movement in corridors.
  \item \textbf{Arrival}: orderliness and organisation of pupil arrival at the
  start of the school day.
  \item \textbf{Canteen}: conduct and atmosphere in the dining area.
  \item \textbf{Recreational}: conduct and atmosphere in recreational areas during
  break and lunch.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Warmth-relevant items} (all scored 1--5):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Transitions}: quality of student conduct and relationships
  observed during movement between lessons.
  \item \textbf{Interactions (break)}: quality of observed interactions between
  staff and students during unstructured time.
  \item \textbf{Relationships (break)}: overall quality of the relational
  atmosphere during break time.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Sub-score~S2} (out-of-lesson strictness) is the unweighted mean of
Sanctions, Corridors, Arrival, Canteen, and Recreational. \textbf{Sub-score~W2}
(school warmth) is the unweighted mean of Transitions, Interactions (break), and
Relationships (break).

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsection{Headteacher Interview}
\label{sec:p1_interview}

A semi-structured interview of approximately 60 minutes was conducted with the
headteacher at each school. The interview schedule comprised 11 substantive
questions (Q2--Q12) and 10 Likert-scale agreement statements (S1--S10). The full
interview guide is reproduced in \cref{app:2A:interview_guide}. Responses to
substantive questions were scored on a 1--5 scale by two researchers present
during the interview (the questioner and an observer) working independently;
where the two scores differed, a final reconciled score was agreed. Likert
statements were completed by the headteacher directly.

A subset of interview responses contributes to the warmth and strictness scores.

The espoused scores are built from the statement battery alone: nine Likert
items on which the headteacher rated their own school from 1 to 5.\footnote{
Throughout this chapter the suffix ``-stmt'' denotes one of these statement
items. They are distinct from the observation-derived sub-scores S1 and S2
defined in \cref{sec:p1_obs_class,sec:p1_obs_out}.}

\textit{Warmth statements}: S2-stmt, ``Reward and recognition systems are
applied consistently across the school''; S7-stmt, ``Pupils in this school
feel safe''; S8-stmt, ``Pupils in this school feel that their teachers care
about them''.

\textit{Strictness statements}: S1-stmt, ``Sanction systems are applied
consistently across the school''; S5-stmt, ``Pupil behaviour in lessons is
generally good''; S6-stmt, ``Pupil behaviour outside lessons is generally
good''; S9-stmt, ``Teachers feel respected by pupils in this school''.

\textit{Staff climate statements}: S3-stmt, ``Teacher turnover at this school
is low''; S4-stmt, ``Staff morale at this school is high''.

\textbf{Sub-score~W3} (espoused warmth) is the unweighted mean of the three
warmth statements. \textbf{Sub-score~S4} (espoused strictness) is the
unweighted mean of the four strictness statements. \textbf{Sub-score~SC}
(espoused staff climate) is the mean of the two staff climate statements. It
should be stressed that SC is a measure of staff climate and nothing more:
the interview contains no espoused measure of teaching practice, and none is
constructed. The reason for this, and for two further exclusions, is set out
below, because the exclusions were not part of the original design. They were
forced by evidence, and what forced them is informative.

\textbf{What was excluded, and why.} The interview schedule also produced two
other kinds of material that earlier versions of this instrument folded into
the espoused scores, and both were removed when their properties were
measured. The first was a set of question-level ratings: after each open
question (the headteacher's approach to wellbeing, to sanctions, to marking,
and so on) the response was scored from 1 to 5 against a rubric whose top
band required a ``detailed response with a clear explanation of the
rationale''. Four measurements showed that these ratings measure how well
the headteacher explains themselves rather than what the school does. They
barely discriminate between constructs (the average correlation between
ratings of the same construct is $+0.31$, and between ratings of different
constructs $+0.29$); they collapse onto a single general factor; that factor
tracks the observer's own ratings of the headteacher's confidence and
willingness at $+0.34$ to $+0.47$, where the statement items track them at
roughly zero; and against the visit scores every one of them is null.
Averaging them into the espoused scores was therefore injecting a measure of
articulacy into a measure of culture. Under the original construction about
half of the variance in espoused warmth was articulacy variance; under the
construction used here it is essentially none. The ratings survive in the
dataset as a variable in their own right, \texttt{ht\_articulacy}, and
\cref{ch:paper2} enters it as a control, where it moves nothing --- how
articulate a headteacher is predicts neither their school's observed culture
nor its results, which is reassuring for everything else the interview is
used for.

The second exclusion was a count of structural behaviour systems. Binary
questions recorded whether the school operates eight such systems:
centralised detentions, an internal exclusion room, demerits, line-ups,
silent corridors, after-school revision, weekend revision, and a mobile
phone ban. Earlier versions averaged this count into espoused strictness
with equal weight. The count turns out to carry almost no information about
observed strictness: on the schools where both are available it correlates
with the visit strictness score at $+0.08$, against $+0.41$ for the
statement battery it was being averaged with, so including it was diluting
the better measure. The count is retained in the dataset as a descriptive
variable. The contrast between the two figures is worth a sentence in its
own right, because it recurs throughout this thesis: what systems a school
\emph{names} is weakly informative; whether the headteacher reports that
its systems are applied \emph{consistently} is what tracks observed
practice. Within the statement battery itself the same pattern holds. The
consistency-of-practice items predict observed strictness ($+0.39$ for
sanctions applied consistently, $+0.29$ for rewards) while the outcome and
feeling items do not ($+0.16$ for pupils feel safe, $+0.11$ for teachers
care, neither significant). Headteachers report reliably on whether their
systems are applied, and unreliably on how people feel.

Three meta-ratings were also recorded after each interview --- the
researcher's confidence in the responses, the headteacher's willingness to
answer fully, and their patience with the interview's duration. An earlier
version of this instrument used their mean to discount espoused warmth for
reticent interviewees. That adjustment has been removed. It scaled a content
score by the quality of the headteacher's self-presentation, so the score
and the adjustment shared rater variance by construction, and the apparent
improvement in validity it produced was that shared variance rather than a
genuine gain. The meta-ratings are retained as data and contribute to
\texttt{ht\_articulacy}.

\textbf{An external validation.} One result gives particular confidence that
the rebuilt espoused scores measure what headteachers know rather than what
they wish. The staff climate sub-score, built from two statements about
turnover and morale, can be checked against the Department for Education's
own workforce records. Across the interviewed schools it correlates with the
school's actual recorded teacher turnover at $-0.52$ ($p < 0.001$), and the
association is essentially unchanged ($-0.48$) when the full set of school
controls is added. For comparison, \texttt{ht\_articulacy} predicts recorded
turnover at $+0.05$. When a headteacher says their turnover is low, it is
low. This is the strongest validation in this thesis of any self-reported
measure against an independent administrative record, and it comes from
outside the project's own instruments entirely.

\subsection{Score Construction: Enacted and Espoused}
\label{sec:p1_composite}

The nine sub-scores are combined into two parallel score sets, one for each
source. The \emph{enacted} scores are built from the observational evidence
alone; the \emph{espoused} scores are built from the headteacher interview
alone:
%
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:enacted}
  \big(W^{\text{enac}},\; S^{\text{enac}},\; T^{\text{enac}}\big)
    &= \big(\tfrac{1}{2}(W1 + W2),\;\; \tfrac{1}{2}(S1 + S2),\;\; T1\big)
    \\[4pt]
  \label{eq:espoused}
  \big(W^{\text{esp}},\; S^{\text{esp}}\big)
    &= \big(W3,\;\; S4\big)
\end{align}
%
Both sets are computed on a $[0, 5]$ scale and rescaled to $[0, 10]$ by
multiplying by two. Every school that received a visit has an enacted score;
every school that gave an interview has an espoused score; \NTierOne{} schools
have both.

The two sets are reported separately and are not combined. Earlier versions of
this instrument defined a single gold standard score as a fixed
60/40 weighted average of the observational and interview components, on the
reasoning that direct observation is the more objective source while the
interview captures intentions and systems that a single visit cannot see. That
specification has been withdrawn. Its premise is that the two sources are noisy
readings of one latent quantity, so that averaging them cancels error; the
convergence evidence in \cref{sec:p1_convergence} shows they are not. On the
schools where both are observed the two agree at $r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ for
warmth and $r = \GoldStrictnessSplit$ for strictness. (Teaching has no
espoused--enacted split to report: once the explanation-quality question scores
were removed from the interview instrument, its remaining ``teaching'' items
measured staff turnover and morale---a staff-climate construct, not teaching
practice---so the interview supplies no espoused teaching measure at all.)
A weighted average of two measures that share that little variance
does not estimate the quantity either one estimates: it yields enacted culture
contaminated by self-report, or espoused culture contaminated by observation,
according to the weight chosen. The weight has no defensible value because there
is no single target for it to be right about.

Keeping the two apart also makes the second source do analytic work rather than
absorb it. The gap between what a headteacher claims and what an observer
records is itself a finding---it is systematic, dimension-specific, and reported
below---and averaging is precisely the operation that destroys it.\footnote{Both
espoused scores now rest on the statement battery alone, and two earlier
ingredients were removed deliberately. An earlier espoused strictness score
averaged in S3, an objective count of structural behaviour-management systems;
the count was dropped because it carries almost no information about observed
strictness ($r = +0.084$ against the visit score, versus $+0.413$ for the
self-report battery it was diluting)---what systems a school \emph{names} is
not how strictly it runs, and equal-weight averaging with the noisier count was
halving the construct. An articulacy adjustment~$\omega$, which discounted
espoused warmth when the head was unresponsive, was likewise removed: it scaled
a content score by how well the head explained themselves, so score and
explanation quality shared rater variance by construction. Its removal is
validated directly: how articulate a head is predicts neither the school's
observed culture nor its outcomes, and where the old espoused warmth score was
roughly half articulacy variance, the rebuilt score is essentially none.}

\textit{Interview and national tier scores.} For Tier~2 and Tier~3 schools, the
sub-scores derived above serve as input features to Ridge regression calibration
models. The interview-tier model takes the sub-scores $W3$ and $S4$ and the
systems count $S3$ as predictors (the count forms no part of any score, but a
prediction model is free to use it), alongside the text-derived
features described in \cref{sec:p1_text}. Its target is the \emph{enacted}
score, not a blend: the models are trained to predict what an independent
observer recorded, using what the school and the public record say about
itself. Espoused scores need no calibration model, since a school with an
interview already has one directly. The national-tier model takes the
text-derived features alone as predictors and is applied to all schools in the
national sample. Both models are described fully in \cref{sec:p1_enriched} and
\cref{sec:p1_text} respectively.

score sets for the \NTierOne{} full-data schools.
\cref{fig:warmth_strict_joint} plots the joint distribution of enacted warmth
and strictness for these schools.

\subsection{Convergence between visit and interview scores}
\label{sec:p1_convergence}

The \NTierOne{} full-data schools carry both score sets, which permits a direct
test of whether the two sources measure the same thing. If the enacted and
espoused scores are noisy readings of one latent construct, they should
correlate highly; if they capture substantively distinct facets of school
culture, they will not. This is the test on which the decision to report them
separately rests.

The results indicate substantial divergence. Enacted and espoused warmth
correlate at $r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ ($p = \GoldWarmthSplitP$, $n = \NTierOne$);
strictness at $r = \GoldStrictnessSplit$ ($p = \GoldStrictnessSplitP$).
(Teaching cannot join this comparison: the interview has no espoused teaching
measure---see \cref{sec:p1_composite}.) Both correlations are positive
and in the expected direction, but neither approaches the level that would
justify treating the two sources as alternative measures of a single construct.
Squared, they imply shared variance below a fifth on either dimension: most of
what a visit records is invisible to the interview, and conversely. The divergence is directionally asymmetric:
headteachers rate their schools on average $0.51$ points higher on warmth than
independent observers record (both on the $[0,10]$ scale; paired
$p < 0.001$), while the corresponding strictness gap is near zero and not
distinguishable from it ($+0.07$ points, $p = 0.53$). Warmth discrepancies
exceeding one point in magnitude occur in 55\% of schools; discrepancies
exceeding two points in 21\%.

These findings carry two implications. First, the low cross-source correlations
establish that the visit and the interview are not two readings of one
construct. The interview reflects the headteacher's \textit{espoused}
culture---their stated philosophy, commitments, and self-assessment---while the
visit records the \textit{enacted} culture as experienced by an independent
observer. This is what rules out blending them. A weighted average is the right
operation for two fallible measures of a common target, because the errors are
independent and partially cancel; it is the wrong operation for two measures of
different targets, because it produces a third quantity that is neither, whose
composition depends on an arbitrary weight and whose interpretation changes with
it. The evidence that once justified combining the sources---that each contains
something the other lacks---is in fact the evidence against it. What a source
uniquely contains cannot be recovered by averaging it away.

The practical cost of this decision is small and the gain is specific. Neither
source is discarded: both are carried forward, and the analyses in
\cref{ch:paper2} report them side by side. What is given up is the convenience
of a single headline number per school. What is bought is that every estimate
in this thesis can be attributed to a known source, so that a result holding for
enacted culture but not espoused culture---or the reverse---is visible as a
finding rather than absorbed into a weighting.

Second, the directional asymmetry is interpretively informative.
The over-claim is concentrated in warmth: headteachers rate their schools
$+0.51$ points above what observers record, while strictness self-report
is essentially unbiased ($+0.07$ points). This asymmetry is consistent with
the different character of the two dimensions. Strictness is partly captured
by an objective systems tally ($S3$: count of structural behaviour management
systems in place), which leaves little scope for motivated misrepresentation;
headteachers either have centralised detentions or they do not. Warmth, by
contrast, is assessed entirely through expressed relational philosophy and
Likert agreement, where social desirability pressures are strongest. This has a
direct consequence for the calibration models, whose predictors are drawn from
the espoused side and whose target is the enacted score. For warmth the
predictors carry a construct the target does not contain, and a bias in the
predictors that the target does not share; for strictness self-report and
observation are aligned in level even where they are weakly correlated in
ranking. The expected difficulty is therefore concentrated in warmth, and
\cref{sec:p1_enriched_model} reports exactly that pattern.

\section{Inter-rater Reliability}
\label{sec:p1_irr}

Inter-rater reliability was assessed separately for the headteacher interview
data and the school visit observation data, using data from sessions where two
researchers were present simultaneously.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsection{Interview}
\label{sec:p1_irr_int}

Each interview was attended by a questioner and an observer, who scored
Q2--Q12 independently on a 1--5 scale during the session. Inter-rater reliability
is assessed by comparing questioner and observer scores across the full sample of
interviews with paired ratings.

\cref{tab:irr_interview} in \cref{app:2A:irr} reports, for each of Q2--Q12, the
number of schools with paired ratings, Pearson~$r$, mean absolute difference, the
proportion of pairs agreeing exactly, the proportion agreeing within one scale
point, and weighted Cohen's~$\kappa$.

Overall, Pearson correlations ranged from $r = 0.61$ (Q6: excellent teaching) to
$r = 0.74$ (Q12: assessing pupil progress), with a median of $r = 0.70$ across
Q2--Q12. Agreement within one scale point exceeded 90\% for all questions, with
five questions exceeding 94\%, indicating substantial consistency across raters.
The items with the weakest inter-rater agreement were Q6 (what excellent teaching
looks like; $r = 0.61$) and Q7 (co-curricular offer; $r = 0.62$), consistent
with the greater interpretive latitude and the greater variability in headteacher
responses these questions permit. Items eliciting more structured responses---Q12
(pupil progress monitoring; $r = 0.74$) and Q5 (approach to rewards and
sanctions; $r = 0.73$)---showed the strongest agreement, reflecting that concrete
policy descriptions are more reliably scored than open-ended reflections on
teaching philosophy. Weighted Cohen's $\kappa$ ranged from 0.42 to 0.61
(median~0.53), broadly consistent with the ``moderate to substantial'' range
in the \citet{landis1977} classification. These results provide reassurance that
the interview-derived sub-scores are not primarily an artefact of idiosyncratic
researcher interpretation.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsection{School Visit Observations}
\label{sec:p1_irr_obs}

School visits were conducted by teams of two or three researchers who observed
the same lessons and outside-of-lesson environments simultaneously and
independently. For schools observed by three researchers, three pairwise
comparisons are available.

\cref{tab:irr_classroom} and \cref{tab:irr_outside} in \cref{app:2A:irr}
report pairwise inter-rater reliability statistics for classroom observation
items and outside-of-lesson items respectively, including pairwise Pearson~$r$,
exact agreement, agreement within one scale point, and mean absolute difference.

For classroom observations, the items with the strongest inter-rater reliability
were Disruption ($r = 0.79$) and Discussion ($r = 0.72$), which can be assessed
from relatively focal observational events: a lesson is either disrupted or not,
and a class is either engaged in discussion or not. The weakest agreement was
observed for Resource ($r = 0.53$) and Methods ($r = 0.55$), which require
integrating multiple simultaneous pedagogical behaviours across the lesson. Across
all 20 classroom items, within-one-point agreement ranged from 84\% to 94\%.

For outside-of-lesson observations, the strongest items were Reward ($r = 0.81$)
and Relationships ($r = 0.72$), while the weakest were Arrival ($r = 0.47$) and
Verbal instruction ($r = 0.49$)---items that require synthesising multiple
brief interactions across an extended observation window. Within-one-point
agreement ranged from 85\% to 100\% (Students: 100\%) across the 13 outside
items. These findings are consistent with expectations: items requiring a global
assessment of a discrete event are more amenable to consistent coding than items
requiring the synthesis of many small interactions observed over an extended
period.

\section{Interview-Tier Calibration Model}
\label{sec:p1_enriched}

Warmth and strictness scores for the \NInterviewed{} interview schools are generated by a
Ridge regression calibration model trained on the \RidgeTrainN{} full-data
schools with complete predictor data, in
which the \emph{enacted} scores are the outcome variables and
interview-derived sub-scores together with text-derived features from publicly
available documents are the predictors. This section describes the construction
of the predictor set, the specification of the model, and its leave-one-out
cross-validated performance.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsection{Interview Predictor Features}
\label{sec:p1_enriched_features}

The core interview predictors are the sub-scores derived in
\cref{sec:p1_interview}: $W3$ (espoused warmth), $S4$ (espoused strictness),
and $S3$ (the systems count). The count forms no part of any espoused score,
for the reasons given in \cref{sec:p1_interview}, but a prediction model is
free to use whatever carries signal, so it is retained as a feature here.
These three features are used in the \textit{main} specification.

In addition to the scored interview responses, thematic codings of interview
responses were made by the researcher who conducted each interview. The key
phrases coding sheet records, for each of the 11 substantive questions, which of
a pre-specified set of themes was mentioned by the headteacher. These codings
comprise 71 binary (Yes\,/\,No\,/\,Vague) indicators across Q2--Q12, covering
warmth-related language (relational framing in Q2, Q4, and Q6), strictness-related
language (escalating sanction frameworks and ``sweating the small stuff'' in Q5;
strictness language in Q2, Q4, and Q6), and teaching practice themes. Indicators
are averaged within dimension to give a warmth keyword score, a strictness keyword
score, and a teaching keyword score, each rescaled to a $[0, 5]$ range. In the
\textit{enriched} specification, these three keyword scores are included as
additional predictors alongside the core interview features.

Key phrase codings are absent for 9 of the \NInterviewed{} interviewed schools
(3.0\%).\footnote{The nine schools with no key phrase codings are: ARK
Alexandra; E-ACT Crest Academy; Edgbarrow School; Guru Nanak Sikh Academy;
Isaac Newton Academy; Oasis Academy Hadley; Saint Martin's Catholic Voluntary
Academy; St John Fisher Catholic Voluntary Academy; and Stoke Newington School.}
A further two schools have partial codings. Missingness appears to be unrelated
to observable school characteristics; none of the nine schools is in the
full-data tier. The main specification covers all \NInterviewed{} interviewed schools; the
enriched specification is restricted to the 294 schools with complete keyword
codings.

Text-derived features from Ofsted inspection reports and behaviour policy
documents are included as predictors in both specifications, as all interview
schools also have publicly available documents. The text features are the
holistic LLM scores described in \cref{sec:p1_text_method}: the Ofsted LLM score
and the behaviour policy LLM score for the target dimension (warmth or strictness),
giving two text features per dimension. The full predictor set for the main
interview-tier model therefore comprises the three core interview
features---$W3$, $S3$, and $S4$---plus these two text
features, for five predictors in total. The enriched model additionally includes
keyword scores.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsection{Calibration Model and Validation}
\label{sec:p1_enriched_model}

The interview-tier calibration model is estimated separately for warmth and
strictness. For each dimension, a Ridge regression is fitted on the 100
full-data schools with a complete predictor vector:
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ridge_interview}
  \hat{y}_s = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}' \mathbf{f}_s
\end{equation}
%
where $\hat{y}_s$ is the predicted gold standard score for school~$s$ and
$\mathbf{f}_s$ is the vector of predictor features. Ridge regularisation is
well-suited to the modest sample-to-predictor ratio available here, and the
leave-one-out cross-validation procedure provides an empirical rather than
asymptotic assessment of out-of-sample predictive accuracy. The Ridge penalty
$\lambda$ is selected by leave-one-out cross-validation on the 100 training
schools. Predicted scores are clipped to the $[0, 10]$ range.

The target is the \emph{enacted} score. This matters more than any other
specification choice in the model, so it is worth stating what it rules out.
Every predictor is something the school or the public record says about
itself---the headteacher's own account, an inspection report, a published
behaviour policy---and the target is what an independent observer recorded on a
visit. No quantity appears on both sides. Whatever predictive accuracy the model
achieves is therefore accuracy about observed practice, and cannot be the model
recovering an input it was handed.

The training sample is \RidgeTrainN{} rather than the full \NTierOne{} Tier~1
schools because both calibration models take Ofsted inspection text as a
predictor, and two Tier~1 schools have no scored inspection report. They are
dropped by listwise deletion on the predictor vector rather than imputed, since
with two features per dimension drawn from that source there is nothing left to
impute from. The same \RidgeTrainN{} schools train the national-tier model in
\cref{sec:p1_text_method}, so the two models are estimated and cross-validated
on an identical sample and their performance is directly comparable.

Leave-one-out cross-validated performance is reported in
\cref{tab:interview_model_loo}. Model~A recovers enacted strictness at
$r = \RidgeAStrictness$ ($R^2 = 0.115$, $\text{RMSE} = 0.796$) and enacted
warmth at $r = \RidgeAWarmth$ ($R^2 = 0.087$, $\text{RMSE} = 0.915$). These are
modest figures, and they are the honest ones.

They are also markedly lower than the figures reported in earlier versions of
this model, which is worth explaining rather than passing over. That model was
trained against the 60/40 composite and achieved LOO-CV $r = 0.767$ for warmth
and $r = 0.701$ for strictness. Those numbers were inflated by construction.
The interview sub-scores as then constructed --- the quality-adjusted warmth
score and a strictness average that included the systems count --- entered as
predictors while also constituting 40~per~cent of the target, so a substantial
share of the apparent skill was the model recovering what it had been given.
The scale of the problem is visible in the sub-scores taken alone: the warmth
sub-score correlated at $r = 0.758$ with composite warmth but far more weakly
with the observed component,
and the strictness average at $r = 0.678$ with composite strictness against
$r = \GoldStrictnessSplit$ observed. Withdrawing the composite removes that
circularity at its source rather than measuring and deducting it. The fall from
$0.767$ to $\RidgeAWarmth$ is not a deterioration in the model; it is the
disappearance of a quantity that was never predictive skill.

The dimensional asymmetry that runs through this chapter appears here too, and
in its sharpest form. Strictness is predicted at $\RidgeAStrictness$; warmth at
$\RidgeAWarmth$, an $R^2$ under nine per cent. Five features drawn from what a
school says about itself account for less than a tenth of the variance in the
warmth an observer records. Given the convergence results in
\cref{sec:p1_convergence}---espoused and enacted warmth correlating at
$\GoldWarmthSplit$, with a systematic upward bias in self-report on top---this
is the expected result rather than a surprising one. It sets a real limit on
what the extended tier can claim, and that limit is carried explicitly into
\cref{ch:paper2}.

\input{tables/tab_interview_model_loo}

The main specification is treated as the primary throughout the thesis. Fitted
coefficients are reported in \cref{tab:interview_model_coefs} in
\cref{app:2A:ridge_spec}, and against the enacted target they read as the
convergence results would predict. For strictness the pattern is
interpretable: espoused strictness $S4$ is the largest standardised predictor
($+0.243$), with the Ofsted strictness score close behind
($+0.231$). The headteacher's structured self-report and an externally
authored account of the school do roughly equal work, which is what
\cref{sec:p1_convergence} would lead one to
expect. Notably $S3$, the objective tally of behaviour management systems,
contributes essentially nothing ($-0.030$): having centralised detentions does
not distinguish schools on observed strictness, presumably because most schools
have them.

For warmth the pattern is weaker throughout, as the headline LOO figure
requires. The largest predictor of observed
warmth is again $S4$, the headteacher's \emph{strictness} report ($+0.160$),
while $W3$---their espoused warmth, the feature built for this
purpose---carries little ($+0.088$), and the two text-derived warmth scores
carry nothing at all. Against what an observer records,
a head's stated warmth carries far less information than their stated
strictness, and the model's
weak warmth performance is attributable to precisely this. The coefficient on
$S4$ should not be over-read in the other direction either; at $R^2 = 0.087$ the
warmth model is close enough to uninformative that its internal ordering is not
worth interpreting substantively.

\section{Text-Based Scoring for the National Sample}
\label{sec:p1_text}

For the 3,029 open state secondary schools not covered
by the visit or interview programme, warmth and strictness scores are derived
from two publicly available text sources: Ofsted inspection reports and school
behaviour policy documents.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsection{Document Collection}
\label{sec:p1_text_docs}

Ofsted inspection reports were obtained from the Ofsted website and the Ofsted
Report Card (for inspections published after 2024). The most recent available
full inspection report was used for each school; monitoring visits, short
inspections, and academy conversion assessments were excluded. Reports were
obtained for 3,288 of the 3,312 currently open schools in the national sample
(99.3\%); the 24 unmatched schools had all opened after 2023 and had not yet
been inspected at the time of data collection. Inspection dates range from 2012
to 2026, with the large majority concentrated in 2022--2024 (873 schools in
2024, 910 in 2023, 695 in 2022), reflecting the resumption of routine
inspections after the Covid-19 pandemic suspension. The median lag between the
most recent inspection and the analysis year (2023--24 outcomes) is
approximately one year.

Behaviour policy documents were collected by a systematic crawl of school
websites. The crawl retrieved candidate documents and applied a filtering step
to identify those meeting the criteria for a behaviour policy, as distinct from
anti-bullying policies, safeguarding documents, or other school
policies.\footnote{Full details of the crawl methodology, including the
URL-discovery approach and the document-filtering criteria, are provided in
\cref{app:2A:crawl}.} A behaviour policy was successfully retrieved for
3,365 schools (98.2\% of the national sample), with one school permanently
unresolvable (no publicly available policy) and 117 schools written off as
outside scope (closed, studio, or UTC schools that were excluded from the spine
after download). Schools for which
either source is unavailable---no full Ofsted inspection report on record, or no
behaviour policy retrieved---are excluded from the public-data analysis. A
representativeness check comparing excluded and included schools on key observable
characteristics is reported in \cref{app:2A:sample}.

A systematic quality audit of the retrieved documents identified two categories
of concern. First, a byte-for-byte comparison of document files revealed that
351 schools (10.4\%) share an identical document with at least one other school
in the national sample. In every identifiable case this reflects a
Multi-Academy Trust or federation-level behaviour policy template distributed
uniformly across member schools: the largest groups comprise 24 schools sharing
a single document (Star Academies and associated trusts), 27 schools (Outwood
Academy, across two overlapping groups), and nine schools (Academy
Transformation Trust). These schools are retained in the analysis with
identical document-derived scores. Second, a name-match check---verifying that
significant words from the school's registered name appear in the opening
section of each retrieved document---flagged a subset of documents as
potentially belonging to a different institution. A targeted manual review of
the highest-risk cases found no confirmed mis-matches among the schools not
already identified by the duplicate-document check. Both audit outputs are
recorded in \texttt{behaviour\_policy\_doc\_audit.csv}.

\subsection{Scoring Methodology}
\label{sec:p1_text_method}

Warmth and strictness scores are derived from the two document types via a
two-step pipeline comprising an evidence-extraction step followed by LLM
scoring. For Ofsted reports the extraction step is programmatic; for behaviour
policy documents it is itself an LLM call. The approaches differ accordingly,
reflecting the different structures and purposes of the two source types.

\textit{Ofsted inspection reports.} For each report, the full narrative body of
the section reporting ``What is it like to attend this school?'' and, where
present, the section reporting ``What does the school do well?'' is extracted
programmatically and submitted to \texttt{gpt-5-nano} together with the Ofsted behaviour
and attitudes sub-judgement (if recorded).\footnote{For all-through schools
(\textit{n}\,=\,170), Ofsted reports were pre-filtered to remove paragraphs
containing only primary-stage terminology (e.g.\ Years~1--6, KS1/2, phonics,
EYFS) with no secondary signal (e.g.\ Years~7--13, KS3/4, GCSE) before
narrative extraction. Where paragraphs were removed, the model was additionally
instructed to score secondary provision only. Validation on a random sample of
20 all-through schools showed minimal impact of the filter (mean absolute score
change: warmth 0.35 points, strictness 0.20 points), suggesting that Ofsted
inspectors already focus their behaviour commentary on the secondary phase in
these reports. The filter is retained for methodological transparency.} The
extracted narrative is typically 4,000--6,000 characters in length and covers the
inspector's most characterising language about the school's relational culture and
behavioural environment. Warmth, strictness, and teaching quality are scored in a
single unified LLM call using holistic rubrics validated empirically against the
gold standard. Only warmth and strictness are carried forward. Teaching quality
is not part of the two-dimensional framework this chapter constructs, and the
validation evidence gives no reason to extend it. Against teaching as observed
in lessons the Ofsted teaching score reaches only
$r = \OfstedTeachingEnacted$ ($p = 0.085$). Nor can it be triangulated against
a self-report side the way warmth and strictness are, because the interview
supplies no espoused teaching measure at all (\cref{sec:p1_composite}). Its
nearest neighbour in the interview---a staff-climate scale built from the
turnover and morale statements---is a different construct, and the correlation
the Ofsted teaching score reaches against it
($r = \OfstedTeachingVsStaffClimate$) is a cross-construct association that
cannot stand in for convergent validity.\footnote{An earlier draft quoted that
association as validation against ``espoused teaching''. The relabelling
matters: once the explanation-quality question scores were removed from the
interview instrument, the items remaining under its teaching heading measured
staff turnover and morale, and a correlation with them validates nothing about
a teaching scorer.} In the gold standard itself, espoused and enacted scores
correlate at $r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ ($p = \GoldWarmthSplitP$) for warmth and
$r = \GoldStrictnessSplit$ ($p = \GoldStrictnessSplitP$) for strictness,
computed on all \NTierOne{} full-data schools since neither score depends on an
inspection report. What a headteacher says about a dimension and what a visit
records of it are related on both dimensions, but weakly---which
is why the two are reported as separate quantities rather than averaged into one.
Warmth is retained despite its own weak text validity because it is one of
the two constructs under study and is triangulated against interview and visit
measures throughout; a national teaching estimate would have no such
scaffolding, and would carry a label inviting exactly the reading the evidence
does not support.

No correlation threshold decides this. At $n = \RidgeTrainN$ the sampling
interval around a correlation of this size is wide, so a bright line drawn
anywhere in the plausible range
would sort sampling noise rather than validity. The interview-transcript
teaching score is weaker still ($r = 0.067$, $p = 0.52$, $n = 96$) and is
likewise not used; it was produced by an earlier scoring pass that truncated
long transcripts, so its weakness is not on its own evidence about what
interview transcripts can support.

Each dimension receives a single holistic score on a $1$--$5$ scale, accompanied
by a confidence rating and a brief reason that is required to state why the score
is not one point higher or lower.\footnote{Confidence ratings (high, medium, or
low) are recorded for each document but play no role in the Ridge calibration
models. They are better understood as indicators of document quality than of
school quality: a low confidence rating reflects thin, vague, or contradictory
evidence in the text rather than anything about the school itself.} The risk that
a score is driven by a single salient passage is addressed by grounding: the
model is supplied with the evidence passages selected from the report and its
stated reason must rest on them, rather than on a free reading of the document as
a whole. The full prompts are reproduced in \cref{app:2A:prompts}.

An earlier version of the instrument scored four subcomponents of each dimension
before aggregating to a holistic score---for strictness, clarity of expectations,
consistency of enforcement, behavioural outcomes, and response to misbehaviour;
for warmth, staff--pupil relationships, safety and belonging, pastoral support,
and relational climate. It was withdrawn, because on the volume of narrative an
inspection report supplies the four facets did not behave as distinct
measurements. Across the Tier~1 schools, 77~per~cent received an identical score
on all four warmth facets and 35~per~cent on all four strictness facets; the
median intercorrelation among the facets was $0.73$ for warmth and $0.57$ for
strictness; and in each dimension the strongest facet correlated with the
holistic score at $r = 0.95$. What looked like four judgements was one judgement
recorded four times. Inspection reports are too short, and too conventional in
what they choose to record, to evidence four facets of a construct
independently, and the disaggregation is not attempted.

\textit{Behaviour policy documents.} Each selected document is processed via a
two-stage LLM pipeline. In the first stage, an extraction call identifies and
removes content relating to special educational needs, disability, mental health,
safeguarding, therapeutic interventions, anti-bullying, uniform, attendance, and
external agencies---language that reflects statutory obligations or specialist
provision rather than the school's general relational culture. For all-through
schools, this extraction step additionally isolates text pertaining to
secondary-phase provision, applying the same phase-filtering logic used for
Ofsted reports. Where the extracted excerpt falls below 100 words the full
document text is used as a fallback. In the second stage, the extracted excerpt
is submitted to \texttt{gpt-5-nano} for
scoring using an anchored rubric calibrated iteratively on eight schools with
known warmth and strictness characteristics. Anchoring---embedding representative
policy excerpts at key score levels---was adopted because initial calibration
without anchoring produced severely compressed distributions, with over 60\% of
policies assigned a single score value. The rubric treats strictness score~3 as
the explicit default for a policy that describes adequate expectations without
distinguishing features. The principal discriminating criteria are: score~4
requires a named tracking or monitoring system in which a pupil's position
automatically determines the next consequence, eliminating staff discretion at
the point of sanction; score~5 additionally requires that sanctions are issued
mandatorily (no discretionary exemptions) together with explicit deportment norms
such as posture, SLANT protocols, or eye-contact requirements. For warmth,
score~4 requires both a named reward system \textit{and} explicit operational
guidance to staff on building or maintaining individual relationships with pupils
(both criteria are necessary); score~3 is the default for policies expressing a
caring ethos without specifying relational mechanisms. The model returns a final
warmth score and a final strictness score with brief rationale. The full prompt
is reproduced in \cref{app:2A:prompts}.

For both document types, the holistic LLM score enters the Ridge calibration
models as a single text feature per dimension: one Ofsted score and one
behaviour-policy score, giving two text features per dimension in total.

\subsection{National-Tier Calibration Model and Validation}
\label{sec:p1_text_valid}

The LLM-derived holistic scores are not used directly as warmth and
strictness estimates; instead they serve as input features to a Ridge
regression calibration model trained on the same \RidgeTrainN{} full-data
schools, following
the same approach as the interview-tier model (\cref{sec:p1_enriched_model}),
and against the same enacted target.
The national-tier model is estimated separately for warmth and strictness:
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ridge_national}
  \hat{y}_s = \hat{\alpha} + \beta_O x_{O,s} + \beta_P x_{P,s}
\end{equation}
%
where $x_{O,s}$ is the Ofsted LLM score and $x_{P,s}$ is the behaviour-policy
LLM score for school~$s$, both for the relevant dimension. The Ridge penalty
$\lambda$ is selected by leave-one-out cross-validation on the same
\RidgeTrainN{} full-data schools.

Leave-one-out cross-validated performance is reported in
\cref{tab:national_model_loo}. The two dimensions separate completely. The
national-tier strictness model achieves a LOO-CV $r = \RidgeBStrictness$
($R^2 = 0.114$, $\text{RMSE} = 0.796$)---within sampling error of what Model~A
achieves with four additional interview features. The national-tier warmth model
achieves LOO-CV $r = \RidgeBWarmth$: a negative cross-validated correlation, which
is to say the model is worse than predicting the sample mean for every school.
Two publicly available text sources contain no recoverable information about the
warmth an observer records.

The warmth result is not a failure of calibration that a better model might
repair, because the raw ingredient is absent: in every version of the
instrument the Ofsted warmth score is null against the warmth an observer
records, and such signal as it carries attaches to the espoused side and to
enacted \emph{strictness} rather than to its own construct. Strictness behaves
quite differently: the Ofsted strictness score reaches
$r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$ against enacted strictness and
$r = \CorrOfstedStrictEspoused$ against the espoused statement battery. It
converges on the construct from both directions---agreement across an external
inspector, an independent visit and the head's own self-report---while warmth
fails against every criterion.\footnote{An earlier draft presented this
contrast as a double dissociation: strictness tracking only what observers
record, warmth only what schools say of themselves. The strictness half of
that claim rested on an espoused measure later shown to be broken---it scored
how articulately heads explained themselves rather than what they
claimed---and repairing the measure dissolved the dissociation into the
convergence reported here. The methodological lesson is worth stating plainly:
a dissociation is a claim about a \emph{difference} between two correlations,
and it can be manufactured entirely by measurement error in one cell.}

One further property of the Ofsted warmth score must be reported here, because
it constrains how the score may be used anywhere in this thesis. The score is
produced from report text from which every stated grade has been removed, and
human raters reading that same grade-stripped text produce warmth labels that
track the inspection verdict only weakly ($-0.25$, not significant). The
model's warmth score tracks the verdict at $-0.52$. In other words, the
scoring model recovers the inspection outcome from prose that never states
it, and it does so far more strongly than human readers of the same text.
The size of the resulting distortion is roughly $+0.4$ of a band at
Outstanding falling to $-0.4$ at Inadequate, and the property is inherited:
every version of the instrument ever built shows it. The explanation appears
to be that inspectors write about relationships in a register that moves with
their overall judgement --- better-judged schools receive more emphatic
relational prose --- and the model reads that register fluently. There is a
genuine tension here that cannot be engineered away: the textual features
that make the score agree better with raters are emphasis and repetition,
and Ofsted supplies more of both to schools it judges favourably.
Accordingly, the warmth score is carried in this thesis as a
characterisation measure only. It is never presented as a clean predictor,
and wherever a warmth--outcome association involving it is reported, the
possibility that part of the association is the inspection verdict
re-expressed is stated alongside it. The same reconstruction test applied to
strictness gives useful contrast: the strictness score also tracks the
verdict ($-0.51$), but unlike warmth it also tracks what an independent
observer records in the school, so its association with outcomes survives
conditioning on the grade itself (\cref{ch:paper2}), which the warmth score's
does not.

Adding the four interview features in Model~A moves warmth from
$\RidgeBWarmth$ to $\RidgeAWarmth$ and leaves strictness essentially unchanged
($\RidgeBStrictness$ to $\RidgeAStrictness$). Conducting a headteacher interview
therefore buys almost nothing on strictness that a published inspection report
does not already supply, and buys warmth prediction that remains too weak to
carry an argument. Both statements are conclusions about the sources rather than
about the models, and both follow from the convergence evidence in
\cref{sec:p1_convergence}.

\input{tables/tab_national_model_loo}

A key interpretive consideration is that the two text sources capture the two
dimensions asymmetrically. Ofsted inspection reports are structured around
behaviour and attitudes, leadership, and curriculum quality; the language
inspectors use to describe conduct and expectations maps directly onto the
strictness dimension. Validation against the gold standard confirms this: the
LLM-derived Ofsted strictness score correlates at
$r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$ ($p < 0.001$, $n = \RidgeTrainN$) with enacted
strictness, and at $r = \CorrOfstedStrictEspoused$ with espoused strictness.
The Ofsted warmth score reaches neither criterion: against enacted warmth it
is null in every version of the instrument. Strictness is recoverable from
inspection prose; warmth is not. Reporting the two
criteria separately is what makes this visible---against a blended criterion
both scores would have shown a moderate correlation and the difference between
them would have been averaged out of sight. This asymmetry is not
a failure of the warmth rubric so much as a
structural feature of what Ofsted inspections report: inspectors focus on
curriculum quality, behavioural order, and safeguarding, with little systematic
variation in the language used to describe the relational warmth experienced by
pupils. Behaviour policy documents, by contrast, contain explicit language about
the relational philosophy of the school: the vocabulary of pastoral care,
restorative approaches, praise and recognition, and adult tone provides the
signal most relevant to the warmth dimension. Both text features enter the national-tier model for both dimensions, but for
warmth the behaviour-policy coefficient is negative ($\hat{\beta}_P < 0$),
consistent with the policy-as-aspiration effect. For strictness the Ofsted
coefficient dominates, with a small positive contribution from the behaviour
policy score. Fitted coefficients are reported in \cref{tab:national_model_coefs}
in \cref{app:2A:ridge_spec}.

The fitted values bear this out. Coefficients below are in enacted-score points
per one standard deviation of the feature, so they are directly comparable
within a model. In the national text model, a standard deviation of Ofsted
strictness moves predicted strictness by $+0.314$ points, against an enacted
strictness standard deviation of $0.87$; the behaviour policy score adds
$+0.017$, which is nothing. The externally authored source does effectively all
the work and the school-authored one none. The warmth model has no comparable
structure to report: Ofsted warmth carries $+0.074$ and the behaviour policy
warmth score $-0.043$---the wrong sign for a measure supposed to be reading
warmth, and the policy-as-aspiration effect appearing in the Ridge itself---and
the model as a whole does not cross-validate.

The interview model tells the same story with more features. For strictness the
Ofsted score remains the largest predictor at $+0.263$, ahead of $S4$ at
$+0.191$; the systems tally $S3$ contributes $-0.022$. For warmth no predictor
reaches $+0.17$. The substantive point is the reverse of what earlier drafts
reported: it is the text features, not the interview features, that carry what
signal exists, and they carry it for one dimension only. The earlier claim that
interview features dominate was an artefact of scoring them against a target
they partly constituted.

It is worth being precise about the nature of the warmth limitation, because
the obvious explanation turns out to be the wrong one.
\Cref{fig:ofsted_distributions} plots the distribution of Ofsted LLM scores
across the national run alongside espoused scores for the interviewed schools.
Ofsted warmth scores have mean $3.82$ and SD $0.57$ on the
$1$--$5$ scale; Ofsted strictness scores have mean $3.70$ and SD $0.86$. The
natural reading---that Ofsted warmth fails because it is too compressed to
carry signal---is not supported. Expressed as a fraction of the available scale
range, the Ofsted warmth score is as dispersed as any culture measure used in
this chapter ($14.4$~per cent of range, against $14.8$~per cent for espoused
warmth and $9.9$~per cent for enacted warmth). Range restriction is therefore
not the binding constraint.

Nor is the variation merely noise. Re-scoring the Tier~1 reports with a
different and more capable language model reproduces the same school ordering
($r = 0.631$ between the two models' warmth scores), so the measure is
\emph{reliable}. What it is not is \emph{valid}: that reliable variation
recovers enacted warmth at $r = 0.161$ ($p = 0.11$) while recovering enacted
strictness, a dimension it is not measuring, at $r = 0.303$. What the Ofsted
warmth score appears to track instead is the general favourability of the
inspection narrative---across the national run it correlates with the
LLM-derived Ofsted strictness score at $r = 0.645$, the leadership score at
$r = 0.576$, and the teaching score at $r = 0.534$---rather than any
distinctively relational quality of the
school. In the terms of \cref{sec:p1_cf}, inspection reports describe warmth in
the register of institutional approval rather than of observed interaction, and
that register varies with how positive the inspection was overall. This is a
property of the source, not of the rubric or the model, and it is why
national-tier warmth prediction is not attempted.

\Cref{tab:ofsted_convergent_discriminant} presents the full convergent and
discriminant validity matrix. Convergent validity requires that each Ofsted
score correlates more strongly with the corresponding interview dimension than
with the other. The evidence is sharply asymmetric across the two dimensions,
and the asymmetry is the substantive finding of this section.

The matrix has two panels rather than three, one per criterion, and the result
is a clean crossover: each Ofsted score satisfies the discriminant requirement
against one criterion and fails it against the other, and the two scores pass
against opposite criteria.

Against the \emph{espoused} criterion (Panel~A, $n = 300$), Ofsted warmth
behaves impeccably. It correlates at $r = +0.264$ ($p < 0.001$) with espoused
warmth and at $r = -0.032$ with espoused strictness---convergent validity with a
wide discriminant margin. Ofsted strictness on the same criterion fails: it
reaches only $r = +0.098$ ($p = 0.09$) with espoused strictness while
correlating at $r = +0.225$ ($p < 0.001$) with espoused warmth, so it predicts
the wrong dimension more than twice as well as its own. The two panels use
different samples, since only \RidgeTrainN{} of the 300 schools were also
visited; restricting Panel~A to those \RidgeTrainN{} schools so that all four
cells rest on identical data does not change the pattern.

Against the \emph{enacted} criterion (Panel~B, $n = \RidgeTrainN$) the pattern
reverses exactly. Ofsted strictness now satisfies the requirement decisively,
at $r = +\CorrOfstedStrictness$ ($p < 0.001$) convergent against $r = +0.241$
discriminant. Ofsted warmth now fails it, at $r = +0.161$ ($p = 0.11$)
convergent against $r = +0.303$ ($p = 0.002$) discriminant---again predicting
the wrong dimension roughly twice as well as its own.

The crossover is the substantive finding, and the withdrawal of the composite is
what makes it visible. A blended criterion would have shown both scores
performing moderately against a mixed target and would have concealed the fact
that they are reading different things: inspection text tracks warmth as schools
describe it and strictness as an observer finds it. The practical conclusions
follow directly. Ofsted strictness is a usable enacted-strictness measure and
is used as one throughout this thesis. The Ofsted warmth score is not used as an
enacted warmth measure anywhere, and warmth is omitted from the national-tier
analysis in \cref{ch:paper2} on these grounds---not because the score is noise,
which Panel~A shows it is not, but because what it reliably measures is the
school's self-presentation.

A natural objection is that the warmth null reflects the capability of the
scoring model rather than the content of the reports. To test this, the entire
Tier~1 sample was re-scored with a substantially more capable model
(\texttt{gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07}) under an identical prompt and rubric. The null
is unchanged: re-scored Ofsted warmth correlates at $r = 0.055$ ($p = 0.598$,
$n = 96$) with the gold standard warmth score then in use and $r = 0.022$
($p = 0.834$) with enacted warmth, while re-scored strictness retains convergent
validity ($r = 0.374$, $p < 0.001$) without improving on the smaller model
($r = 0.441$ on the same 96 schools). The two models agree closely with one
another (warmth $r = 0.631$; strictness $r = 0.792$) and produce near-identical
dispersion (warmth SD $0.564$ versus $0.580$), indicating that they are reading
the same underlying text signal. Two scoring models of different sizes, reading
the same reports, recover the same enacted-warmth null. That null is therefore a
property of what Ofsted inspection reports record, not an artefact of the
scoring model.\footnote{This cross-model comparison was run against the earlier
scoring pass and is reported as it was conducted. It bears on whether the
enacted-warmth null survives a change of model, which is the question it was
designed to answer, and both passes agree that it does.}

\subsection{School Websites}
\label{sec:p1_text_web}

A third public-text source---school websites---was collected and scored as an
independent external validation of the warmth and strictness constructs. School
website text is substantively distinct from Ofsted inspection reports (produced by
an external regulator) and behaviour policies (governance documents). Website text
represents the school's own public-facing self-presentation: mission statements,
pastoral philosophy pages, and descriptions of community and ethos written for a
prospective-parent audience.

A national crawl of school website content yielded 3,053 usable text files
covering 2,962 schools with ``ok'' quality status. Text was scored using a
four-step pipeline: crawl, extract, clean, and score. The scoring rubric
(\texttt{website-strictness-warmth-v4}, run on \texttt{gpt-4o-mini}) assessed warmth and
strictness independently on a 1--5 scale, calibrated to a 10-school anchor set
with known gold-standard scores.\footnote{The v4 rubric was signed off as
satisfactory for strictness (anchor rank correlation 10/10) but showed a ceiling
effect for warmth (anchor agreement 3/10), driven by the tendency of website text
to use uniformly positive relational language irrespective of enacted warmth.} In
the national distribution ($n = 3{,}311$ schools with a scored website),
strictness scores are left-skewed (mean~$2.44$, SD~$0.90$), with
most schools showing limited explicit conduct language on their websites; warmth
scores sit higher (mean~$2.78$, SD~$0.79$) but the W=2 lower boundary is known to be
weakly discriminating.

Validation against the gold-standard scores reveals that website LLM warmth does
not predict enacted warmth
($r = -0.060$, $p = .55$, $n = \NTierOne$) and website strictness shows no predictive
validity ($r = +0.037$, $p = .71$). Neither predicts the espoused criterion
either ($r = -0.027$ and $r = -0.006$ on $n = 303$), which distinguishes website
text from the other two sources: Ofsted and behaviour policies each track at
least one criterion, and the website tracks neither. Teaching philosophy classification from
website text---a three-category variable (traditional\,/\,progressive\,/\,
unmarked) derived from \texttt{website\_identity\_scores.csv}---is separately
validated in \cref{ch:paper2} as a national predictor and is not discussed
further here.

Website warmth and strictness scores are interpreted in this chapter as indicators
of \textit{espoused} rather than enacted culture and are not used as predictors in
\cref{ch:paper2}; their role in the analysis is to provide a third independent
replication of the warmth signal failure documented in \cref{sec:p1_policy_aspiration}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Policy-as-Aspiration Effect}
\label{sec:p1_policy_aspiration}

A consistent pattern emerges across all three publicly available text sources ---
Ofsted inspection reports, school behaviour policies, and school websites --- that
provides a substantive finding in its own right. In each case, the text-based
warmth signal fails to predict independently observed enacted warmth, and in
the case of behaviour policy and website text, the direction is counter-intuitive:
schools whose documents express the strongest warmth intentions tend to receive
lower warmth scores from independent observers.

This pattern is consistent with Argyris and Sch\"{o}n's distinction between
\emph{espoused theory} (what people say they believe) and \emph{theory-in-use}
(what their behaviour implies they believe). Applied to school culture, a
behaviour policy is an organisational statement of intent --- the warmth that
leaders aspire to, or present to external audiences --- rather than a record of
the warmth experienced by pupils day-to-day. Schools that face the greatest
relational challenges may produce the most aspirational policy documents;
schools whose warmth is securely embedded in practice may feel less need to
articulate it. The same logic applies to school websites: the public-facing
description of the school's relational culture is aspirational communication,
not an audit of enacted behaviour.

A further line of evidence reinforces this interpretation. The document quality
audit described in \cref{sec:p1_text_docs} established that approximately one
in ten schools in the national sample shares an identical behaviour policy
document with at least one other school, in every identifiable case reflecting
a Multi-Academy Trust template applied uniformly across member schools. These
schools have no school-specific behaviour policy at all: their document is an
institutional governance instrument produced at the trust level and distributed
without adaptation. That this proportion reaches 10.4~per cent---over 350
schools---is itself evidence of how far behaviour policies are removed from the
enacted culture of individual schools. If even the \emph{existence} of a
school-specific document cannot be taken for granted, the assumption that any
such document captures the particular norms of a given school is correspondingly
weakened. The finding is consistent with the broader policy-as-aspiration
pattern: behaviour policies function as compliance artefacts serving external
accountability audiences rather than as descriptions of how any particular
school operates.

The three independent replications of this near-zero or negative warmth signal
against the enacted criterion (Ofsted $r = +0.161$, $p = .108$,
$n = \RidgeTrainN$; behaviour policy $r = -0.083$, $p = .40$, $n = 102$; school
website $r = -0.060$, $p = .55$, $n = \NTierOne$) are more
informative than any individual null result. They provide strong evidence that
the espoused--enacted gap is a genuine structural feature of school culture, not
a measurement artefact arising from a single flawed source. Nor is it an
artefact of the scoring model: re-scoring the entire Tier~1 sample with a
substantially more capable model leaves the warmth null intact while preserving
strictness convergence, and the two models agree closely with each other
(\cref{sec:p1_text_valid}). The remaining explanation is the one the pattern
points to---these sources do not record warmth. This finding
strengthens the methodological case for using direct observation as the primary
culture measure in \cref{ch:paper2}, and motivates the decision to omit
national-tier warmth prediction from the main analysis.

The website strictness result ($r = +0.037$, $p = .71$, $n = \NTierOne$)
reinforces
this interpretation: the explicit conduct language visible on school websites is
similarly disconnected from the strictness observable inside the school. The
behaviour policy strictness score behaves the same way ($r = +0.013$,
$p = .90$, $n = 102$), which is the sharper result of the two, since a behaviour
policy is the one document written specifically to state how a school handles
conduct. Together, the three independent null or negative warmth signals and the
two near-zero strictness correlations constitute a consistent pattern across
five separate measurement attempts using four distinct text sources. Ofsted is
the exception rather than the rule: it is the only source in the set that
recovers enacted strictness, and the only one whose author is an outside
observer who visited the school.

A distinct but related consideration is whether some of the compression visible
across all score distributions in this project---including the gold-standard
interview sub-scores themselves---reflects genuine similarities between English
secondary schools, rather than instrument artefacts alone. Approximately 68~per
cent of English state secondary schools are rated Good by Ofsted and a further
15~per cent Outstanding; the system's shared regulatory architecture---a common
national curriculum, inspection pressure generating isomorphic compliance, shared
teacher training pipelines---tends to push schools toward a common modal standard.
The two sources leave different signatures in the score distributions, and the
gold-standard interview sub-scores can be used to tell them apart. The
researcher-coded warmth sub-score~$W3$ has a mean of~$4.08$ and a standard
deviation of~$0.39$ on the 1--5 item scale ($n = 303$): compressed, but smoothly
so, spanning $2.60$ to $5.00$ with only 12~per cent of schools at the modal
value. That is the signature of a genuinely narrow but real distribution, and it
appears in the instrument most sensitive to between-school differences. The LLM
scores taken from the same interview transcripts behave quite differently: 84~per
cent of schools receive a warmth score of exactly~4, 85~per cent a management
score of exactly~4, and 78~per cent a strictness score of exactly~4
(\cref{sec:p1_leadership}). A distribution with four-fifths of its mass on a
single integer is not a compressed measurement of a homogeneous population; it is
a measuring instrument that has stopped discriminating. Score compression
therefore has two distinct sources: instrument artefacts---the espoused--enacted
gap documented above, and the modal collapse of the LLM transcript scores---and
real between-school homogeneity, which is a substantive empirical finding. The
latter implies that the statistical power available to detect warmth and
strictness effects on pupil progress in \cref{ch:paper2} may be inherently
constrained by restricted range, irrespective of instrument quality.
Separating these two sources is methodologically important: the measurement
failures documented in this chapter do not invalidate the warmth and strictness
constructs, but they do suggest that the English school system, as a whole,
occupies a relatively narrow band on both dimensions.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Management and Leadership}
\label{sec:p1_leadership}

A fourth dimension of school culture---management and leadership---was scored
from the headteacher interview and from Ofsted inspection reports, extending the
espoused--enacted comparison to a domain where the aspiration-echo dynamic might
be expected to operate differently. Where warmth and strictness concern
pupil-facing relational norms, management and leadership concern how the
headteacher organises and directs their staff.

From the headteacher interview, Q3 (``How do you communicate your school's
culture to staff?'') and Q10 (``How do you hold staff to account?'') were scored
by an LLM on a 1--5 scale of management deliberateness and systematicity. The
resulting score---hereafter $M_{\mathrm{trx}}$---has $N = 290$ schools,
mean~$3.12$, and SD~$1.26$, with \PctTrxManagementModal~per cent of schools on
the modal band.\footnote{An earlier unified scorer, which read all four
constructs from the full transcript in a single call, returned a degenerate
distribution on this dimension: SD~$0.38$ with 85~per cent of schools scoring
exactly~4. That was read at the time as evidence that headteachers converge on a
common language of systems-oriented management. It was an artefact of the
scorer. Segmenting the transcript so that each construct is scored from only the
questions that address it recovers the dispersion reported here; the substantive
conclusion that headteachers speak a common management vocabulary is not
supported and is withdrawn.}

From Ofsted inspection reports, the full 20,000-character narrative body
(rather than the 4,000--6,000-character summary extract used for warmth and
strictness) was passed to \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} for a holistic leadership score
on a 1--5 scale. Ofsted grades are inversely coded (1~=~Outstanding,
4~=~Inadequate) while the LLM score runs in the conventional direction, so a
\emph{negative} raw correlation is the one that indicates agreement. Both
validations are reported below on the raw coding to keep the signs comparable.

A pilot validation against actual Ofsted leadership sub-grades for 30~schools
yielded $r = -0.739$ on the raw coding---equivalently $r = +0.739$ against the
reversed grade, the form in which the $r > 0.5$ acceptance threshold was
specified. The national run covered $N = 3{,}277$ schools (mean~$3.81$,
SD~$0.76$), and the national-scale LLM leadership score correlates at
$r = -0.588$ ($n = 3{,}206$) with actual Ofsted leadership grade: the same
direction, at a somewhat lower magnitude. The attenuation is expected by
design. The pilot sampled schools stratified by grade, targeting eight schools
in each of the four grade bands, which spreads the sample across the full range
of the criterion and inflates the correlation relative to the national
distribution, where the great majority of schools are graded Good. The pilot
figure should therefore be read as a check that the scorer discriminates
between grade bands, not as an estimate of national-scale accuracy; $-0.588$ is
the figure to carry forward.

The correlation between headteacher interview management score and Ofsted
LLM leadership score is $r = -0.025$ ($p = .68$, $n = 286$): a precise null
at the level of the interview sample. This is the leadership analogue of the
warmth-signal failure: the headteacher's self-reported management deliberateness
in interview is unrelated to the leadership quality independently assessed from
inspection report text. Two interpretations are consistent with this null. First,
a London confound: the interview sample is heavily London-weighted, and London
schools exhibit a distinct management profile under conditions of intensive
accountability; if the interview scoring captures a London-specific management
discourse rather than between-school variation in management quality, the
cross-source correlation will be attenuated. Second, the aspiration-echo dynamic
applies in the management domain as readily as in the warmth domain: headteachers
who most explicitly articulate management systems and accountability structures
in interview may be precisely those whose schools are under greatest pressure to
improve---the same schools that Ofsted is most likely to rate below their
self-reported management standard. On either account, the management
espoused--enacted gap completes the multi-source argument that public
self-presentation of school culture diverges systematically from externally
assessed reality.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:p1_results}

The \NTierOne{} gold-standard schools have enacted warmth scores ranging
from~4.33 to~8.81 (mean~$6.56$, SD~$0.99$) and enacted strictness scores
from~4.85 to~8.96 (mean~$7.02$, SD~$0.87$). Across all \NInterviewed{}
interviewed schools the espoused scores are wider on warmth (3.04--9.60,
mean~$6.71$, SD~$1.48$) and comparable on strictness (3.25--10.00,
mean~$7.21$, SD~$0.88$); headteachers distinguish themselves from one another on
warmth considerably more than observers distinguish their schools. The positive
correlation between warmth and strictness ($r = \CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$,
$p < .001$ enacted; $r = 0.194$, $p < .001$ espoused) is consistent with the
authoritative-school model, in which high-support and high-demand environments
co-occur more often than not, but refutes a simple unidimensional hierarchy. The
enacted figure is the larger of the two and should be read with the caveat that
both of its components come from one observer on one visit.

Both models shrink their predictions toward the training mean, and the degree
of shrinkage tracks the LOO-CV results almost exactly. Against gold-standard
enacted scores with SD~$= 0.99$ (warmth) and $0.87$ (strictness), the
interview-tier model (Model~A, $N = 300$ prediction targets) produces predicted
warmth with SD~$= 0.24$ and predicted strictness with SD~$= 0.33$. Model~B
(national text only, $N = 3{,}264$ targets) is more compressed still on
strictness (SD~$= 0.30$) and drastically so on warmth (SD~$= 0.09$), under a
tenth of the dispersion of the quantity it is predicting. The warmth figure is
the visible form of a LOO correlation of $r = \RidgeBWarmth$: a model with no
out-of-sample signal has nothing to vary its predictions with and returns
something close to a constant. Model~B strictness, at
$r = \RidgeBStrictness$, retains enough signal to spread its predictions across
roughly a third of a gold-standard standard deviation, which is modest but real.

Neither model's predictions are used as a regressor in \cref{ch:paper2}. That
chapter's primary specification uses the directly observed visit scores for the
gold-standard schools, and its national extension regresses progress on the raw
Ofsted LLM strictness score rather than on $\widehat{S}$ from either Ridge
model. The choice is deliberate rather than incidental: a predicted score
inherits both the sampling error of the training set and the shrinkage
documented above, and regressing an outcome on a shrunken prediction attenuates
the coefficient by an amount that is not recoverable without knowing the
prediction error variance. Using the raw LLM score keeps the right-hand-side
variable a direct measurement, at the cost of measuring only one dimension in a
1--5 scale. The Ridge models are therefore best read as a measurement
contribution of this chapter---an estimate of how much of the observed culture
signal survives when the visit is removed---rather than as an instrument for
extending \cref{ch:paper2} to the national population. Model~B in particular is
a negative result worth reporting: it establishes that publicly available text
alone cannot rank English secondary schools on warmth, which is itself
informative about what the text does and does not contain.

\Cref{tab:espoused_enacted_gap} reports the espoused--observed gap across three
independent sources. A word on the benchmark is needed here. Elsewhere in this
chapter the enacted measure is the researcher visit, and Ofsted text is treated
as one of several espoused public sources. These comparisons, however, run at
national scale, where no visit benchmark exists. Ofsted is used as the observed
side of the comparison for two reasons: it is the only source in the set not
authored by the school itself, being written by an external inspector following
a site visit; and its strictness signal has already been shown to converge with
directly observed strictness ($r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$, \cref{sec:p1_text_valid}). That
warrant is dimension-specific---Ofsted warmth carries no such validity---so the
comparison is restricted to strictness and leadership. The gaps below should
accordingly be read as school self-description measured against external
inspection, not against the researcher visits used in the primary analysis.

The behaviour-policy gap (espoused BP strictness minus
Ofsted-observed strictness) is close to zero on average ($\Delta = -0.14$,
SD~$= 1.21$, $N = 3{,}269$), with 40~per cent of schools showing a negative
gap (espoused lower than enacted) and 29~per cent a positive gap. The website
gap is considerably larger and more consistent in direction ($\Delta = -1.15$,
SD~$= 1.25$, $N = 3{,}273$; 70~per cent negative), confirming that school
websites substantially understate the strictness recorded by inspectors. The
management gap is near zero on average ($\Delta = +0.02$, SD~$= 0.89$,
$N = 286$), consistent with the null correlation reported above.

\input{tables/tab_espoused_enacted_gap}

A fourth measurement cell provides further convergent evidence from the
recipients of school culture rather than its producers. Parent View is the Ofsted
online survey of parental perceptions; two items---``My child is happy at this
school'' (Q1) and ``My child feels safe at this school'' (Q2)---capture parental
perceptions of the relational and emotional environment. Averaging the
proportion of parents giving positive responses to Q1 and Q2 gives a measure
that reproduces the same crossover as the Ofsted and website sources, from a
fourth and wholly independent respondent group. It correlates at $r = +0.225$
($p < .001$, $n = 300$) with \emph{espoused} warmth but at only $r = +0.156$
($p = .12$, $n = \RidgeTrainN$) with \emph{enacted} warmth---and at $r = +0.282$
($p = .004$) with enacted \emph{strictness}, again predicting the other
dimension better than its own. Among the 93 schools with temporally aligned
September 2025 release data---where the survey refers to the same academic year
as the observation---the espoused association is $r = +0.259$ ($p = .012$).
That parents, inspectors, and schools' own websites all track espoused warmth
while failing to track enacted warmth is the most robust single finding in this
chapter, and it is available only because the two criteria are kept apart.
No Parent View items isolate strictness from overall
satisfaction.

These results frame parental perception as a fourth independent data source on
school culture, distinct from the researcher-observed (enacted), headteacher-reported
(espoused), and document-derived (aspirational) cells. The moderate positive
correlation with gold-standard warmth is expected given the source-type mismatch:
parents perceive culture through a different lens than trained observers, and
Parent View responses conflate warmth with overall school quality. The stronger
association in the temporally aligned release underlines that survey timing
matters; older Parent View releases attenuate the correlation as school culture
drifts relative to the survey reference period.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:p1_conclusion}

This chapter has introduced and validated a two-dimensional measurement framework
for school culture---warmth and strictness---using a three-tier prediction cascade
calibrated to a gold standard of direct classroom and school observation. Three
findings warrant emphasis.

First, the gold-standard instrument produces internally coherent and reliable scores.
Inter-rater reliability for the headteacher interview items is substantial across
all eleven questions (Pearson $r$ range 0.61--0.74; within-one-point agreement
exceeding 90\% throughout), and classroom and outside-of-lesson observation items
show similar levels of pairwise consistency. The visit and interview sub-scores
capture meaningfully distinct aspects of school culture rather than providing
redundant measures of a single construct: their within-school correlation is
weak ($r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ for warmth, $r = \GoldStrictnessSplit$ for
strictness; teaching has no espoused counterpart to compare). Earlier versions
of this
framework read that divergence as a reason to combine the two sources in a fixed
60/40 weighting. It is in fact a reason not to: two measures sharing at most a
sixth of their variance are not fallible readings of one quantity, and their
weighted average is a third quantity that is neither. The enacted and espoused
scores are therefore reported separately throughout, and the enacted scores
predict pupil progress in \cref{ch:paper2}, providing external criterion
validity for the measurement framework.

Second, and most consequentially, a systematic espoused--enacted divergence
characterises school culture self-presentation across multiple independent sources.
Headteachers rate their schools on average $0.51$ points higher on warmth
than independent observers record---a systematic positive bias---while the
corresponding strictness gap is near zero ($+0.07$ points, $p = 0.53$),
indicating that the systems-based strictness self-report is substantially more
accurate than warmth self-report. The warmth bias is replicated in LLM scores
derived from Ofsted reports, behaviour policy documents and school websites, and
in Parent View survey responses. In none of these public-text sources does
the warmth signal predict independently observed enacted warmth; in the case of
behaviour policy and website text, the direction is counter-intuitive---schools
expressing the strongest warmth aspirations tend to score lower on observed
warmth. The management dimension exhibits the same pattern: the correlation between
headteacher interview management scores and Ofsted LLM leadership scores is
$r = -0.025$ ($p = .68$), a precise null that mirrors the warmth-signal failure
in a different domain. This convergence across four independent
source-type comparisons implies that the gap is a genuine structural feature of
English school culture rather than an artefact of any single measurement
instrument, and provides direct empirical evidence for Argyris and Sch\"{o}n's
distinction between espoused theory and theory-in-use at the level of
institutional culture.

Third, despite the warmth measurement challenge, enacted strictness is recoverable
at national scale from Ofsted inspection report text ($r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$, $p < 0.001$,
$n = \RidgeTrainN$, against directly observed strictness), enabling a national-tier
analysis of strictness effects on pupil
progress that does not depend on direct observation. The text-based warmth signal
shows no such validity---and re-scoring the entire Tier~1 sample with a more
capable language model leaves the null unchanged---so national-tier warmth
prediction is not attempted in \cref{ch:paper2}.

Two limitations of the design should be noted. The three-tier structure means
that scores are not fully comparable across tiers: the gold standard carries
direct observation, interview-tier scores derive
from self-report calibrated against that standard, and national-tier scores rely
on text proxies alone. Score compression---a restricted empirical range visible
across all sources---partly reflects genuine between-school homogeneity in the
English system (approximately 68\% of state secondary schools rated Good by
Ofsted, 15\% Outstanding), rather than instrument failure alone; even a perfectly
specified measure would recover limited between-school variance in a system shaped
by common regulatory pressures.

The most productive directions for future extension follow directly from these
limitations. The gap between visit-only and interview-only scores constitutes a
measure of cultural coherence---whether the headteacher's stated philosophy is
reflected in the observable environment---that could be studied as an outcome
predictor in its own right. And the warmth sub-scores most resistant to text-based
recovery (W1: in-lesson staff--pupil warmth; W2: warmth in transitions and breaks)
represent the highest-priority targets for future observational data collection at
scale.

\section{Scoring Guide and Item Definitions}
\label{app:2A:guide}

This section documents how the individual proforma items combine into the nine
sub-scores and the two score sets. The items themselves---question
wording, response format, and the anchor description attached to each rating
level---are reproduced in full in \cref{app:2A:interview_guide} for the interview
and \cref{app:2A:visit_protocol} for the two observation forms.

Every item is recorded on a 1--5 scale. Two observation items admit a zero code
with a distinct meaning: a zero on \emph{response of pupils to sanctions} records
that no sanction was issued during the lesson, and a zero on \emph{effectiveness
of pupil discussion} records that no discussion was initiated. The first is not
a genuine low score---an orderly lesson requiring no sanction should not be
counted as one where sanctions were ignored---so it is replaced by
$6 - \text{disruption}$, which assigns a calm unsanctioned lesson a high value
and a disrupted unsanctioned lesson a low one. The second is treated as a
genuine negative, since a lesson containing no discussion did not achieve
effective discussion.

Observation item scores are averaged across lessons and across researchers to
give one value per school before the sub-scores are formed.
\cref{tab:p1_subscore_items} lists the constituent items of each sub-score.

\input{snippets/subscore_items}

Two items on the outside-of-lessons form---consistency of verbal feedback and
quality of differentiation across lessons---are recorded on the form as
cross-lesson averages of the corresponding lesson observation items, and so
enter no sub-score of their own. Of the ten yes/no systems in the interview, S3
uses the eight that represent behavioural or academic requirements; the two
reward systems (reward assemblies and reward points) are excluded, as their
presence indicates a warmer rather than a stricter regime. S3 is the count of
those eight, rescaled to 0--5. Every other sub-score is the mean of its items,
computed over the items present so that a missing item reduces the number of
contributing items rather than the school's score.

Sub-scores combine into two score sets grouped by source, observed behaviour and
stated philosophy kept apart:

\text{Espoused:}\quad

\cref{sec:p1_interview}: once the question-level explanation ratings are

staff turnover and morale, and they form the staff climate score SC instead.
Two earlier adjustments --- the averaging of the systems count $S3$ into
espoused strictness, and an interview-quality multiplier applied to espoused
warmth --- were removed when their properties were measured, as described in
\cref{sec:p1_interview}. The pre-revision formulas and scores are retained in
\texttt{warm\_strict\_scores\_iq\_all\_adjusted.csv} so that any earlier
figure can be reproduced.

Schools with only one of the two instruments carry only the corresponding score
set: an interview without a visit yields espoused scores alone, a visit without
an interview yields enacted scores alone. No imputation is attempted across the
two, since \cref{sec:p1_convergence} establishes that neither predicts the

\section{Behaviour Policy Crawl Methodology}
\label{app:2A:crawl}

Behaviour policies are not held in any central register, so each document had to
be located on the school's own website. The crawl proceeds in three stages, run
by \texttt{download\_behaviour\_policies.py} and
\texttt{select\_behaviour\_policy\_documents.py}.

\paragraph{URL discovery.} Starting from the school website recorded in GIAS,
the crawler tries a fixed list of conventional policy-page paths
(\texttt{/policies/}, \texttt{/key-information/policies},
\texttt{/statutory-information/policies} and around thirty further variants,
each in both capitalisation conventions), since English school sites cluster
heavily on a small number of content-management templates. Pages that are found
are parsed for links to documents with a \texttt{.pdf}, \texttt{.doc} or
\texttt{.docx} extension. Where the path list fails, the crawler falls back to
site-scoped web search---Brave Search, with Google Custom Search as a
secondary---restricted to the school's own domain and querying a set of title
phrases (``behaviour policy'', ``relationships and behaviour
policy'', ``positive behaviour policy'', ``behaviour for learning''
and similar, including the American spelling and the initialisms some trusts
use). The two spellings matter: a non-trivial number of English schools publish
under ``behavior''.

\paragraph{Retrieval.} Candidate documents are scored on link text, file name
and URL path, and the highest-scoring few per school are downloaded rather than
only the single best, so that the selection stage has something to choose
between. Requests are rate-limited and identify themselves as
\texttt{school-policy-downloader/1.0}; each file is stored with its SHA-256
digest so that repeat runs are idempotent and identical documents shared across
a multi-academy trust can be detected. The retrieval stage returned 8,277
candidate documents.

\paragraph{Policy identification.} The selection stage picks one document per
school and records why. Candidates are scored on weighted regular-expression
matches against both document metadata and the first portion of extracted text.
Positive patterns range from an exact ``behaviour policy'' title match
down to weaker signals such as ``discipline policy'' or ``conduct
policy''; negative patterns penalise the documents most easily confused with a
behaviour policy---anti-bullying policies, exclusions procedures, home--school
agreements, and primary-phase policies published by all-through trusts. The gap
between the best and second-best candidate determines a confidence label. Of
3,363 schools with at least one candidate, selection was high-confidence for
2,755, medium for 387 and low for 79, with a further 142 resolved by hand; 334
were flagged for manual review, of which the sampled subset is discussed in
\cref{sec:p1_text_docs}.

The residual risk this procedure cannot eliminate is that a school publishes a
current policy the crawl never reaches---behind a login, inside a newsletter, or
as a web page rather than a document. That failure is not random with respect to
school characteristics, which is why coverage is reported by tier in
\cref{app:2A:sample} rather than assumed uniform.

The national-tier model takes the language model's holistic score for each
document as a single number per source. The question that specification invites
is whether the model's reading contributes anything that a deterministic counter
could not recover from the same text.
\Cref{tab:ridge_spec_comparison} tests this directly, comparing two feature
representations under the identical estimation procedure: (A) the two holistic
scores used throughout the chapter, and (D) the keyword-rule scores computed by
the pattern matcher that runs alongside the language model on the same
documents, as a floor against which to judge whether the language model
contributes anything beyond term counting.\footnote{The labels (A) and (D) are
retained from an earlier four-specification design that also substituted four
Ofsted ``sub-dimension'' scores for the holistic one, singly and in combination.
Those specifications have been withdrawn along with sub-component scoring
itself, for the reason given in \cref{sec:p1_text}: the four facets were not
independent measurements of anything, and a comparison between a holistic score
and four copies of it is not a feature-representation test.}

\input{tables/tab_ridge_spec_comparison}

The comparison rests on the identical \RidgeTrainN{}~schools in every cell, so
it is like for like.\footnote{The Ofsted keyword-rule scores are taken
from the national scoring run, the same file that supplies the holistic Ofsted
scores used elsewhere in the chapter.} The result is dimension-specific, and the
split runs along the same seam as the rest of the chapter.

On strictness, the language model's reading contributes something the counter
cannot recover. The holistic specification reaches $r = \RidgeBStrictness$ with
a cross-validated $R^2$ of $0.114$; the keyword baseline on the same documents
reaches $r = 0.094$ and an $R^2$ of $0.008$, barely distinguishable from
predicting the sample mean. Whatever the strictness score captures, it is not
the frequency of particular words. It is located in an act of reading rather
than in any countable property of the text, which is the opposite of the pattern
a feature-representation artefact would produce.

On warmth, neither specification recovers anything. Both select a penalty at or
near the top of the grid and shrink almost entirely to the training mean, and
both return a negative cross-validated $R^2$ ($-0.015$ for the holistic scores,
$-0.020$ for the keyword rules), meaning each predicts the enacted warmth score
worse than the sample mean does; the keyword specification's predictions are too
nearly constant for a correlation to be defined at all. This is not evidence
that the warmth score is empty---\cref{sec:p1_text_valid} shows it predicts the
espoused criterion perfectly respectably---but it does establish that the part
of it that survives to the national tier carries no enacted-warmth signal for
either representation to find. The feature-representation test can only
discriminate between two readings of the text where there is something in the
text to read.
