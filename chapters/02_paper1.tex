\begin{chapterabstract}
How warm a school is towards its pupils, and how strictly it runs, are
widely thought to affect how much progress pupils make. Neither is measured
in any dataset that covers every school. This paper measures both, and asks
how much of each can be read from the documents that every school already
produces. In \NTierOne{} schools I collected a gold-standard measure: a day
of structured observation recorded what each school does, and an interview
with its headteacher recorded what the head says it does. I then read every
school's inspection report, behaviour policy and website in two ways. A
prediction model learns, from the visited schools, which words and phrases
go with warmer or stricter schools, and is tested only on schools it has
never seen. A set of one-page marking schemes is applied by a language
model, once, and judged against the same observations. There are three
findings. First, inspection reports carry a modest signal of how warm a
school actually is: the prediction model reads it at $\PredWOfsted$, even after removing what a school's intake statistics explain. Second, prediction models read strictness only weakly and fragilely. The marking scheme reads it at $\RubricSOfstedDec$ after adjusting for pupil intake, and $\RubricSOfstedRaw$ before. Its reading, unlike the models', survives a change of language model and the other checks applied to it. The two
dimensions are written down differently: warmth shows in a report's
everyday wording, strictness in specific claims that have to be asked for.
Third, what schools write about themselves reflects, at most, what their heads say: their strictness and staff-morale language tracks the head's private account, their warmth language tracks nothing, and neither tracks what observers see. Of the \TopQN{} schools whose heads placed them in the top quarter for warmth, only \TopQWMatch{} were there when observed. Chance alone would put six or seven there; two noisy readings of the same quantity would put about \TopQExpectedSameW{}. The measures that pass these tests
become the explanatory variables of the next chapter.
\end{chapterabstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:p1_intro}

How warm a school is towards its pupils, and how strictly it runs, are two of
the things most often said to affect how much progress its pupils make.
Neither is measured in any administrative dataset. In this paper I construct
measures of these two dimensions of school culture, which I call warmth and
strictness, for a sample of English state secondary schools, and I ask how
well each of them can be recovered from data that exist for all 3,332
schools in the country.

The two dimensions have precise meanings here. Warmth is
the relational quality of a school: whether pupils are known, cared for and
supported by the adults around them. In a lesson it shows in whether the
teacher uses pupils' names, how praise is given and how the teacher and the
class speak to one another. Between lessons it shows in how staff and pupils
deal with each other in corridors and at break, and in whether rewards are
used consistently across the lessons of the day. Strictness is the school's
behavioural structure: whether
expectations of conduct are clear, enforced and met. In a lesson it shows in
how much misbehaviour is tolerated before a sanction, how much disruption
occurs and how pupils respond when they are corrected. Outside lessons it
shows in how pupils arrive, how they move between lessons and how the dining
hall runs. Strictness in this sense means clear rules applied consistently rather than harsh punishment. A school whose
sanctions are applied by every member of staff in the same way is strict in
this sense, and whether it is also warm is a separate question.

The two dimensions come from the parenting typology of \citet{baumrind1971}
and \citet{maccoby1983}, which classifies parenting by demandingness and
responsiveness and finds that the combination of both, the authoritative
style, produces the best child outcomes across cognitive and non-cognitive
domains. \citet{majumder2015} and \citet{zhang2020} find the same pattern in
educational attainment, with each dimension carrying an independent effect.
The transfer to schools is direct: demandingness is strictness and
responsiveness is warmth. \citet{pellerin2005} and
\citet{gregory2010,gregory2011} apply the typology to schools and find that
pupils in schools combining support with structure report less victimisation
and more engagement, and \citet{gottfredson2005} reviews the related evidence on school discipline.

What makes the two dimensions measurable in a school,
as opposed to in a family, is that both are enacted in public. A researcher
sitting in a lesson can see whether names are used and how disruption is
dealt with, and a researcher standing in a corridor can see whether pupils
move calmly and whether staff speak to them. Neither requires access to
anything private. I treat warmth and strictness as two separate dimensions
rather than as the two ends of one scale, because a school can be warm
without being orderly and orderly without being warm. Whether the two go
together in practice is a question the data answer rather than something I assume.

I also observed a third dimension, the quality of teaching practice.
This is not part of culture as I define it, but it is the most obvious rival
explanation for any association between culture and results, and it is
carried through the analysis for that reason.

The difficulty in measuring any of this is that none of it is recorded for
every school. What is recorded, for every school, is text: an inspection
report written by an external inspector, a behaviour policy written by the
school, and a website written by the school for parents. For a smaller set
of schools this study adds what no administrative source holds, which is
direct observation of the school at work and a structured interview with its headteacher.

The design follows from that asymmetry. For \NTierOne{}
schools I have a research visit, made up of structured observations in
lessons and around the school, and an interview with the headteacher, and
from these I construct a gold-standard measure of each dimension. Because
the interview and the visit disagree substantially about the same school,
the gold standard is in fact two measures rather than one: an \emph{enacted}
score, from what the observers recorded, and an \emph{espoused} score, from
what the headteacher reported. For a further \NTierTwo{} schools I have the
interview alone, and for the remaining national population only the public
text.

I then ask how much of the gold standard the public text contains, in two
ways. The first is a prediction model: using the visited schools, a
statistical model learns which words and phrases of a document go with
higher or lower observed scores, and it is tested only on schools it was
not fitted on. Before fitting, identifying details are removed from the text,
and the part of each score that school demographics could explain is
removed from the target, so the model earns credit for reading culture in prose and nothing for recognising a school's intake or its paperwork. The
second is a marking scheme: a one-page written rubric for each dimension,
applied to each document once by a language model, and judged by one criterion: correlation with what observers recorded. The first method
assumes nothing about where culture shows up in text; the second states a
theory of it. Comparing them, source by source and dimension by dimension,
shows not only which measures can be extended to every school in the
country but what kind of reading each dimension requires.

There are three main findings. The first is that inspection reports
carry a modest but real signal of observed warmth. A prediction model
reading only the report's wording reproduces the observed warmth of
held-out schools at $\PredWOfsted$. The most that any correlation with a score this noisy could reach is about $0.83$, a ceiling derived below from the score's reliability. Before intake is removed
from the target the correlation is $\PredWOfstedRaw$, and after it is
$\PredWOfsted$. Removing intake therefore strengthens the signal rather
than creating it, so the signal is carried by the prose itself. The
signal is clearest in what marks the colder schools. Their reports fall
back on stock assurances such as ``pastoral support is in place'' and
``pupils feel confident they have a trusted adult''. The warmer schools'
reports instead spend their sentences describing pupils and adults
dealing with one another, spread across many small choices of phrase
rather than concentrated in quotable lines. Observed teaching behaves
like warmth, with a signal of $\PredTOfsted$.

The second finding is that observed strictness must be read differently.
The prediction model reads it only weakly, at the edge of what the
scrambled-score benchmark allows. One embedding model reads it more
strongly, but the result does not replicate across encoders, and the
appendix records it as unresolved. The one-page marking scheme reads
strictness at $\RubricSOfstedDec$. That reading is indistinguishable in
size from the best model's. However, it survives a change of language
model and demographic adjustment, and the models' readings do not. The
reason is that the two dimensions are written down differently. Warmth
lives in a report's habitual register, which a model with no
instructions can learn. Strictness lives in specific, checkable claims,
for example ``low-level disruption is rare'', which only directed
questions extract.

The third finding is that the enacted and espoused measures diverge,
and that the divergence is a fact about warmth. The interview and the
visit are correlated at only $\GoldWarmthSplit$ for warmth, against
$\GoldStrictnessSplit$ for strictness. Of the \TopQN{} schools whose
heads rated them in the top quarter for warmth, only \TopQWMatch{} were
in the top quarter as observed. Chance alone would put roughly seven
there, and two noisy readings of the same quantity would put about
\TopQExpectedSameW{}. For strictness the match is \TopQSMatch{} of
\TopQN{}, against a ceiling of about \TopQExpectedSameS{} once
measurement error is allowed for. The same asymmetry runs through the
public text. What schools write in their policies and on their websites
tracks what their heads say about strictness and about staff morale.
However, their warmth language shows no relation to the observers, or
even to their own head's private account, though the espoused warmth
scale is coarse, so only a weak relation can be excluded.

The paper sits between four literatures and adds something to each of them.

The school-climate literature measures culture through pupil, teacher and
parent surveys \citep{hoy2001,cohen2009,thapa2013}. Those surveys have
produced consistent associations between climate and wellbeing, attendance and attainment. However, they capture perceptions rather than practice and are open to social desirability. They are also costly at national scale.
\citet{vanhoutte2005} argues that climate, meaning the aggregate of members'
perceptions, should be kept apart from culture, meaning the shared norms that
govern behaviour, and it is culture in that sense that the measures here aim
at. Administrative proxies such as exclusion rates \citep{machin2020}
conflate culture with intake, and the inspection grade folds many dimensions
into a single verdict.

The school-effectiveness tradition that begins with \citet{coleman1966} and
\citet{rutter1979} located the difference schools make in what Rutter called ethos rather than in resources. Ethos here means the expectations and routine practice that a school sustains. Later syntheses agreed on a list that includes
an orderly environment and a supportive climate
\citep{edmonds1979,sammons1995,teddlie2000}. That strand has stayed largely
qualitative, because ethos has been measured by case study.

Structured classroom observation, in particular the CLASS framework \citep{pianta2008}, measures emotional support and classroom
organisation directly and predicts pupil gains at secondary level
\citep{allen2013} and in English primary schools \citep{sammons2008}. The
observation forms I use draw on those two domains, but they aggregate across
lessons and out-of-lesson settings so as to characterise a school rather
than a teacher. To my knowledge no study has combined such observation with
a leadership interview and public text in order to separate what a school
does from what it says.

The literature on high-expectation, high-structure
charter and academy models
\citep{abdulkadiroglu2011,angrist2010,dobbie2013,fryer2014} finds large
effects of a bundle of practices in which strict behaviour norms and warm,
individual relationships come together. It is the origin of the strictness
dimension and the reason to expect it to matter, but it has not measured the two dimensions apart.

Finally, the text-as-data literature in economics
\citep{gentzkow2019} supplies the tools for reading the public record at
scale. Its standing warning is that general-purpose methods misread
domain-specific text \citep{loughran2011,muddiman2019}. This paper uses both of that literature's modern tools, supervised text regression and marking schemes applied by a large language model \citep{gilardi2023,eloundou2024}. It tests each against direct observation and reports which dimension of culture each can read. For one concrete measurement problem, it answers the question posed by \citet{ludwig2025} of when instructed language-model
measurement adds value over statistical alternatives.

Against those four literatures, the paper contributes three things: a measure of enacted culture built from observation rather than perception, with the perception data from headteachers and parents kept as comparisons against it; national extensions of culture through public text, whose validity is tested against observation rather than assumed; and a like-for-like comparison, on one criterion, of the two ways economists now read text, prediction models and marking schemes. The comparison shows that which one works is a property of the construct.

The next section describes the data. The paper then constructs the gold standard, describes how the two text readings are built and judged, reports what the measures show, and concludes.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:p1_data}

The paper uses four kinds of data on English state secondary schools: a
research visit and a headteacher interview that were carried out for this
study, three collections of public text, a national survey of parents, and
the Department for Education's school-level administrative data. The sources divide by whose account of the school they give. The visit
records what trained observers saw the school do, and the inspection report
records what an inspector saw, so these two are sources on \emph{enacted}
culture. The interview, the behaviour policy and the website record what the
school says about itself, the first to a researcher and the other two in
writing, so these three are sources on \emph{espoused} culture. The parent
survey belongs to neither group. Parents report their own family's experience
of the school, and whether that experience follows what observers see or
what the school says about itself is one of the questions the paper answers.

Together the sources define three tiers of coverage: \NTierOne{} schools with
a visit, an interview and the public sources; \NTierTwo{} schools with an
interview and the public sources (a handful lack one of the three); and
the rest of the national population
with the public sources alone. \Cref{tab:tier_summary} sets out the tiers and
compares the schools in each on intake, size and inspection grade.

\input{tables/tab_tier_summary}

\textbf{Visits and interviews.} Between November 2023 and April 2025 the
research team, working under my direction, interviewed the headteacher at
304 schools and visited \NTierOne{} of them. In eight cases the head was
unavailable and we interviewed the senior leader responsible for behaviour
and culture instead.\footnote{The counts that follow differ by a school or
two from place to place, for four reasons which I state once here. One of
the 304 heads left the statement battery blank, so 303 schools carry an
espoused score and the interview-only tier numbers \NTierTwo{}; that school
belongs to neither tier in \cref{tab:tier_summary}. Of the \NTierOne{}
visited schools, two have no scored inspection report and two have no
published parent survey, so correlations and prediction models that use
those sources run on 101 schools. The deconfounded correlations run on 100, because one school lacks a control variable. Among the interviewed schools, between 299
and 302 have each text source. Finally, one visited school and three
interviewed schools could not be matched to the 2023/24 characteristics
panel used in \cref{app:2A:sample}, which therefore describes 102 and 300
schools.} Schools were recruited by approaching headteachers directly by
email and telephone, and by referral from schools that had already taken
part. No quota was applied by region, school type or inspection grade.
Three-quarters of the interviews and half of the visits took place in the
2024/25 academic year. At the end of every interview the headteacher was
asked whether the school would be willing to host a visit, which means that
the visited schools are a self-selected subset of the interviewed ones.

A visit lasted a school day and was made by a team of two, and occasionally
three, researchers. The school was asked in advance to arrange four to six
lessons spread across year groups and subjects, and it chose the particular
lessons itself. The team observed those lessons, typically five of them,
spread across Years 7 to 11. The researchers knew which school they were
visiting but not its inspection grade, and the school knew it was being
observed but not which items were being rated. Each school also allowed us
to observe pupils moving between lessons, and pupils and staff interacting
at break and lunch, which is what the outside-of-lessons form rates. Each observation covered either a whole lesson or half of one, and every researcher present rated it independently on the lesson observation form. The team also watched arrival to lessons, movement between lessons, break and lunch, and at the end of the day each researcher completed the outside-of-lessons form once, summarising the whole day.

The lesson form has twenty items; the mapping from the printed form to the scored items is in \cref{app:2A:guide}. Ten of the twenty bear on warmth and strictness, for example the
frequency with which the teacher uses pupils' names, and the level of
misbehaviour tolerated before a first sanction. The other ten bear on
teaching practice, such as questioning, feedback, explanation and structure.
The outside-of-lessons form has thirteen items: nine on the school outside
the classroom (sanctions and rewards, corridors, arrival, the dining hall,
and interactions and relationships at break), two on the condition of wall
displays, and two in which the observer compares the teaching seen across
the day. Not every item enters a score, and the measurement section says which do and why the rest do not. Every item is rated from 1 to 5
against written anchors at 1, 3 and 5, and the item ratings are averaged across researchers and across lessons to give one value per school.

The school choosing the lessons is a limitation of the visit as a measure of practice. However, the most likely direction of the problem is known. A school will tend to show a visitor its better lessons, so if anything the enacted scores are too high and too similar to one another, which makes differences between schools harder rather than easier to find. What this argument cannot exclude is differential staging, meaning that some schools are better at performing for a visitor than others, and its direction is unknown.

The interview lasted about an hour and followed a fixed guide. It had three
parts: twelve open questions on the school's approach to behaviour,
relationships, wellbeing and teaching, the first of which records the
head's tenure; ten statements on which the
headteacher rated their own school on a five-point agreement scale (for
example ``The sanction systems are applied consistently by all staff'' and
``Pupils feel like their teachers care''); and a
checklist of ten named behaviour systems (centralised detentions, an
internal exclusion room, a mobile phone ban and so on) recorded as present
or absent. Two researchers attended every interview, one asking the
questions and one observing, and both scored the open questions
independently. The observation forms and the interview guide are reproduced
in full in \cref{app:2A:visit_protocol,app:2A:interview_guide}, and the
agreement between researchers on the observation and interview items is reported together with the measures below.

\textbf{Public text.} Three collections of text cover the national
population, and their coverage is best read against the analysis panel of
3,332 open state secondary schools. A scored inspection report exists for
3,272 of them (98.2 per cent); the rest had opened too recently to have been
inspected. A behaviour policy document exists for 3,294 (98.9 per cent),
one document per school, taken from the school's website. About one document
in ten is a trust-wide policy adopted word for word by the school. A website
crawl exists for 3,311 schools (99.4 per cent). The crawl takes the home,
values and welcome pages, which are the pages on which a school describes
itself to prospective parents. Of those crawls, 3,284 contain prose that can
be scored, and where a crawl returned no prose the school is flagged rather
than dropped, so that missingness is recorded as a variable and not treated
as a silent exclusion. The counts differ by a few dozen schools because a
handful of schools have no report, no policy or no website;
\cref{app:2A:sample} gives the reconciliation.

\textbf{Parent survey.} Ofsted's Parent View survey is open to the parents
of every school and is published once enough parents have responded. I use
the most recent release for each school and the two items closest to
warmth: the share of responding parents who agree that their child is happy
at the school, and the share who agree that their child feels safe there.
The two shares are averaged into one parent-reported warmth measure, which
is available for 3,255 schools in the panel and for 101 of the visited
schools. Parents choose whether to respond. The
typical release rests on about 120 responses at a response rate of about 12
per cent, so the measure is the view of the parents who chose to give one. Responses also accumulate over several years, so for some schools the parent data are older than the culture data. The survey has no item on strictness.

\textbf{Administrative data.} All schools are matched on their unique
reference number to a school-year panel that I built from the Department for
Education's published tables for 2015/16 to 2024/25. The panel carries pupil
composition, prior attainment, Progress~8 and its components, workforce and
finance. This paper uses the panel only to describe the samples; the
following chapter uses it for outcomes and controls. The panel also carries
each school's most recent overall inspection grade. Inspections from
September 2024 onwards no longer award an overall grade, so the grade used
throughout is the last one each school received before that date, on the
scale 1 (Outstanding) to 4 (Inadequate). Schools inspected only since then,
or never inspected, have no grade and are missing on that variable.

\textbf{Representativeness.} \Cref{tab:2A:representativeness} compares the
visited and interviewed schools with the national population. Both samples
resemble the population on school type, phase, admissions, size and urban
location, and they differ from it on deprivation by at most two percentage
points. Both over-represent London (45 per cent of visited schools against
15 per cent nationally), which shows up as a higher share of pupils with
English as an additional language. Both also lean towards stronger schools:
25 per cent of visited schools held an Outstanding inspection grade as at
August 2024 against 15 per cent nationally, and their mean Progress~8 in
2018/19 was $+0.17$ against $+0.04$. That lean is what one would expect from recruiting through headteachers,
since the schools most likely to agree to take part are those that are
confident about what a visitor will find, and it has two consequences. The first is that estimates from the visited sample apply most directly to a population that is somewhat stronger than the national average. The second is a restricted range: the sample under-represents the weakest schools, so both culture and outcomes vary less than nationally. In the following chapter this works against finding associations. The next section turns these records into scores.

\section{Measurement}
\label{sec:p1_measurement}

This section builds the measures in the order that the design requires. It
first constructs the gold-standard scores from the visits and the interviews
and reports how reliable they are. It then builds the prediction models that will carry the gold standard to the schools that were not visited. It ends with the marking schemes read by a large language model, and with how both routes are judged.

\subsection{The gold standard}

The scores are built up in two steps, and they come in two sets that are kept apart. Every observation item and every interview statement is rated from 1 to 5. First, items are averaged into
\emph{sub-scores}, each of which covers one dimension in one setting: warmth
in lessons, warmth outside lessons, strictness in lessons, strictness outside
lessons, and so on. Second, the sub-scores for a dimension are averaged into
that dimension's \emph{score}, so that each school ends up with one warmth
score and one strictness score, and each score is doubled from the 1--5 range of the items to a 0--10 scale, on which attainable scores run from 2 to 10.

Two sets of scores are constructed and kept apart. The \emph{enacted} scores use the observations alone. Enacted
warmth is the average of in-lesson warmth (the use of names, praise, the
quality of interaction, pupil participation and motivation) and
out-of-lesson warmth (relationships and interactions during transitions and
breaks, and how consistently rewards are used). Enacted strictness is the
average of in-lesson strictness (misbehaviour tolerated, disruption,
response to sanction, respect and concentration) and out-of-lesson
strictness (how consistently sanctions are used, corridors, arrival, the
dining hall and recreational space). Enacted teaching is the average of the
ten lesson items on teaching practice. The \emph{espoused} scores use the
headteacher's statement ratings alone. Espoused warmth is the average of the
three warmth statements (rewards applied consistently; pupils feel safe;
pupils feel their teachers care) and espoused strictness is the average of
the four strictness statements (sanctions applied consistently; behaviour in
lessons; behaviour outside lessons; teachers feel respected). The two
remaining scored statements, on staff turnover and morale, form a
staff-climate score. The item definitions and the mapping of items to
sub-scores are given in \cref{app:2A:guide}.

Not everything that was recorded enters a score. On the visit, the four
outside-of-lessons items left out are the two on wall displays (their
condition, and their alignment with the school's stated values) and the two that compare teaching across the day. From the interview, the
ratings of how fully the headteacher answered each open question, the
checklist of named behaviour systems and the statement on parental
engagement are left out, and the interview gives no espoused measure of
teaching. The reasons are given below and in full in \cref{app:2A:guide}.

To make the construction concrete, consider the in-lesson strictness
sub-score of a single school. In each of five lessons, two researchers rated
the level of misbehaviour tolerated before a sanction, the frequency of
disruption, the pupils' response to correction, how respectfully they treated
the teacher, and how well they concentrated. Misbehaviour and disruption are
reverse-scored so that a higher value means a stricter lesson. One item
needs a rule. If no pupil was sanctioned during a lesson there is no response
to correction to rate, and the researcher records a zero. That zero is not a
low score, and dropping it would throw away what the lesson shows. It is
therefore replaced by the reverse of the disruption rating, which means that
a lesson with no disruption and no sanction is scored as if the pupils had
responded fully, and a lesson with constant disruption and no sanction as if
they had not responded at all. The five ratings a researcher makes in a
lesson are averaged to give that researcher's value for the lesson, the
lesson values are averaged
across lessons, and the researchers' values are averaged across researchers,
giving one in-lesson strictness figure on the 1--5 scale. That figure is
averaged with the out-of-lesson strictness figure and doubled to give the
school's enacted strictness on the 0--10 scale. Espoused strictness is
simpler: the headteacher's ratings on the four strictness statements are
averaged and doubled. Every school that received a visit has an enacted
score, every school that gave an interview has an espoused score, and
\NTierOne{} schools have both. \Cref{tab:score_dist} reports the distribution
of every sub-score and score across the visited schools.

\input{tables/tab_score_dist}

There are two larger exclusions from the interview. The first is the ratings of how fully the headteacher answered each open question, which measure how articulate the head is rather than the culture of the school. They load
on one general factor which tracks the interviewer's rating of the head's confidence and willingness at $r = 0.34$ to $0.48$, and they are uncorrelated
with the observed scores. The second is the count of the named behaviour systems (S3 in the tables), which counts eight of the ten and excludes the two reward systems. It correlates with observed strictness at $-0.01$, compared with $0.41$ for the
statement battery. In other words, the systems a school names are not a
guide to how strictly it runs. Both are kept in the data as variables in
their own right.

Agreement between researchers is moderate at the level of a single item, and
the school-level scores built from many items, lessons and raters are
reliable enough to use, as I show below. Of the 517 lessons observed,
\LessonsTwoRaters{} were rated independently by two researchers and
\LessonsThreeRaters{} by three; in the remaining \LessonsOneRater{} only one
researcher was available for that lesson, and it carries one rating. The
outside-of-lessons form was completed by two or more researchers for every one of the \NTierOne{} visits, though individual items
were left blank when not observed, so the item-level comparisons below
rest on fewer days than that. Where two raters saw the
same lesson, the pairwise Pearson correlations across the classroom items
range from $0.53$ to $0.79$; on the outside-of-lessons form, completed once
per day, they range from $0.47$ to $0.81$; and the two raters are within
one point of each other close to 90 per cent of the time
(\cref{tab:irr_classroom,tab:irr_outside}). Agreement is highest on the
items with the most concrete anchors, namely the consistency of the reward
system ($0.81$, a day item) and the frequency of disruption ($0.79$), and
lowest among the classroom items on the teaching-practice ratings that ask
the observer for the most interpretation, such as the use of resources and
the choice of methods ($0.53$ and $0.55$). The interview items were rated
by the interviewer and the observer, with correlations of $0.61$ to $0.74$
and within-one-point agreement at or above 90 per cent
(\cref{tab:irr_interview}). These interview
figures concern the open questions, which enter no score; the espoused scores of record come from the statement battery, rated by the headteacher alone. The espoused scales, made up of three and four statements, have internal consistencies of \AlphaWThree{} and \AlphaSFour{}.

However, the paper's claims do not rest on the agreement of two raters on
one lesson. They rest on the reliability of a school's score once it has
been averaged over its lessons and raters. \Cref{tab:score_reliability} gives this. Take every rating that a
researcher gave a lesson for in-lesson strictness: one number, for one
lesson, from one researcher. Those ratings differ from one another for three
reasons. The first is that the lesson was in a stricter or a less strict
school, which is the difference the measure is meant to capture. The second
is that, within the same school, that lesson was calmer or rowdier than the
school's other lessons, because it had a different teacher, a different
class or a different period of the day. The third is that, looking at the
same lesson, one researcher scored it a little higher than the other. The
table divides the spread of the ratings into those three shares. For
in-lesson strictness the shares are \ShareSchoolS{} per cent school,
\ShareLessonS{} per cent lesson and \ShareRaterS{} per cent rater. This means
that well over half of the variation in single-lesson strictness ratings is
lessons within the same school differing from one another, only about a
fifth is schools differing from each other, and under a fifth is the two
researchers disagreeing. For in-lesson warmth the shares are
\ShareSchoolW{}, \ShareLessonW{} and \ShareRaterW{} per cent. Lessons within
a school vary less in warmth than in strictness, but the researchers
disagree more about warmth, which matches the item-level pattern described
above.

\input{tables/tab_score_reliability}

This decomposition has two implications. The first is that
averaging matters. A single lesson is a poor guide to its school, but when
five lessons and the ratings of the researchers who saw them are averaged,
the lesson-to-lesson and rater-to-rater variation is greatly reduced. The reliability of the in-lesson warmth score, averaged over a school's own lessons and raters, is \RelWLessons{}, and that of the in-lesson strictness score is \RelSLessons{}. The
outside-of-lessons sub-scores, which summarise a whole day, are more
reliable (\RelWOutside{} and \RelSOutside{}). The in-lesson and out-of-lesson halves of
each enacted score, which were rated in different settings, are correlated
at \HalvesW{} for warmth and \HalvesS{} for strictness across the \NHalves{} schools. This puts the reliability of each combined enacted score at about \RelEnactedW{} for warmth and \RelEnactedS{} for strictness. The components are computed before rounding. The ceiling on any correlation with the enacted warmth score is the square root of its reliability, $\sqrt{0.69} \approx 0.83$. These
figures treat the two settings as parallel forms of one measure; treating
them instead as distinct facets of the construct gives \RelEnactedWUpper{}
and \RelEnactedSUpper{}. The corrections below use the lower figures, so
the corrected correlations are upper bounds. Each school was visited once, so day-to-day variation within a school
cannot be separated from the school itself: these are reliabilities of the
school as seen on its visit day.

The lessons that carry only one rating cost little here. Had every lesson been rated twice, the in-lesson
reliabilities would have been \RelWOneTwoRaters{} and \RelSOneTwoRaters{}
rather than \RelWLessons{} and \RelSLessons{}, because the noise that averaging has to
remove is mostly between lessons and not between raters. They do, however,
carry one caution. Single-rated lessons were rated lower on average than double-rated ones, by \SingleRatedGapW{} on warmth and \SingleRatedGapS{} on strictness on the 1--5 scale. The \SchoolsWithSingleRated{} schools with such lessons have slightly lower scores as a result: the share of a school's single-rated lessons correlates with its in-lesson warmth at $r = \CorrSingleShareW{}$. The following chapter therefore checks that its
estimates do not depend on those schools.

The second implication concerns schools themselves rather than the
measurement. Behaviour varies more from classroom to classroom within a
school than it does between schools. This means that a school's enacted
score is an average over quite heterogeneous lessons, that a visit of five
lessons measures that average with appreciable error, and that every
correlation with the enacted scores reported below is attenuated as a
result. The attenuation is not large enough to change any conclusion. The
enacted--espoused correlations, corrected for the unreliability of both
sides, rise to about \DisSplitW{} for warmth and \DisSplitS{} for
strictness, which is still far short of what two readings of the same quantity would give. The marking-scheme strictness correlation of $\RubricSOfstedRaw$ reported below, corrected for the enacted score's unreliability alone, would be about $0.36$. I show these corrections to give a sense of
scale; the uncorrected figures are the ones reported throughout.

The correlation between the two enacted scores carries the production process inside it. Enacted warmth and strictness are correlated at $r = \CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$
across the \NTierOne{} visited schools, and the espoused pair at
$\CorrWarmthStrictnessEspoused$ across the same schools. The enacted figure reflects that process because the same people produced both scores on the same day. The double-rated lessons allow that
halo to be measured rather than assumed. Within a lesson, one observer's
warmth rating correlates with the other observer's strictness rating at
\CorrWSCrossObs{}, against \CorrWSSameObs{} when both ratings come from
the same observer, so the individual halo is small. Correcting the cross-observer figure for measurement error puts the correlation between
the two traits at about \CorrWSTrue{}. The gap between \CorrWSTrue{} and
$\CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$ reflects the aggregation to school level
and the shared occasion, since both observers formed their impressions at
the same school on the same day. The two dimensions are therefore distinct
constructs whose true correlation is materially lower than the scored
figure, and the scored figure should not be read as a fact about how
strongly warmth and strictness go together in schools.

The gold standard is two measures rather than one because the interview and the visit disagree about the same school. Two measures that share about a sixteenth of their variance (warmth) or about a sixth (strictness) are not two fallible readings of the same quantity. For strictness, where the corrected correlation reaches \DisSplitS{}, the statement is one of degree. A weighted average is the right thing to do with two
noisy measures of a common target, because their errors partly cancel out.
It is the wrong thing to do with two measures of different targets, because
it produces a third quantity that is neither of them and whose composition
depends on the weight chosen. I therefore carry the two measures separately
throughout, and their divergence is itself a result, reported with the results. Two schools show what the divergence looks
like in the ratings. At the first, the headteacher rated the school 8.0 on
warmth and 8.0 on strictness, and the observers, across their lessons and
the day, recorded 7.9 and 8.5; the in-lesson and out-of-lesson sub-scores
agree with one another to within half a point. At the second, the
headteacher rated warmth 9.3 and strictness 7.8, near the top of the scale,
while the observers recorded 4.6 and 5.9. In-lesson warmth at that school
averaged 2.2 out of 5 and out-of-lesson warmth 2.4, while the head's ratings
on the three warmth statements averaged 4.7 out of 5. There is nothing in
the interview that would tell these two heads apart. Across the \NTierOne{} schools, four
in five have a warmth gap larger than one point on the 0--10 scale and half
have a gap larger than two.

One espoused measure can be checked against an outside record. Heads rated how low their staff turnover was and how high
morale was, and the staff-climate score built from those two statements is
correlated with the school's actual teacher turnover, as recorded by the
Department for Education, at $r = -0.52$, and at $-0.48$ once the other
espoused scores, disadvantage and school size are held constant. On the one
question where a head's answer can be checked against the record, heads
report accurately. This check concerns staff turnover, though, and says nothing about the warmth statements.

\subsection{Reading the text with prediction models}
\label{sec:p1_prediction}

The first way of reading the documents uses no theory of culture at all. It asks only whether the words a document uses tend to go with the culture observers found in the school.

The method works as follows. Each document is turned into a list of counts:
how often each word, and each two-word phrase, appears in it. Phrases that
appear in almost every document count for little; phrases that distinguish
one document from another count for more. A statistical model is then
fitted on the visited schools. It learns which phrases tend to appear in
the reports of schools observed to be warm, and which in the reports of
schools observed to be cold, and it attaches a small positive or negative
weight to each. To score a new school, the model adds up the weights of the
phrases in its document. No single phrase decides anything: a school's
score is thousands of tiny nudges summed. (For the technical reader: the
representation is TF--IDF over words and bigrams, and the model is a ridge
regression; \cref{sec:p1_app_prediction} gives the specification.)

Three safeguards hold the evaluation to the criterion.

First, it never grades its own homework. Every school's reported prediction
comes from a model fitted with that school left out, so each number says
how well the model reads a school it has never seen. As a further check, I
re-ran the whole procedure two hundred times on deliberately scrambled
scores; a real result has to beat what scrambling produces, and the
reported significance levels come from that comparison.

Second, it cannot recognise the school. Before anything is counted, school
and trust names, place names, dates, page numbers and standard boilerplate
are removed from every document. Without this step, the model learns to spot which school or which era of report it is reading, rather than
what the report says. With it, the weights fall on ordinary prose.

Third, it gets no credit for reading demographics. A school's intake
statistics predict part of its culture score, and a document often reveals
the intake. So each observed score is first split into the part that
intake statistics explain and the part they do not, and the model is asked
to predict only the second part. A model that merely recognised a
disadvantaged school from its report would score nothing. Results are
shown with and without this adjustment.

I also tried more elaborate ways of representing the text, and none beat simple counting: longer phrases, topic groupings, and meaning-vectors from a neural network that scores passages as similar when they say similar things in different words. \cref{sec:p1_app_prediction} records them. The counting representation is the one reported throughout.

\subsection{Reading the text with marking schemes}
\label{sec:p1_rubrics}

The second way of reading the documents states what to look for. For each
source and dimension I wrote a marking scheme of one page: a sentence
saying what is being measured, five numbered bands, and two or three
tie-break rules. To give the flavour, the inspection-report warmth scheme
sets band~3, the default, as ``a safe and generally positive school,
described through labels, provision or outcomes, without describing how
staff and pupils actually relate'', and band~4 as ``the report describes
the relationships themselves''. All seven schemes are printed in full in \cref{sec:p1_app_rubrics}.

The schemes and the visit instrument are compared cell by cell in the results, and one deliberate asymmetry between them matters for that comparison. The visit instrument counts consistently used rewards as warmth, because a reward given in person is a relational act: an adult noticing and affirming a child. The policy scheme excludes reward machinery from warmth, because a document describing a reward economy is provision on paper rather than a relationship observed. Consistent sanctions, by contrast, count as strictness in both instruments, because consistency of enforcement is what makes conduct rules real, whether watched or written.

A language model reads each document against the scheme and returns a band from 1 to 5, together with one quoted sentence as evidence (the models and their pinned versions are given in \cref{sec:p1_app_rubrics}). The same fixed model reads every document.

I froze the schemes in a version-controlled file (committed 24 August 2026) before any document was scored, and applied them in a single run. I revised nothing after seeing any result against the criterion, and no version of the schemes was tested against the enacted scores before the frozen run, so the reported numbers are first attempts. The schemes' wording drew on the project's earlier reading of these documents. An earlier, since-retired scoring design had read inspection-report strictness at about $0.40$. The freeze therefore protects the schemes' wording; the choice of what to attempt was informed by results.

Applying marking schemes by machine and validating them against human benchmarks is an established workflow in the social sciences, with economics adopting it quickly \citep{eloundou2024,gilardi2023}. Here the benchmark is what observers recorded in the school rather than agreement with labels on the same text.

Three checks accompany the scores, and their outcomes are reported with the results. The first is a retest: thirty documents per source were scored twice, and the agreement between the two runs is reported cell by cell. The second is a change of language model: the whole exercise was repeated with a second, larger language model. Its role is fixed in advance by one rule: it never sets a headline number. However, where the two routes to a dimension tie in point estimate, the route whose reading survives the change of language model is preferred.

The third is a length check. Because a model reading a longer document has more evidence to cite, the correlation between band and document length is computed for every cell, and each scheme correlation is computed again with document length partialled out.

\subsection{Judging both methods}
\label{sec:p1_judging}

Both readings face the criterion: correlation with what trained observers recorded in the school. Neither is judged by how plausible its
output looks or how well it agrees with anything else. Setting the two
side by side, for each source and each dimension, answers two questions at
once: how much information about culture the public record contains, and
whether extracting it requires a stated theory or only statistics. One
further rule of evidence is applied throughout: if some feature of the text appears to help predict every dimension at once, it is treated as reading the document rather than the school, and discarded. Document age, format and length all failed this test, and so did several apparent improvements (\cref{sec:p1_app_prediction}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:p1_results}

The results come in three parts: what direct measurement shows on its own, what the public record carries and which method extracts it, and two checks from outside the project's own data.

\subsection{What direct measurement shows}

A school can be warm without being orderly, and orderly without being warm, and the visited sample contains both kinds (\cref{fig:p1_quadrants}). Warmth and strictness are related but distinct: once observer effects and measurement noise are removed, the two correlate at \CorrWSTrue{}. The enacted scores are reliable enough to use, and their limits are measured rather than assumed; \cref{tab:score_reliability} reports the reliabilities.

  dot is a school. Schools are found in all four quadrants: warm-and-strict, warm-and-permissive, cold-and-strict, and cold-and-permissive. The evidence that the dimensions are distinct is the cross-observer trait correlation of about $0.36$ (Section 3) rather than the scatter's spread, which noise alone would produce.}

What heads say and what observers see are different measures. The interview and the visit are correlated at only $\GoldWarmthSplit$ for warmth ($p = \GoldWarmthSplitP$) and $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ for strictness ($p \GoldStrictnessSplitP$), on the \NTierOne{} schools with both. The clearest way to see
the gap: of the \TopQN{} schools whose heads placed them in the top
quarter for warmth, only \TopQWMatch{} were in the top quarter as
observed. Chance alone would produce six or seven matches; if heads and
observers were measuring the same thing, the reliability of the
instruments would allow about \TopQExpectedSameW{}. Breaking ties on the coarse espoused scale the other way gives 9 rather than \TopQWMatch{}, so the conclusion does not depend on the tie rule. For strictness the match is \TopQSMatch{} of \TopQN{}, close to the ceiling of about \TopQExpectedSameS{}. In other words, heads place their school's strictness almost as accurately as the measures allow. Their warmth placements are far weaker: the disattenuated correlation is $0.42$, and top-quartile agreement sits near chance.

The grid (\cref{tab:p1_grid}) has a simple shape. Inspection reports are the only documents
that carry information about what a school is actually like, and the two

The three inspection-report model cells all clear the scrambled-score benchmark (warmth \PermPOfstedW{}, teaching \PermPOfstedT{}, strictness \PermPOfstedS{}), and every own-voice cell fails it. After correction for the fourteen tests in the grid (seven source--dimension cells, each read by both methods), four cells survive at $q < 0.05$: the model's warmth and teaching readings and the scheme's strictness and teaching readings (the strictness scheme at $q = \QGridSOfsted$). $\PredTOfsted$; for both, the correlation is essentially unchanged, or
higher, when everything demographics could explain is removed. No prediction model reads observed strictness well. The best attempt reaches $\PredSOfsted$, which clears the scrambled-score benchmark only narrowly (\PermPOfstedS{}), and its confidence interval touches zero ($\CIPredSOfsted$). The weakness repeated across every word-count representation tried. The appendix records one embedding model that reads strictness more strongly, but the result does not replicate across encoders.

The marking scheme reverses the pattern in point estimate, though the difference between the two methods' strictness readings is within sampling noise at this sample size ($z = \StrictDiffZ$, $p = \StrictDiffP$). What distinguishes the scheme is that its reading survives the three checks set out in the measurement section. On the retest, the two runs give the same band \GridRetestExact{} of the time, with the inspection-report and policy strictness schemes at 100\%, the website scheme at 97\%, and one weak cell, the policy warmth scheme, at 83\%. Under the change of language model, the second model's strictness scheme reads the enacted scores at $\RubricSFourO$, close to the primary model's $\RubricSOfstedRaw$, while its warmth and teaching schemes read essentially nothing ($\RubricWFourO$, $\RubricTFourO$). On the length check, the band--length correlations are $\LenOfstedS$ and $\LenOfstedW$ for the inspection-report schemes, and partialling length out moves the inspection strictness reading only from $+0.25$ to $\RubricSOfstedLenP$, so length does not carry the result. The website schemes sit at $\LenWebS$ and $\LenWebW$ on length; partialled, their cells move to $\RubricWWebLenP$ and $\RubricSWebLenP$ and remain qualified. The scheme's strictness reading also survives demographic adjustment ($\RubricSOfstedDec$). The model route offers no comparable stability, since its primary reading is marginal.

The one-page strictness scheme scores $\RubricSOfstedRaw$ raw and $\RubricSOfstedDec$ with demographics removed. The warmth scheme fails entirely: it reads the reports at $\RubricWOfstedDec$ and puts three-quarters of schools in one band. The model reads the same reports at $\PredWOfsted$, and its advantage is itself significant (paired test, $p = \WarmDiffP$).

The two dimensions are therefore written down differently. Warmth shows
in a report's everyday register, spread across many small choices of
phrase, which a model with no instructions can learn but a marking scheme
cannot pin down. Strictness shows in specific, checkable claims, for example whether disruption is rare or whether rules are applied consistently, which have to be asked for, and which the prediction model cannot distinguish from their negations. Teaching, carried through the analysis as the rival explanation for the next chapter, sits in between and is read about equally well by both routes in the primary run. However, only the model's reading survives the change of language model (the second model's teaching scheme reads $\RubricTFourO$), so teaching stays with the prediction model.

Behaviour policies and websites show no signal of enacted culture distinguishable from zero at this sample size, by either method: the
prediction models find at most $\OwnVoiceMax$ in any cell, and the
marking schemes find less. Where a scheme did fail to spread schools across bands, the model column carries the evidence for that cell. Both policy schemes concentrated nearly all schools in one band in this way. The two methods never disagree about a source.

\subsection{What the models read}

The models read partly the constructs and partly the tone of a good school, and the split can be measured. Every sentence in these
reports carries an independent topic tag (whether it is about
relationships, behaviour, teaching, or something else), so the models'
strongest sentences can be checked for topic. Of the hundred sentences
the warmth model scores highest, \WarmTopShare{} are about staff--pupil
relationships, against a base rate of \WarmBaseShare{} across all
sentences: the model seeks out relational content at well above chance.
For teaching the corresponding shares are \TeachTopShare{} against \TeachBaseShare{}. There is no enrichment, because teaching is what inspection reports mostly discuss anyway, and the teaching model reads
a broad register rather than one topic. Therefore part of each model's signal is construct-specific language and part is the general register of a school doing well. The deconfounding step removes the part that the four adjustment variables (three intake measures and report age) explain linearly. However, the scores should still be understood as reading the report's whole account of the school, weighted toward the construct.

\Cref{tab:p1_examples} shows example sentences under stated selection
rules, and the two models illustrate differently, in a way that echoes the chapter's central contrast. For teaching, the strongest sentences are about pedagogy: choosing core knowledge, embedding learning in long-term memory, pupils focusing in lessons. However, some of the strongest are criticisms in that vocabulary, because the model counts the discourse without registering negation (the appendix discusses this).
For warmth, the evidence is more diffuse. The clearest single sentences are the negative ones. ``Many pupils feel confident they have a `trusted adult' they can go to'' is among the strongest score-lowering sentences in the corpus, and the pastoral-provision formulas sit beside it. In other words, reassurance boilerplate marks the colder school. The reading is less strange than it first seems, because inspectors reach for these assurances when there is provision to certify but no relationships to describe. On the positive side no single sentence carries much weight:
the model's warmth evidence is spread across many small choices of
phrase (attentive verbs, names used, care described in passing), and its strongest whole sentences read as
engaged-community tone rather than tenderness. The diffuseness reflects the construct rather than the display. Warmth leaves no single sentence for a rule to find, so the model's validity rests on the held-out correlations and the topic statistics rather than on any quotable line. \Cref{sec:p1_app_examples} also lists the model's leader-to-staff warmth sentences: it credits schools whose
leaders listen to their staff, an interpretable part of the signal, though one step removed from the classroom.

\subsection{The school's own voice}

The documents a school writes about itself reveal what its head says, not
what the school does. Read against the interview rather than the visit, on the roughly 300 interviewed schools, models fitted on the policy and website text predict the head's private account of strictness at $\PredEspS$ (demographics removed) and of staff morale at $\PredEspClim$, but of warmth at $\PredEspW$, not significant at the 5 per cent level:
public warmth language shows no detectable relation even to what the same school's head says in private. The espoused warmth scale is coarse and compressed, however, so only a weak relation can be excluded. The inspection report's apparent knowledge of espoused strictness is substantially reduced, though not eliminated, once observed strictness is taken into account, falling from $\CrossWireRaw$ to $\CrossWireNet$. Reports track what schools do, and their relation to what heads say comes mostly from the overlap between the two. Each kind of document mainly reflects its own side of the espoused--enacted divide.

\subsection{A check against parents}
\label{sec:p1_pv}

The last check comes from parents, through the parent-reported warmth measure described with the data, with the caveats on response and staleness given there.

The main test uses the prediction model at national scale. For the \PVPredWarmthN{} schools that played no part in fitting the warmth model, the model's predicted warmth, computed from each school's inspection report alone, correlates with parent-reported warmth at $\PVPredWarmth$ (\cref{fig:p1_pv}). Because none of these schools was used to build the model, nothing about the correlation can be an artefact of fitting.

On the visited schools the parent measure behaves less cleanly than in that national check: it correlates positively but imprecisely with observed warmth ($+0.17$, $p = 0.08$, $n = 101$) and considerably more strongly with the head's espoused warmth ($+0.43$). Parent satisfaction shares a register of positivity with headteacher self-description that direct observation does not, so the survey is not an arbiter between voice and observation. What it can do is corroborate the national text signal.

A related check asks whether the model is merely reading the inspection grade, the free one-number summary every school already has. On the 99 schools with a pre-COVID grade, the held-out warmth correlation is $+0.32$, against $+0.33$ on the full 100. Partialling the pre-COVID grade out of the validation moves it only to $+0.29$, and teaching behaves the same way, so the model is not reading back the school's old grade. Partialling out the grade of the very report the text comes from halves the warmth correlation, to $+0.16$. However, that removes the outcome of the same inspection event, prose and verdict together, so it is an over-correction. The national Parent View correlation falls to about half strength when the grade is partialled out, from $+0.29$ to $+0.14$. For comparison, the grade itself correlates with observed warmth on the visited schools at only $-0.20$ (grades run from 1, Outstanding, to 4, so a negative sign means better-graded schools are warmer).

I tested one caveat. Inspectors consult Parent View during inspections, and about 3 per cent of reports cite the survey, so the report text is not automatically independent of the parents' answers. However, removing every survey-referencing sentence and re-running the whole prediction leaves the correlation unchanged at $+0.29$.

The two measures also share no machinery and no text. Parents answer from their child's daily experience; the model reads an inspector's prose. Agreement between two such different sources is encouraging, though both respond in part to general school quality, so the agreement is corroboration rather than proof of construct. Schools
whose inspection reports read warmer are schools whose parents report
happier, safer children.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:p1_conclusion}

Warmth and strictness can both be measured. The chapter's central
lesson is that the two dimensions need different instruments, and that
the pattern of which instrument works where is itself informative
about what each dimension is.

Consider warmth first. In person, it is the harder dimension to
observe reliably: it takes more lessons and more settings to pin down
than strictness does. In public text, it can be read only statistically. A prediction model finds it spread thinly across an inspection report's everyday wording, in whether the inspector writes about adults attending to pupils or falls back on stock assurances. A written marking scheme, by contrast, cannot see it at all, because no single sentence carries it.

Strictness reverses the pattern. In person it is easy to observe
reliably. In text, prediction models read it only weakly, and the reading does not survive a change of encoder, because it lives in specific claims, such as whether disruption is rare and whether rules are enforced consistently, and word patterns cannot distinguish such claims from their opposites. However, because it lives in claims, the marking scheme recovers it.

The documents schools write about themselves track what heads say about strictness and staff morale. However, they carry no warmth signal distinguishable from zero at this sample size, either against the observers or against the head's private account, though the espoused scale's compression means only a weak relation is excluded.

Parents supply the one outside register. At national scale their reports of happy, safe children agree with the model. On the visited schools, however, they track the head's account more closely than the observers', so they corroborate the text signal without deciding between voice and observation.

The practical product is two national measures, each validated against direct observation: a prediction model for warmth
(and teaching), and a one-page marking scheme for strictness. These become the explanatory variables of the next chapter.

There are six limitations. Each school was visited once, so a school's
day may not be its year. The visit instrument's own reliability caps
every correlation reported here well below one. The marking schemes
were scored in a single frozen run, so their figures are first attempts rather than tuned bests. That protects credibility at some cost in polish. And inspection reports are written in different years under different frameworks, a source of noise that cleaning reduces but cannot remove. The marking schemes were only ever applied by language models, so there is no check that a human reader would apply them the same way; the retest and the second model are the only tests of that. The warmth result also depends on how the inspection grade is handled. Removing the pre-COVID grade from the validation leaves the correlation at $+0.29$. Removing the grade of the same report that provided the text lowers it to $+0.16$, because that takes out the inspection's verdict as well as its prose.

\section{Scoring Guide and Item Definitions}
\label{app:2A:guide}

This section sets out how the individual items on the forms combine into
the nine sub-scores and the two sets of scores. The items themselves, that
is, the question wording, the response format and the anchor description
attached to each rating level, are reproduced in full in
\cref{app:2A:interview_guide} for the interview and in
\cref{app:2A:visit_protocol} for the two observation forms.

Every item is recorded on a 1--5 scale. Two observation items can also take
a zero, with a distinct meaning in each case. A zero on \emph{response of
pupils to sanctions} records that no sanction was issued during the lesson,
and a zero on \emph{effectiveness of pupil discussion} records that no
discussion was initiated. For the reasons given in the chapter, the first of these is not a genuine low score. It is replaced by $6 - \text{disruption}$, which gives a calm unsanctioned lesson a high value
and a disrupted unsanctioned lesson a low one. The second is treated as a
genuine negative, since a lesson containing no discussion did not achieve
effective discussion.

Observation item scores are averaged across lessons and across researchers
to give one value per school before the sub-scores are formed.
\Cref{tab:p1_subscore_items} lists the constituent items of each sub-score.

\paragraph{Items recorded but not scored.} Every item on the lesson
observation form enters a sub-score. Four items on the outside-of-lessons
form do not. Two of them, the consistency of verbal feedback and the quality
of differentiation across lessons, are recorded as cross-lesson summaries of
the corresponding lesson observation items, and so they enter no sub-score
of their own, because the lesson items already capture them. The other two,
the condition of wall and corridor displays and the extent to which displays
align with the school's stated values, are ratings of the building rather
than of conduct or relationships, and they fit neither dimension as it is
defined. Both are retained in the data.

From the interview, three components are recorded but not scored. The first is the set of ratings of how fully the headteacher answered each open question (Q5 to Q8, Q11 and Q12). As the chapter reports, these measure how articulate the head is rather than how the school runs, and they enter no score. The second is the checklist of ten named behaviour systems, counted as S3. Its near-zero correlation with observed strictness, reported in the chapter, is why it enters no espoused score. Of the ten systems, S3 counts the eight that
represent behavioural or academic requirements and omits the two reward
systems (reward assemblies and reward points), whose presence indicates a
warmer rather than a stricter regime, and it is the count of those eight
rescaled to 0--5. The third is the tenth statement, on parental engagement,
which belongs to neither dimension. Two further statements, on staff turnover
and morale, are scored but form the staff-climate score SC rather than a culture score. The remaining
questions (Q1 to Q4, Q9 and Q10, which cover length of tenure, the
description of the school's culture and how it is communicated, the
characteristics of an effective school, the staff structure and
accountability) were asked for context, and their ratings, where they were
taken, enter no score.

Every other sub-score is the mean of its items, computed over the items
present so that a missing item reduces the number of contributing items
rather than the school's score.

The sub-scores combine into two sets of scores, grouped by source, with
observed behaviour and stated philosophy kept apart:
%
\begin{align}
\text{Enacted:}\quad
  W^{\text{enac}} &= \overline{(W1, W2)}, &
  S^{\text{enac}} &= \overline{(S1, S2)}, &
  T^{\text{enac}} &= T1 \label{eq:enacted} \\
\text{Espoused:}\quad
  W^{\text{esp}}  &= W3, &
  S^{\text{esp}}  &= S4, &
  \mathrm{SC} &= \overline{(\text{SC}_{1}, \text{SC}_{2})} \label{eq:espoused}
\end{align}
%
There is no espoused teaching score. The two interview statements under the
teaching heading concern staff turnover and morale, and they form the staff
climate score SC instead. The systems count $S3$ enters no espoused score.

Schools with an interview but no visit have the espoused set of scores alone
(every visited school was also interviewed). No imputation is attempted across the two sets: as the chapter reports, the espoused and enacted scores are too weakly correlated for either to stand in for the other. All
scores are finally doubled from the 1--5 item scale to the 0--10 range.

The mapping in \cref{tab:p1_subscore_items} is generated directly from the
scoring code, so it cannot drift from what the scoring code does.

\section{Prediction Model Specification}
\label{sec:p1_app_prediction}

The text representation is TF--IDF over words and two-word phrases
(English stop words removed; terms kept if they appear in at least five
documents and at most ninety per cent of them; sublinear term frequency;
each document's vector normalised to unit length). The model is a ridge
regression with an unpenalised intercept, estimated exactly in kernel
form. Every reported correlation is leave-one-out: the model is refitted
with each school excluded, and the penalty is re-chosen within every fold
by closed-form inner leave-one-out, so the held-out school takes no part
in any fitting decision. Predictions are clipped to the range of the
training scores. Significance comes from a permutation test: the identical
pipeline is run on two hundred permutations of the outcome, and the
reported $p$ is the share of permutations matching or beating the real
correlation.

Before modelling, documents are cleaned of identifying and clerical
material: establishment, trust and local-authority names (from the
national register), month names and years, postcodes, tokens containing
digits, inspection-report page furniture, letter salutations, and
framework boilerplate. The deconfounded specification regresses each
outcome on the school's free-school-meals share, share of pupils with
English as an additional language, prior attainment and report age, and
takes the residual as the target.

Alternative representations were evaluated in the identical harness and none improved on the specification above. In brief: phrases of three and four words changed no correlation by more than noise. Topic groupings
performed worse than counting everywhere. Passage embeddings from two
pretrained encoders read enacted warmth and teaching at essentially
zero (the signal is vocabulary rather than paraphrasable meaning) while confirming the espoused-strictness result. One cell disagrees between encoders. The larger encoder reads enacted strictness at $+0.33$ with mean pooling, where the smaller encoder reads nothing and no word-based model finds it. As a single-encoder result failing the consistency rule, it is recorded here as unresolved rather than used.
Sentence-level annotations (topic and polarity tags) carried strictly
less information than the raw wording, and counts signed by sentence
polarity passed the correlation test but failed the negative control:
their gain loaded on clerical vocabulary and lifted every dimension at
once.

\section{What the Models Read: Examples}
\label{sec:p1_app_examples}

This appendix supports the validity of the warmth and teaching prediction
models by showing their evidence in readable units. The models work on
weighted word and phrase counts, but a sentence's score is simply the sum
of its phrases' weights, so every sentence in the corpus can be ranked by
how strongly it pushes a school's score up or down. The listings below
give the strongest sentences in each direction for each model, followed by the strongest longer phrases (\cref{tab:p1_phrases}). The pattern is consistent: sentences
describing adults attending to pupils, and teaching designed and checked,
raise the scores; stock assurances and provision boilerplate lower them.
One oddity in the teaching listing should be read with care: several of
the score-raising sentences are criticisms (``pupils are not remembering
knowledge deeply''). The model counts the vocabulary of the
memory-and-retrieval discourse without registering negation, and a report only discusses long-term memory at all, approvingly or not, when the school works in that idiom. This blindness to negation is the same property that makes the model fail on strictness, where the negations carry the signal.
