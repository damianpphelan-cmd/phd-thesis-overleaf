\begin{chapterabstract}
School culture is widely held to matter for what pupils learn, but it is
rarely measured. This paper constructs measures of two dimensions of the
culture of English secondary schools, warmth and strictness, and asks how far
each can be recovered from data that exist for every school. For \NTierOne{}
schools a research visit and a headteacher interview give a gold standard in
two parts: an enacted score from what observers saw, and an espoused score
from what the head reported. For every school in the country, inspection
reports, behaviour policies and websites are scored by language-model
instruments, and models fitted on the visited schools predict the gold
standard from those text scores. Three findings follow. Strictness is legible
in public text and warmth is not: inspection-report strictness is correlated
with observed strictness at $r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$ and a text-only model
predicts it out of sample at $\RidgeBStrictness$, while no text source is
correlated with observed warmth. Espoused and enacted culture diverge, and
more for warmth: headteachers rate their schools \GapWarmth{} points warmer on
a ten-point scale than observers record and \GapStrictness{} points stricter,
and every public source tracks what heads say about warmth rather than what
observers see. And the instruments themselves teach a lesson: how closely a
language-model scorer agrees with the labels it was tuned on says nothing
about whether its scores track what happens in the school.
\end{chapterabstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:p1_intro}

School culture is widely held to matter for what pupils learn, but it is
rarely measured. This paper constructs measures of two dimensions of the
culture of English secondary schools, warmth and strictness, for the full
population of around 3,330 state secondary schools, and asks how far each
dimension can be recovered from data that exist for every school.

Warmth is the relational quality of a school: whether pupils are known,
cared for and supported by the adults around them. In a lesson it shows in
whether the teacher uses pupils' names, how praise is given and how the
teacher and the class speak to one another; between lessons it shows in how
staff and pupils deal with each other in corridors and at break, and in
whether rewards are used consistently. Strictness is the school's behavioural
structure: whether expectations of conduct are clear, enforced and met. In a
lesson it shows in how much misbehaviour is tolerated before a sanction, how
much disruption occurs and how pupils respond when they are corrected; outside
lessons it shows in how pupils arrive, how they move between lessons and how
the dining hall runs. Strictness here means clear rules applied consistently,
not harsh punishment. A school whose sanctions are applied by every member of
staff in the same way is strict in this sense. Whether it is also warm is a
separate question.

The two dimensions are the axes of the parenting typology of
\citet{baumrind1971} and \citet{maccoby1983}, which classifies parenting by
demandingness and responsiveness and finds that the combination of both, the
authoritative style, produces the best child outcomes across cognitive and
non-cognitive domains; \citet{majumder2015} and \citet{zhang2020} find the
same pattern in educational attainment, with each dimension carrying an
independent effect. The transfer to schools is direct: demandingness is
strictness and responsiveness is warmth. \citet{pellerin2005} and
\citet{gregory2010,gregory2011} apply the typology to schools and find that
pupils in schools combining support with structure report less victimisation
and more engagement, and \citet{gottfredson2005} reviews the related evidence
on school discipline. What makes the two dimensions measurable in a school,
as opposed to a family, is that both are enacted in public. A researcher in a
lesson can see whether names are used and how disruption is met, and a
researcher in a corridor can see whether pupils move calmly and whether staff
speak to them; neither requires access to anything private. Warmth and
strictness are treated here as distinct dimensions rather than the ends of one
axis, because a school can be warm without being orderly and orderly without
being warm, and whether the two co-vary is a question the data answer rather
than a premise. A third dimension, the quality of teaching practice, is
observed alongside them; it is not part of culture as defined here but is the
obvious rival explanation for any association between culture and results,
and it is carried for that reason.

The difficulty is that none of this is recorded in any administrative
dataset. What is recorded, for every school, is text: an inspection report
written by an external observer, a behaviour policy written by the school, and
a website written by the school for parents. What is recorded for a smaller
set of schools, because it was collected for this study, is direct observation
and a structured interview with the headteacher. The design follows from that
asymmetry. For \NTierOne{} schools there is a research visit, comprising
structured lesson and out-of-lesson observations, and a headteacher interview;
from these a gold-standard measure of each dimension is constructed. Because
the interview and the visit disagree substantially about the same school, the
gold standard is two measures, not one: an \emph{enacted} score, from what an
observer recorded, and an \emph{espoused} score, from what the headteacher
reported. For a further \NTierTwo{} schools there is the interview alone, and
for the remaining national population only the public text. For those schools
the gold standard is estimated from what is observable, using prediction
models fitted on the schools where both the predictors and the gold standard
exist, and the paper reports how well the predictions reproduce the gold
standard out of sample.

Three findings follow. First, the two dimensions are recoverable to very
different degrees. Strictness scored from inspection-report text is
correlated with observed strictness at $r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$, and a
text-only prediction model retains a leave-one-out correlation of
$\RidgeBStrictness$ with the gold standard. Warmth is not recoverable from
any public source: no text-derived warmth score is correlated with observed
warmth, and the text-only model has no out-of-sample skill (leave-one-out
$R^2$ of $-0.02$). Second, the enacted and espoused measures diverge in a
systematic and dimension-specific way. Headteachers rate their schools two
points warmer on a ten-point scale than observers record and about one point
stricter; the interview and the visit are correlated at $\GoldWarmthSplit$ for
warmth and $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ for strictness. Every public source,
including a survey of parents, tracks the espoused warmth measure and not the
enacted one. Third, the language-model instruments teach a lesson of their
own. An instrument can be tuned until it agrees closely with the labels it is
given, but that agreement says nothing about whether its scores track what
happens in the school; across every version tested, the two were unrelated,
and the version that agreed best with its labels was usually the one that
tracked the school worst.

The paper sits between five literatures and adds something to each. The
school-climate literature measures culture through pupil, teacher and parent
surveys \citep{hoy2001,cohen2009,thapa2013}. Those surveys have produced
consistent associations between climate and wellbeing, attendance and
attainment, but they capture perceptions rather than practice, are open to
social desirability, and are costly at national scale; \citet{vanhoutte2005}
argues that climate, the aggregate of members' perceptions, should be kept
apart from culture, the shared norms that govern behaviour, and it is culture
in that sense that the measures here aim at. Administrative proxies such as
exclusion rates \citep{machin2020} conflate culture with intake, and the
inspection grade folds many dimensions into one verdict. This paper's
contribution to that literature is a measure of enacted culture built from
observation rather than perception, with the perception data, from
headteachers and from parents, used as a comparison against it rather than as
a component of it.

The school-effectiveness tradition that begins with \citet{coleman1966} and
\citet{rutter1979} located the difference schools make not in resources but
in what Rutter called ethos, the expectations and routine practice a school
sustains, and later syntheses converged on a list that includes an orderly
environment and a supportive climate \citep{edmonds1979,sammons1995,teddlie2000}.
That strand has stayed largely qualitative because ethos has been measured
by case study; the contribution here is to make two of its correlates
measurable across a national population. Structured classroom observation,
in particular the CLASS framework of \citet{hamre2007}, measures emotional
support and classroom organisation directly and predicts pupil gains at
secondary level \citep{allen2013} and in English primary schools
\citep{sammons2008}. The observation schedules used here draw on those two
domains, but aggregate across lessons and out-of-lesson settings to
characterise a school rather than a teacher. To the author's knowledge no
study has combined such observation with a leadership interview and public
text to separate what a school does from what it says. The literature on
high-expectation, high-structure charter and academy models
\citep{abdulkadiroglu2011,angrist2010,dobbie2013,fryer2014} finds large
effects of a bundle of practices in which strict behaviour norms and warm,
individual relationships come together. It is the origin of the strictness
dimension and the reason to expect it to matter, but it has not measured the
two dimensions apart. Finally, the text-as-data literature in economics
\citep{gentzkow2019} supplies the tools for reading the public record at
scale. Its standing warning is that general-purpose methods misread
domain-specific text \citep{loughran2011,muddiman2019}. This paper uses large
language models rather than dictionary or topic methods, tests every
instrument against direct observation, and reports what that choice costs and
what it buys.

Against those five literatures, the paper contributes three things: a measure
of enacted culture built from observation; a national extension of one
dimension through public text, whose validity is tested against observation
rather than assumed; and the school-level scores that are the explanatory
variables of the following chapter.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:p1_data} describes the data.
Section~\ref{sec:p1_measurement} constructs the gold standard, the text-based
instruments and the prediction models, and reports how well the predictions
reproduce the gold standard. Section~\ref{sec:p1_results} reports what the
measures show. Section~\ref{sec:p1_conclusion} concludes.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:p1_data}

The paper uses three kinds of data on English state secondary schools: a
research visit and a headteacher interview conducted for this study, three
corpora of public text, and the Department for Education's school-level
administrative panel. \Cref{tab:tier_summary} sets out coverage.

\textbf{Visits and interviews.} Between November 2023 and April 2025 I
interviewed the headteacher, or where unavailable the senior leader
responsible for behaviour and culture, at 304 schools, and visited
\NTierOne{} of them. Schools were recruited by direct approach to
headteachers by email and telephone and by referral from participating
schools; no quota was applied by region, school type or inspection grade.
Three-quarters of interviews and half of visits took place in the 2024/25
academic year. Every headteacher interviewed was asked at the end of the
interview whether the school would host a visit, so the visited schools are a
self-selected subset of the interviewed ones.

A visit lasted a school day and was made by a team of two, occasionally
three, researchers. The team observed lessons across the timetable, typically
four to six in a day and five at the median, spread across Years 7 to 11 and
across subjects. Each observation covered either a whole lesson or half of
one, and every researcher present rated it independently on the lesson
schedule. The team also watched arrival to lessons, movement between lessons, break and
lunch, and at the end of the day each researcher completed the
outside-of-lessons schedule once, summarising the whole day. The lesson
schedule has nine items bearing on warmth and strictness (for example the
frequency with which the teacher uses pupils' names; the level of misbehaviour
tolerated before a first sanction) and nine on teaching practice (questioning,
feedback, explanation, structure and the like). The outside-of-lessons
schedule has two parts: eight items on the school outside the classroom
(sanctions and rewards, corridors, arrival, the dining hall, interactions and
relationships at break), and a short section in which the observer compares
the lessons seen across the day. Every item is rated 1 to 5 against written
anchors at 1, 3 and 5, and item ratings are averaged across researchers and
across lessons to give one value per school.

The interview lasted about an hour and followed a fixed schedule of eleven
open questions on the school's approach to behaviour, relationships, wellbeing
and teaching, ten statements on which the headteacher rated their own school
on a five-point agreement scale (for example ``Sanction systems are applied
consistently across the school''; ``Pupils in this school feel that their
teachers care about them''), and a checklist of ten named behaviour systems
(centralised detentions, an internal exclusion room, a mobile phone ban and
so on) recorded as present or absent. Two researchers attended every
interview, one asking the questions and one observing, and both scored the
open questions independently. The full schedules are reproduced in
\cref{app:2A:interview_guide,app:2A:visit_protocol}, and agreement between
researchers on the observation and interview items is reported with the
measures in Section~\ref{sec:p1_measurement}.

\textbf{Public text.} Three corpora cover the national population, and their
coverage is best read against the analysis panel of 3,332 open state
secondary schools. A scored inspection report exists for 3,272 of them
(98.2 per cent); the remainder had opened too recently to have been
inspected. A behaviour policy document exists for 3,294 (98.9 per cent), one
document per school, located by crawling from the school's website and
selected by a rule that prefers the current whole-school behaviour policy over
annexes, predecessor versions and sixth-form-only documents; about one
document in ten is a trust-wide policy adopted verbatim by the school. A
website crawl exists for 3,311 (99.4 per cent), taking the home, values and
welcome pages, which are the pages on which a school describes itself to
prospective parents; 3,284 of those crawls contain scorable prose, and where a
crawl returned no prose the school is flagged rather than dropped, so that
missingness is a variable and not a silent exclusion. The counts differ by a
few dozen schools because each corpus was gathered from the register of open
schools at a different date and because a handful of schools have no report,
no policy or no website; \cref{app:2A:sample} gives the reconciliation.

\textbf{Administrative data.} All schools are matched on their unique
reference number to a school-year panel built from the Department for
Education's published tables for 2015/16 to 2024/25, carrying pupil
composition, prior attainment, Progress~8 and its components, workforce and
finance. This paper uses the panel only to describe the samples; the
following chapter uses it for outcomes and controls. The panel also carries
each school's most recent overall inspection grade. Inspections from
September 2024 no longer award an overall grade, so the grade used throughout
is the last one each school received before that date, on the scale 1
(Outstanding) to 4 (Inadequate); schools inspected only since then, or never,
have no grade and are missing on that variable.

\textbf{Representativeness.} \Cref{tab:2A:representativeness} compares the
visited and interviewed schools with the national population. Both samples
resemble the population on school type, phase, admissions, size, deprivation
and urban location, and both over-represent London (45 per cent of visited
schools against 15 per cent nationally), which shows in a higher share of
pupils with English as an additional language. Both also lean toward stronger
schools: 25 per cent of visited schools held an Outstanding inspection grade
as at August 2024 against 15 per cent nationally, and their mean Progress~8
in 2018/19 was $+0.17$ against $+0.04$. That lean is the expected result of
recruiting through headteachers, since confident schools say yes, and it has
two consequences. Estimates from the visited sample apply most directly to a
population somewhat stronger than the national average; and because the
sample under-represents the weakest schools, the range of both culture and
outcomes is restricted, which works against finding associations rather than
for them.

\section{Measurement}
\label{sec:p1_measurement}

This section builds the measures in the order the design requires. It first
constructs the gold-standard scores from the visits and interviews and reports
their reliability. It then sets out how the public text is scored by
language-model instruments and how those instruments were tested. It ends
with the prediction models that carry the gold standard to schools that were
not visited, and the evidence on how well they do so.

\subsection{The gold standard}

Every observation item and interview statement is rated 1 to 5. Sub-scores
are item means; the dimension scores are sub-score means, rescaled to 0--10.
Two sets are constructed and kept apart. The \emph{enacted} scores use the
observations alone: enacted warmth averages in-lesson warmth (names, praise,
quality of interaction, pupil participation and motivation) and out-of-lesson
warmth (relationships and interactions during transitions and breaks, and the
consistency with which rewards are used); enacted strictness averages
in-lesson strictness (misbehaviour tolerated, disruption, response to
sanction, respect, concentration) and out-of-lesson strictness (the
consistency with which sanctions are used, corridors, arrival, dining hall,
recreational space); enacted teaching averages the nine lesson items on
practice. The remaining items of the outside-of-lessons schedule, in which the
observer compares teaching across the lessons of the day, enter no score,
because the same qualities are rated lesson by lesson in the teaching items.
The \emph{espoused} scores use the headteacher's statement ratings alone:
espoused warmth averages the three warmth statements (rewards applied
consistently; pupils feel safe; pupils feel their teachers care), espoused
strictness the four strictness statements (sanctions applied consistently;
behaviour in lessons; behaviour outside lessons; teachers feel respected). The
two remaining scored statements, on staff turnover and morale, form a
staff-climate score; the tenth statement, on parental engagement, enters no
score, and the interview yields no espoused measure of teaching. Item
definitions and the mapping to sub-scores are in \cref{app:2A:guide}.

To make the construction concrete, take the in-lesson strictness sub-score
of one school. In each of five lessons two researchers rated the level of
misbehaviour tolerated before a sanction, the frequency of disruption, the
pupils' response to correction and how respectfully they treated the teacher.
Misbehaviour and disruption are reverse-scored so that a higher value is
stricter, and where no sanction was needed in a lesson the response item is
filled from the disruption item, since a calm lesson in which no correction
was required is evidence about the school and not a missing value. The
twenty ratings per researcher are averaged to a lesson value, the lesson
values across lessons, and the researchers' values across researchers, giving
one in-lesson strictness figure on the 1--5 scale. That figure is averaged
with the out-of-lesson strictness figure and doubled to give the school's
enacted strictness on 0--10. Espoused strictness is simpler: the
headteacher's ratings on the four strictness statements are averaged and
doubled. Every school that received a visit has an enacted score; every
school that gave an interview has an espoused score; \NTierOne{} schools
have both.

Two components of the interview are recorded but excluded from the scores. The
question-level ratings of how fully the headteacher answered each open
question measure articulacy rather than culture: they load on one general
factor that tracks the interviewer's rating of the head's confidence and
willingness ($r = 0.34$ to $0.48$) and correlate with the observed scores at
zero. A count of the ten named behaviour systems correlates with observed
strictness at $-0.01$, against $0.41$ for the statement battery, and is
likewise excluded: what systems a school names is not how strictly it runs.
Both are retained in the data as variables in their own right.

Agreement between researchers is substantial. Two researchers rated every
lesson and every out-of-lesson period independently; pairwise Pearson
correlations across the observation items range from $0.47$ to $0.81$, and
the two researchers are within one point of each other close to 90 per cent
of the time throughout (\cref{tab:irr_classroom,tab:irr_outside}). Agreement
is highest on the items with the most concrete anchors, the consistency of
the reward system ($0.81$) and the frequency of disruption ($0.79$), and
lowest on the teaching-practice items that ask the observer for the most
interpretation, such as the use of resources and the choice of methods
($0.53$ and $0.55$). The interview items were rated by the interviewer and
the observer, with correlations of $0.61$ to $0.74$ and within-one-point
agreement above 90 per cent (\cref{tab:irr_interview}). Enacted warmth and
strictness are correlated at $r = \CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$ across the
\NTierOne{} visited schools, and the espoused pair at
$\CorrWarmthStrictnessEspoused$ across the same schools. Both correlations
are inflated a little by the fact that the same people produced both scores
on the same day, so the two dimensions are, if anything, more distinct than
these figures suggest.

The gold standard is two measures rather than one because the interview and
the visit disagree about the same school. Enacted and espoused warmth are
correlated at $r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ ($p = \GoldWarmthSplitP$) and enacted and
espoused strictness at $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ ($p = \GoldStrictnessSplitP$),
on the \NTierOne{} schools with both. Two measures sharing at most a sixth of
their variance are not fallible readings of one quantity. A weighted average
is the right operation for two noisy measures of a common target, because
their errors partly cancel; it is the wrong operation for two measures of
different targets, because it produces a third quantity that is neither,
whose composition depends on the weight chosen. The two are therefore carried
separately throughout, and their divergence is itself a result reported in
Section~\ref{sec:p1_results}. Two schools show what the divergence looks like
in the ratings. At the first, the headteacher rated the school 8.0 on warmth
and 8.0 on strictness; the observers, across their lessons and the day,
recorded 7.9 and 8.5, and the in-lesson and out-of-lesson sub-scores agree
with one another to within half a point. At the second, the headteacher rated
warmth 9.3 and strictness 7.8, near the top of the scale, and the observers
recorded 4.6 and 5.9: in-lesson warmth averaged 2.2 out of 5 and out-of-lesson
warmth 2.4, while the head's ratings on the three warmth statements averaged
4.7 out of 5. Nothing in the interview distinguishes the two heads. Across the
\NTierOne{} schools, four in five have a warmth gap larger than one point on
the 0--10 scale and half have a gap larger than two. One espoused measure can
be checked against an outside record. Heads rated how low their staff
turnover was and how high morale was; the staff-climate score built from
those two statements is correlated with the school's actual teacher turnover,
as recorded by the Department for Education, at $r = -0.52$, and at $-0.48$
once the other espoused scores, disadvantage and school size are held
constant. On the one question where a head's answer can be checked, heads
report accurately.

\subsection{Scoring the public text}

For schools without a visit the gold standard must be predicted from what is
observable, and the richest observable is text. Each of the three corpora is
scored by a language-model instrument that reads one document about one
school and returns a 1--5 score for warmth and for strictness (and, for
inspection reports, teaching). All scoring uses gpt-4o-mini at zero
sampling temperature; every score records the prompt version, a hash of the
rubric text and the model version, and identical inputs are served from
cache, so that any score in the dataset can be traced to the exact instrument
that produced it.

The pipeline is the same for every corpus. A document is retrieved and
reduced to plain text; for inspection reports every stated grade and every
field that would let the model infer the grade is stripped from the body,
so that the score is read from the prose and not from the verdict. The text
is presented to the model with the instrument's rubric, and the model's answer
is converted to a band. Two architectures are used at that last step. Which
is better for a given source was not assumed; it was decided by the tests
described below. \emph{Prose} instruments give the model a written marking
scheme and take its band; the inspection-report and behaviour-policy
instruments are of this kind, and each scores its constructs in a single call
on the full document. \emph{Decomposed} instruments never ask for a band: the
model answers a fixed set of factual questions, each answer supported by a
verbatim quotation verified against the document, and the band is assigned
by a fixed rule in code. The website strictness instrument, for example, asks
whether the page names a specific consequence for a specific behaviour,
whether it describes a rule at the level of a particular (a phone, a
corridor, a uniform item), whether it uses the vocabulary of expectation, and
so on; each answer must be backed by a quotation that appears in the page,
unbacked answers are discarded, and the band is assigned by a fixed ladder
over the answers that survive. The website and interview-transcript
instruments are of this kind. The operative rubric and question set of every
instrument is reproduced in \cref{app:2A:prompts}.

Instruments were tuned against reference labels and then tested three ways.
The reference labels were produced by three independent instances of a
larger language model (Claude, Opus class), each given only the written rule
and the documents, blind to one another and to every model score, with the
majority label as the target; the written rules were drafted and revised by
the author, who ruled on the boundary cases the raters could not and
labelled the development packs personally. Agreement figures below are
therefore model--model agreement, and the final arbiter of every instrument
is not agreement but the gold standard. Agreement is measured by weighted
kappa ($\kappa_w$), the standard statistic for agreement on an ordered scale:
it runs from 0 (no better than chance) to 1 (perfect agreement), and it
counts a rating one band off as a near miss rather than a full disagreement.
The author's agreement with his own labels of the same documents two weeks
apart is $\kappa_w = 0.77$. That figure sets a ceiling: a scorer can agree
with a rater no more closely than the rater agrees with himself, and the
ceiling is the square root of his own reliability, about $0.88$. Tuning and
testing were kept apart by design: each instrument was developed on a
labelled pack of documents, every version of it was archived, and every
archived version was then scored once on a fresh pack that no version had
been tuned on.

The first test is agreement out of sample: $\kappa_w$ against the reference
labels on documents no version of the instrument was tuned on. Every
in-sample figure had read high, and the out-of-sample figures were lower for
every instrument; \cref{tab:instruments_adopted} reports them for the
instruments retained.\footnote{About 85 archived versions across eleven
instrument families were rescored in this way.} The second test is validity:
correlation with the enacted gold standard on the visited schools, and, for
one construct, with an official register. The website instrument that reads a
school's religious character agrees with the register of religious character
held by the Department for Education for 97 per cent of schools
($\kappa = 0.91$, $n = 2{,}889$). That result establishes that the method
reads what is in the text. It matters for what follows, because it means that
where a construct fails against its criterion, the failure tells us about the
text, not about the method. The third test checks that no field available to
the model carries the inspection verdict: report bodies are stripped of every
stated grade before scoring, and how far the verdict can still be read from
the prose that remains is reported below.

The testing produced four results that governed which instruments were kept.
Each is a lesson about measuring with language models rather than about
schools.

First, agreement with labels and correlation with the gold standard are
unrelated across versions, and the version that agreed best with its labels
was usually the one that tracked the school worst. The inspection-report
strictness instrument that agreed best with its labels correlated with
observed strictness at $0.07$; the prose version, which agreed less well,
correlated at $0.42$ on the same grade-stripped text and the same schools.
The reason is that a rubric can be tuned toward what labellers reliably
notice in a document, and what labellers reliably notice is not always what
a school does. Agreement measures the first; only the visits measure the
second. Every instrument here was therefore selected on its correlation with
the observed measure, with agreement treated as a floor and never as the
objective.

Second, asking the model for facts works better than asking it for
judgements. A fact can be checked: does the page name a consequence? does the
report say pupils arrive on time? The model's answers to such questions are
stable and can be verified against the text. A judgement cannot be checked:
how strict does the document sound? The model's answer carries its own
generosity, and that generosity moves between versions and between models.
The decomposed instruments therefore ask only for facts, count the verified
answers in code, and assign the band by a written rule. The judgement is
taken out of the model and put into a rule that anyone can read.

Third, scoring related constructs in separate calls can produce a
correlation between them that the gold standard does not show. Enacted
warmth and strictness are correlated at $\CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$; the
single-call prose instrument, which reads a document once and returns both
scores, gives $0.54$; the separate-call instruments return $0.08$ to $0.18$.
A model that reads for warmth in one call and strictness in another has no
sense of the school as a whole, and returns two readings of two passages
rather than one reading of one school; the single-call figure sits closer to
the observed one for that reason.

Fourth, the model is part of the instrument. An unchanged rubric moved
between models fell from $\kappa_w = 0.63$ to $0.12$, from substantial
agreement to near none, with the text and the labels held fixed. Instruments
are therefore pinned to the model they were tested with, and a change of
model is treated as a new instrument to be tested again rather than as an
upgrade.

Two of the retained instruments have a weakness that should be stated once,
here, and borne in mind wherever their scores appear. The inspection-report
warmth score moves with the school's inspection grade ($r = -0.40$, negative
because 1 is Outstanding and 4 is Inadequate) even though every grade has
been removed from the text it reads, and it does so more strongly than the
reference raters reading the same text ($-0.25$). The model is picking up the
tone in which inspectors write about a school they rate highly. The score is
therefore treated as a description of the report, never as a clean predictor
of anything. The website warmth instrument cannot tell its two lowest bands
apart reliably: the model reads the least warm websites as much the same,
so the score carries less information at the bottom of its scale than at
the top.

\subsection{Predicting the gold standard}

For interviewed schools without a visit (Model~A) and for the national
population (Model~B), the enacted score of each dimension is predicted by
ridge regression fitted on the \RidgeTrainN{} visited schools with complete
predictors; the two visited schools missing are those without a scored
inspection report. Model~A's predictors are the espoused sub-scores, the
systems count and the three text scores for the dimension; Model~B's are the
three text scores alone. There are three text scores because there are three
corpora: the inspection report, the behaviour policy and the website each
yield one score per dimension. All three are entered so that the model
answers a question the paper cares about: whether a second or third public
source adds anything to the inspection report. Ridge is used rather than
least squares because the predictors are few, correlated and measured with
error, and a small penalty on the coefficients trades a little bias for a
useful reduction in variance; the penalty is chosen by generalised
cross-validation. Predictions are evaluated by leave-one-out
cross-validation on the same schools, which is the honest metric here: each
school's prediction comes from a model that never saw it, so the reported
correlation is what the model would achieve on a new school
(\cref{tab:interview_model_loo,tab:national_model_loo}). The fitted
coefficients are in \cref{tab:interview_model_coefs,tab:national_model_coefs}.

Strictness is predictable and warmth is not. Model~A predicts enacted
strictness at a leave-one-out $r = \RidgeAStrictness$ and Model~B, from text
alone, at $\RidgeBStrictness$. For warmth Model~A reaches $\RidgeAWarmth$ and
Model~B has no out-of-sample skill: its leave-one-out $R^2$ is $-0.02$, no
better than assigning every school the sample mean, and its predictions
collapse toward a constant with a standard deviation under a tenth of the
gold standard's. Among the predictors, the espoused strictness score and the
inspection-report strictness score do roughly equal work ($0.24$ and $0.23$
per standard deviation), which is to say that a headteacher's account of
whether the school's systems are applied consistently and an inspector's
description of conduct carry about the same information about what an
observer will find. The behaviour-policy and website scores add nothing
($-0.05$ and $0.05$ per standard deviation in Model~B), and the systems count
contributes nothing either.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:p1_results}

This section reports what the measures show. Three results stand out: the
enacted and espoused measures diverge, and they diverge more for warmth than
for strictness; every public source of text tracks the espoused warmth measure
and none tracks the enacted one; and behaviour policy text is unrelated to
observed practice.

\textbf{Distributions.} \Cref{tab:score_dist} reports the measures for the
visited schools and \cref{fig:score_distributions} draws the enacted and
espoused distributions side by side. Enacted warmth across the \NTierOne{}
visited schools has mean 6.4 and standard deviation 0.9 on the 0--10 scale;
enacted strictness has mean 7.0 and standard deviation 0.9. Espoused scores
across the \NInterviewed{} interviewed schools sit higher (means 8.5 and 8.0)
with standard deviations of 0.9 and 1.0. The two enacted dimensions are
correlated at $r = \CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$: warm schools tend also to
be orderly ones, which is consistent with the authoritative-school framework
and inconsistent with a single warm-to-strict axis, and the four quadrants of
the joint distribution are all populated. Between-school variation is
genuinely narrow in the English system, and score compression partly
reflects that: about two-thirds of secondary schools are graded Good and a
further sixth Outstanding, and common inspection, curriculum and training
pressures compress the distribution of any culture measure. A standard
deviation of enacted warmth is therefore about one point on the ten-point
scale, which is the unit in which the gaps below should be read.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_A_score_distributions.pdf}
  \caption{Enacted (visit) and espoused (interview) scores for the \NTierOne{}
    visited schools, warmth on the left and strictness on the right, on the
    0--10 scale. Dashed lines mark the means. The espoused distribution sits
    above the enacted one on both dimensions, and further above on warmth.}
  \label{fig:score_distributions}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Enacted and espoused culture diverge.} On the \NTierOne{} schools
with both, headteachers rate their schools warmer than observers record by
$\GapWarmth$ points on the 0--10 scale and stricter by $\GapStrictness$ (paired
$p < 0.001$ for both). A gap of two points is about two standard deviations
of the enacted measure: the typical headteacher places their school where an
observer would place a school near the top of the visited range. The two
sources are correlated at $\GoldWarmthSplit$ for warmth and
$\GoldStrictnessSplit$ for strictness. The gap is therefore larger, and the
agreement lower, for warmth: what a headteacher says about relationships is a
poorer guide to what a visitor finds than what they say about behaviour.
Within the interview, the items on which heads report reliably are those about
the consistency of practice (sanctions applied consistently, $r = 0.39$ with
observed strictness; rewards applied consistently, $0.28$ with observed
warmth), not those about how people feel (pupils feel safe, $0.14$; teachers
care, $0.11$ with observed warmth, neither significant). Headteachers report
accurately on whether their systems are applied and inaccurately on
relational climate, which is the pattern one would expect if the first is
something a head can check and the second is something a head must infer.

\textbf{Public text tracks talk, not practice.} \Cref{tab:source_criterion}
sets each source's warmth and strictness score against the enacted and the
espoused criterion. The pattern is uniform. For strictness, the
inspection-report score is correlated with the observed measure at
$\CorrOfstedStrictness$ and with the espoused measure at
$\CorrOfstedStrictEspoused$; behaviour policies and websites carry no
strictness signal against either. For warmth, no source is correlated with
the observed measure, and the inspection report and the parent survey are
correlated with the espoused one ($0.29$ and $0.31$). The parent survey is
the fourth independent respondent group, after observers, headteachers and
inspectors, and it shows the same crossover: parents' reports of their
children being happy and safe track what heads say about warmth ($0.31$) more
than what observers record ($0.17$), and track observed strictness ($0.28$)
better than observed warmth. \Cref{fig:crossover_warmth} draws the warmth half
of the table. What is written and reported about a school's warmth, by
inspectors, by parents and by the school itself, tracks the school's account
of itself and not the classroom. One reading is that the school's account is
the common source: a head who describes the school as warm describes it that
way to inspectors and to parents, and both repeat it. Another is that
inspectors and parents see the same public face that the interview elicits,
and that the classroom and the corridor are where warmth is enacted or not.
The data do not separate the two, and both imply that a national measure of
enacted warmth cannot be read from any of these sources.

\input{tables/tab_source_criterion}

\textbf{Behaviour policies describe aspiration.} A behaviour policy is
written by the school, so one might expect its warmth score to track what
the head says even if it does not track what observers see. It tracks
neither. The behaviour-policy warmth score is uncorrelated with observed
warmth ($0.01$) and with espoused warmth ($-0.06$), and it is slightly
negatively correlated with the parent survey ($-0.04$, $p = 0.03$); the
strictness score is uncorrelated with observed strictness ($-0.08$) and with
espoused strictness ($-0.01$). One school in ten publishes a policy authored
by its trust rather than by the school, and a template shared across a
trust's schools cannot vary with the culture of each. Written behaviour
policies are governance documents that state intent, and schools that state
the warmest intent are not, on these measures, the warmest schools. The same
result appears in the following chapter, where the behaviour-policy warmth
score is, if anything, negatively associated with pupil progress.

\textbf{What the text does and does not contain.} Two further results
qualify the inspection-report scores. The warmth score moves with the
inspection grade ($r = -0.40$; a lower grade is a better school) even though
every grade has been stripped from the text it reads, and it does so more
than the reference raters reading the same text ($-0.25$). Inspectors write
about relationships in a tone that moves with their overall verdict, and the
model reads that tone. The score is therefore a characterisation of the
report rather than a clean measure of the school. The strictness score also
moves with the verdict ($-0.49$), but unlike warmth it tracks observed
practice as well, and in the following chapter its association with pupil
progress survives conditioning on the grade.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:p1_conclusion}

This paper constructs measures of warmth and strictness for English secondary
schools from direct observation, headteacher interview and public text, and
asks how far each dimension can be recovered from data that exist for every
school. Three findings summarise it.

Strictness is legible in public text and warmth is not. An inspection
report's strictness score is correlated with observed strictness at
$\CorrOfstedStrictness$, and a text-only model predicts it out of sample at
$\RidgeBStrictness$; no text source is correlated with observed warmth, and
the text-only warmth model has no out-of-sample skill. The asymmetry is a
fact about the sources, not the instruments: inspectors record conduct and
enforcement in detail and describe relationships in a uniform positive
register, schools' own documents describe intent, and websites are not written
about the thing observers see. For the school-climate literature the result
is a caution: the sources that are cheap at national scale carry one of the
two dimensions and not the other, and a survey of parents carries the same
one.

Espoused and enacted culture diverge, and more for warmth. Headteachers
over-state warmth relative to observation by two points and agree with
observers only weakly on it, while their reports of behaviour systems being
applied consistently track observed strictness. Every public source, including
a survey of parents, tracks the espoused warmth measure and not the enacted
one. The gap is dimension-specific and appears in four independent kinds of
evidence, which suggests it is a feature of how schools present themselves
rather than a quirk of any one instrument. For the school-effectiveness
tradition, which has measured ethos largely through leaders' and teachers'
accounts, the result says which parts of those accounts can be trusted: the
parts about whether systems are applied, and not the parts about how people
feel.

The instruments teach a lesson of their own. How closely a language-model
scorer agrees with the labels it was tuned on says nothing about whether its
scores track what happens in the school: across every version tested the two
were unrelated, and the best-agreeing version was usually the worse measure.
Instruments that ask for facts outperformed those that ask for judgements;
scoring related constructs in separate calls produced correlations between
them that the observations do not show; and an unchanged rubric moved between
models lost most of its agreement. These results apply to any study that
scores text with a language model against a set of labels, and they argue for
testing every such instrument against something outside the labels rather
than against agreement alone.

Within its scope the paper delivers two measures that can be used with
confidence. The enacted scores are a reliable, two-dimensional measure of
school culture for the visited schools, built from direct observation by two
independent raters and checked against an outside record where one exists;
and the inspection-report strictness score extends one of the two dimensions
to the national population. The visited sample leans toward London and toward
stronger schools, and each school was seen on one day, so the following
chapter treats the measures accordingly. It asks what the two dimensions, so
measured, are worth in pupil progress.

\section{Scoring Guide and Item Definitions}
\label{app:2A:guide}

This section documents how the individual proforma items combine into the nine
sub-scores and the two score sets. The items themselves---question
wording, response format, and the anchor description attached to each rating
level---are reproduced in full in \cref{app:2A:interview_guide} for the interview
and \cref{app:2A:visit_protocol} for the two observation forms.

Every item is recorded on a 1--5 scale. Two observation items admit a zero code
with a distinct meaning: a zero on \emph{response of pupils to sanctions} records
that no sanction was issued during the lesson, and a zero on \emph{effectiveness
of pupil discussion} records that no discussion was initiated. The first is not
a genuine low score---an orderly lesson requiring no sanction should not be
counted as one where sanctions were ignored---so it is replaced by
$6 - \text{disruption}$, which assigns a calm unsanctioned lesson a high value
and a disrupted unsanctioned lesson a low one. The second is treated as a
genuine negative, since a lesson containing no discussion did not achieve
effective discussion.

Observation item scores are averaged across lessons and across researchers to
give one value per school before the sub-scores are formed.
\cref{tab:p1_subscore_items} lists the constituent items of each sub-score.

\input{snippets/subscore_items}

Two items on the outside-of-lessons form---consistency of verbal feedback and
quality of differentiation across lessons---are recorded on the form as
cross-lesson averages of the corresponding lesson observation items, and so
enter no sub-score of their own. Of the ten yes/no systems in the interview, S3
uses the eight that represent behavioural or academic requirements; the two
reward systems (reward assemblies and reward points) are excluded, as their
presence indicates a warmer rather than a stricter regime. S3 is the count of
those eight, rescaled to 0--5. Every other sub-score is the mean of its items,
computed over the items present so that a missing item reduces the number of
contributing items rather than the school's score.

Sub-scores combine into two score sets grouped by source, observed behaviour and
stated philosophy kept apart:
%
\begin{align}
\text{Enacted:}\quad
  W^{\text{enac}} &= \overline{(W1, W2)}, &
  S^{\text{enac}} &= \overline{(S1, S2)}, &
  T^{\text{enac}} &= T1 \label{eq:enacted} \\
\text{Espoused:}\quad
  W^{\text{esp}}  &= W3, &
  S^{\text{esp}}  &= S4, &
  \mathrm{SC} &= \overline{(\text{S3-stmt}, \text{S4-stmt})} \label{eq:espoused}
\end{align}
%
There is no espoused teaching score: the two interview statements under the
teaching heading concern staff turnover and morale, and they form the staff
climate score SC instead. The systems count $S3$ enters no espoused score.

Schools with only one of the two instruments carry only the corresponding score
set: an interview without a visit yields espoused scores alone, a visit without
an interview yields enacted scores alone. No imputation is attempted across the
two, since the espoused and enacted scores are correlated at only
$r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ (warmth) and $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ (strictness), too
little for either to stand in for the other. All scores are finally rescaled
from 0--5 to 0--10.

The mapping in \cref{tab:p1_subscore_items} is generated from the scoring code
itself, so it cannot drift from what the scorer does.

\section{Prediction Model Specification}
\label{app:2A:ridge_spec}

The interview-tier model (Model~A) predicts the enacted score of each
dimension from the vector $\mathbf{f}_s$ of interview sub-scores ($W3$, $S3$,
$S4$) and the three text scores for the dimension,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ridge_interview}
  \hat{y}_s = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}' \mathbf{f}_s ,
\end{equation}
%
and the national-tier model (Model~B) from the three text scores alone,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ridge_national}
  \hat{y}_s = \hat{\alpha} + \beta_O x_{O,s} + \beta_P x_{P,s} + \beta_W x_{W,s} ,
\end{equation}
%
where $x_{O,s}$ is the inspection-report score, $x_{P,s}$ the
behaviour-policy score and $x_{W,s}$ the website score for school~$s$. Both are ridge regressions fitted on the
\RidgeTrainN{} visited schools with a complete predictor vector; the penalty
$\lambda$ is selected by leave-one-out cross-validation on the same schools,
and predictions are clipped to $[0, 10]$.
The fitted coefficient vectors are reported in the chapter
(\cref{tab:interview_model_coefs,tab:national_model_coefs}).

The national-tier model takes the language model's holistic score for each
document as a single number per source. The question that specification invites
is whether the model's reading contributes anything that a deterministic counter
could not recover from the same text.
\Cref{tab:ridge_spec_comparison} tests this directly, comparing two feature
representations under the identical estimation procedure: (A) the three
language-model scores used throughout the chapter, and (D) the keyword-rule scores computed by
the pattern matcher that runs alongside the language model on the same
documents, as a floor against which to judge whether the language model
contributes anything beyond term counting.

\input{tables/tab_ridge_spec_comparison}

The comparison rests on the identical \RidgeTrainN{}~schools in every cell, so
it is like for like. The result is dimension-specific, and the
split runs along the same seam as the rest of the chapter.

On strictness, the language model's reading contributes something the counter
cannot recover. The holistic specification reaches $r = \RidgeBStrictness$ with
a cross-validated $R^2$ of $0.107$; the keyword baseline on the same documents
reaches $r = 0.094$ and an $R^2$ of $0.008$, barely distinguishable from
predicting the sample mean. Whatever the strictness score captures, it is not
the frequency of particular words. It is located in an act of reading rather
than in any countable property of the text, which is the opposite of the pattern
a feature-representation artefact would produce.

On warmth, neither specification recovers anything. Both select a penalty at or
near the top of the grid and shrink almost entirely to the training mean, and
both return a negative cross-validated $R^2$ ($-0.020$ for the holistic scores
and for the keyword rules), meaning each predicts the enacted warmth score
worse than the sample mean does; the keyword specification's predictions are too
nearly constant for a correlation to be defined at all. This is not evidence
that the warmth score is empty---\cref{tab:source_criterion} shows it is
correlated with the espoused criterion---but it does establish that the part
of it that survives to the national tier carries no enacted-warmth signal for
either representation to find. The feature-representation test can only
discriminate between two readings of the text where there is something in the
text to read.
