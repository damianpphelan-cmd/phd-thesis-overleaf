\begin{chapterabstract}
How warm a school is towards its pupils and how strictly it runs are widely
held to shape how much progress pupils make, yet neither is measured directly
for more than a handful of schools. This paper constructs measures of these
two dimensions of school culture, warmth and strictness, for English secondary
schools, and asks how far each can be recovered from data that exist for every
school. For \NTierOne{}
schools a research visit and a headteacher interview give a gold standard in
two parts: an enacted score from what observers saw, and an espoused score
from what the head reported. For every school in the country, inspection
reports, behaviour policies and websites are scored by language-model
instruments, and models fitted on the visited schools predict the gold
standard from those text scores. Two findings follow. Strictness is legible
in public text and warmth is not: inspection-report strictness is correlated
with observed strictness at $r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$, and a prediction
model using only text scores reproduces observed strictness out of sample with
a correlation of $\RidgeBStrictness$, while no text source is correlated with
observed warmth. Espoused and enacted culture diverge, and
more for warmth: what heads report about their school's warmth is only
weakly related to what observers record ($r = \GoldWarmthSplit$, against
\GoldStrictnessSplit{} for strictness), and the two outside sources that carry any warmth signal at all, the
inspection report and a survey of parents, track what heads say about warmth
rather than what observers see. The paper also reports what was learned
about scoring text with language models, chiefly that how closely a scorer
agrees with the labels it was tuned on says nothing about whether its scores
track the school. The measures are carried into the following chapter, which
asks what they predict.
\end{chapterabstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:p1_intro}

How warm a school is towards its pupils, and how strictly it runs, are two
of the things most often said to shape how much progress pupils make. Neither
is measured in any administrative dataset. This paper constructs measures of
these two dimensions of school culture, warmth and strictness, for a sample of
English state secondary schools, and asks how far each dimension can be
recovered from data that exist for all 3,330 of them.

Warmth is the relational quality of a school: whether pupils are known,
cared for and supported by the adults around them. In a lesson it shows in
whether the teacher uses pupils' names, how praise is given and how the
teacher and the class speak to one another; between lessons it shows in how
staff and pupils deal with each other in corridors and at break, and in
whether rewards are used consistently. Strictness is the school's behavioural
structure: whether expectations of conduct are clear, enforced and met. In a
lesson it shows in how much misbehaviour is tolerated before a sanction, how
much disruption occurs and how pupils respond when they are corrected; outside
lessons it shows in how pupils arrive, how they move between lessons and how
the dining hall runs. Strictness here means clear rules applied consistently,
not harsh punishment. A school whose sanctions are applied by every member of
staff in the same way is strict in this sense. Whether it is also warm is a
separate question.

The two dimensions are the axes of the parenting typology of
\citet{baumrind1971} and \citet{maccoby1983}, which classifies parenting by
demandingness and responsiveness and finds that the combination of both, the
authoritative style, produces the best child outcomes across cognitive and
non-cognitive domains; \citet{majumder2015} and \citet{zhang2020} find the
same pattern in educational attainment, with each dimension carrying an
independent effect. The transfer to schools is direct: demandingness is
strictness and responsiveness is warmth. \citet{pellerin2005} and
\citet{gregory2010,gregory2011} apply the typology to schools and find that
pupils in schools combining support with structure report less victimisation
and more engagement, and \citet{gottfredson2005} reviews the related evidence
on school discipline. What makes the two dimensions measurable in a school,
as opposed to a family, is that both are enacted in public. A researcher in a
lesson can see whether names are used and how disruption is met, and a
researcher in a corridor can see whether pupils move calmly and whether staff
speak to them; neither requires access to anything private. Warmth and
strictness are treated here as distinct dimensions rather than the ends of one
axis, because a school can be warm without being orderly and orderly without
being warm, and whether the two co-vary is a question the data answer rather
than a premise. A third dimension, the quality of teaching practice, is
observed alongside them; it is not part of culture as defined here but is the
obvious rival explanation for any association between culture and results,
and it is carried for that reason.

What is recorded, for every school, is text: an inspection report
written by an external observer, a behaviour policy written by the school, and
a website written by the school for parents. For a smaller set of schools this study adds
what no administrative source holds: direct observation of the school at work,
and a structured interview with its headteacher. The design follows from that
asymmetry. For \NTierOne{} schools there is a research visit, comprising
structured lesson and out-of-lesson observations, and a headteacher interview;
from these a gold-standard measure of each dimension is constructed. Because
the interview and the visit disagree substantially about the same school, the
gold standard is two measures, not one: an \emph{enacted} score, from what an
observer recorded, and an \emph{espoused} score, from what the headteacher
reported. For a further \NTierTwo{} schools there is the interview alone, and
for the remaining national population only the public text. The question the
paper puts to that text is whether the gold standard can be recovered from
it: prediction models are fitted on the schools where both the text scores
and the gold standard exist, each school is held out in turn, and how well
the predictions reproduce the gold standard is the measure of what the
public record contains. The answer decides what can be carried to the
national population, and it turns out to be one source and one dimension.

Two findings follow, and a third result about the instruments. First, the two dimensions are recoverable to very
different degrees. Strictness scored from inspection-report text is
correlated with observed strictness at $r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$, and a
prediction model using only text scores, fitted with each school held out in
turn, reproduces observed strictness with a correlation of
$\RidgeBStrictness$. Warmth is not recoverable from any public source: no
text-derived warmth score is correlated with observed warmth, and the same
model predicts observed warmth no better than its mean. Second, the enacted and espoused measures diverge in a
systematic and dimension-specific way. The interview and the visit are
correlated at only $\GoldWarmthSplit$ for warmth and $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ for
strictness, and heads place their schools near the top of their scale on
warmth whatever the observers find. Behaviour policies and
websites carry no warmth signal against either measure; the two outside
sources that do, the inspection report and a survey of parents, track the
espoused warmth measure and not the enacted one. Third, the language-model instruments teach a lesson of their
own. An instrument can be tuned until it agrees closely with the labels it is
given, but that agreement says nothing about whether its scores track what
happens in the school; across every version tested, the two were unrelated,
and the version that agreed best with its labels was usually the one that
tracked the school worst.

The paper sits between five literatures and adds something to each. The
school-climate literature measures culture through pupil, teacher and parent
surveys \citep{hoy2001,cohen2009,thapa2013}. Those surveys have produced
consistent associations between climate and wellbeing, attendance and
attainment, but they capture perceptions rather than practice, are open to
social desirability, and are costly at national scale; \citet{vanhoutte2005}
argues that climate, the aggregate of members' perceptions, should be kept
apart from culture, the shared norms that govern behaviour, and it is culture
in that sense that the measures here aim at. Administrative proxies such as
exclusion rates \citep{machin2020} conflate culture with intake, and the
inspection grade folds many dimensions into one verdict. This paper's
contribution to that literature is a measure of enacted culture built from
observation rather than perception, with the perception data, from
headteachers and from parents, used as a comparison against it rather than as
a component of it.

The school-effectiveness tradition that begins with \citet{coleman1966} and
\citet{rutter1979} located the difference schools make not in resources but
in what Rutter called ethos, the expectations and routine practice a school
sustains, and later syntheses converged on a list that includes an orderly
environment and a supportive climate \citep{edmonds1979,sammons1995,teddlie2000}.
That strand has stayed largely qualitative because ethos has been measured
by case study; the contribution here is to make two of its correlates
measurable across a national population. Structured classroom observation,
in particular the CLASS framework of \citet{hamre2007}, measures emotional
support and classroom organisation directly and predicts pupil gains at
secondary level \citep{allen2013} and in English primary schools
\citep{sammons2008}. The observation forms used here draw on those two
domains, but aggregate across lessons and out-of-lesson settings to
characterise a school rather than a teacher. To the author's knowledge no
study has combined such observation with a leadership interview and public
text to separate what a school does from what it says. The literature on
high-expectation, high-structure charter and academy models
\citep{abdulkadiroglu2011,angrist2010,dobbie2013,fryer2014} finds large
effects of a bundle of practices in which strict behaviour norms and warm,
individual relationships come together. It is the origin of the strictness
dimension and the reason to expect it to matter, but it has not measured the
two dimensions apart. Finally, the text-as-data literature in economics
\citep{gentzkow2019} supplies the tools for reading the public record at
scale. Its standing warning is that general-purpose methods misread
domain-specific text \citep{loughran2011,muddiman2019}. This paper uses large
language models rather than dictionary or topic methods, tests every
instrument against direct observation, and reports what that choice costs and
what it buys.

Against those five literatures, the paper contributes three things: a measure
of enacted culture built from observation; a national extension of one
dimension through public text, whose validity is tested against observation
rather than assumed; and the school-level scores that are the explanatory
variables of the following chapter.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:p1_data} describes the data.
Section~\ref{sec:p1_measurement} constructs the gold standard, the text-based
instruments and the prediction models, and reports how well the predictions
reproduce the gold standard. Section~\ref{sec:p1_results} reports what the
measures show. Section~\ref{sec:p1_conclusion} concludes.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:p1_data}

The paper uses four kinds of data on English state secondary schools: a
research visit and a headteacher interview conducted for this study, three
corpora of public text, a national survey of parents, and the Department for
Education's school-level administrative panel. The sources divide by whose
account of the school they carry. The visit records what trained observers
saw the school do, and the inspection report records what an inspector saw:
these are sources on \emph{enacted} culture. The interview, the behaviour
policy and the website record what the school says about itself, the first
to a researcher and the other two in writing: these are sources on
\emph{espoused} culture. The parent survey belongs to neither side; parents
report their own family's experience of the school, and whether that
experience follows what observers see or what the school says is one of the
questions the paper answers. The sources define three tiers of coverage:
\NTierOne{} schools with a visit, an interview and every public source;
\NTierTwo{} with an interview and every public source; and the remainder of
the national population with the public sources alone.
\Cref{tab:tier_summary} sets out the tiers and compares the schools in each
on intake, size and inspection grade.

\textbf{Visits and interviews.} Between November 2023 and April 2025 the
research team, working under my direction, interviewed the headteacher, or
in eight cases where the head was unavailable the senior leader responsible
for behaviour and culture, at 304 schools, and visited \NTierOne{} of them.\footnote{The counts that
follow differ by a school or two from place to place, for four reasons stated
once here. One of the 304 heads left the statement battery blank, so 303
schools carry an espoused score and the interview-only tier numbers
\NTierTwo{}; that school belongs to neither tier in \cref{tab:tier_summary}.
Of the \NTierOne{} visited schools, two have no scored inspection report and
two no published parent survey, so correlations and prediction models that
use those sources run on 101. Among the interviewed schools, 299 to 302 have
each text source. And one visited and three interviewed schools could not be
matched to the 2023/24 characteristics panel used in \cref{app:2A:sample},
which therefore describes 102 and 300.} Schools were recruited by direct approach to
headteachers by email and telephone and by referral from participating
schools; no quota was applied by region, school type or inspection grade.
Three-quarters of interviews and half of visits took place in the 2024/25
academic year. Every headteacher interviewed was asked at the end of the
interview whether the school would host a visit, so the visited schools are a
self-selected subset of the interviewed ones.

A visit lasted a school day and was made by a team of two, occasionally
three, researchers. The school was asked in advance to arrange four to six
lessons spread across year groups and subjects, and chose the particular
lessons itself; the team observed those lessons, typically five at the
median, spread across Years 7 to 11. The researchers knew which school they
were visiting but not its inspection grade; the school knew it was being
observed but not which items were being rated; and the movement between
lessons, break and lunch that the outside-of-lessons form rates were not
arranged by the school. That the school chose the lessons is a limitation of the visit
as a measure of practice, and it cuts in a known direction: a school shows
a visitor its better lessons, so the enacted scores are, if anything,
flattering and compressed, which works against the differences the paper
reports rather than for them. Each observation covered either a whole lesson or half of
one, and every researcher present rated it independently on the lesson
observation form. The team also watched arrival to lessons, movement between
lessons, break and lunch, and at the end of the day each researcher completed
the outside-of-lessons form once, summarising the whole day. The lesson form
has twenty items: ten bearing on warmth and strictness (for example the
frequency with which the teacher uses pupils' names; the level of misbehaviour
tolerated before a first sanction) and ten on teaching practice (questioning,
feedback, explanation, structure and the like). The outside-of-lessons form has
thirteen items: nine on the school outside the classroom (sanctions and
rewards, corridors, arrival, the dining hall, interactions and relationships
at break), two on the condition of wall displays, and two in which the
observer compares the teaching seen across the day. Not every item enters a
score; Section~\ref{sec:p1_measurement} says which do and why the rest do
not. Every item is rated 1 to 5 against written
anchors at 1, 3 and 5, and item ratings are averaged across researchers and
across lessons to give one value per school.

The interview lasted about an hour and followed a fixed guide of eleven
open questions on the school's approach to behaviour, relationships, wellbeing
and teaching, ten statements on which the headteacher rated their own school
on a five-point agreement scale (for example ``Sanction systems are applied
consistently across the school''; ``Pupils in this school feel that their
teachers care about them''), and a checklist of ten named behaviour systems
(centralised detentions, an internal exclusion room, a mobile phone ban and
so on) recorded as present or absent. Two researchers attended every
interview, one asking the questions and one observing, and both scored the
open questions independently. The observation forms and the interview guide
are reproduced in \cref{app:2A:visit_protocol,app:2A:interview_guide}, and
agreement between
researchers on the observation and interview items is reported with the
measures in Section~\ref{sec:p1_measurement}.

\textbf{Public text.} Three corpora cover the national population, and their
coverage is best read against the analysis panel of 3,332 open state
secondary schools. A scored inspection report exists for 3,272 of them
(98.2 per cent); the remainder had opened too recently to have been
inspected. A behaviour policy document exists for 3,294 (98.9 per cent), one
document per school, taken from the school's website; about one document in
ten is a trust-wide policy adopted verbatim by the school. A
website crawl exists for 3,311 (99.4 per cent), taking the home, values and
welcome pages, which are the pages on which a school describes itself to
prospective parents; 3,284 of those crawls contain scorable prose, and where a
crawl returned no prose the school is flagged rather than dropped, so that
missingness is a variable and not a silent exclusion. The counts differ by a
few dozen schools because a handful of schools have no report, no policy or
no website; \cref{app:2A:sample} gives the reconciliation.

\textbf{Parent survey.} Ofsted's Parent View survey is open to the parents of
every school and is published when enough have responded. The paper uses the
most recent release for each school and the two items closest to warmth:
the share of responding parents who agree that their child is happy at the
school and the share who agree that their child feels safe there. The two
shares are averaged into one parent-reported warmth measure, available for
3,255 schools in the panel and for 101 of the visited schools. Parents
choose whether to respond, and the typical release rests on about 120
responses at a response rate of about 12 per cent, so the measure is the
view of the parents who chose to give one. The survey has no item on
strictness.

\textbf{Administrative data.} All schools are matched on their unique
reference number to a school-year panel built from the Department for
Education's published tables for 2015/16 to 2024/25, carrying pupil
composition, prior attainment, Progress~8 and its components, workforce and
finance. This paper uses the panel only to describe the samples; the
following chapter uses it for outcomes and controls. The panel also carries
each school's most recent overall inspection grade. Inspections from
September 2024 no longer award an overall grade, so the grade used throughout
is the last one each school received before that date, on the scale 1
(Outstanding) to 4 (Inadequate); schools inspected only since then, or never,
have no grade and are missing on that variable.

\textbf{Representativeness.} \Cref{tab:2A:representativeness} compares the
visited and interviewed schools with the national population. Both samples
resemble the population on school type, phase, admissions, size and urban
location, differ from it on deprivation by at most two percentage points,
and both over-represent London (45 per cent of visited
schools against 15 per cent nationally), which shows in a higher share of
pupils with English as an additional language. Both also lean toward stronger
schools: 25 per cent of visited schools held an Outstanding inspection grade
as at August 2024 against 15 per cent nationally, and their mean Progress~8
in 2018/19 was $+0.17$ against $+0.04$. That lean is the expected result of
recruiting through headteachers, since confident schools say yes, and it has
two consequences. Estimates from the visited sample apply most directly to a
population somewhat stronger than the national average; and because the
sample under-represents the weakest schools, the range of both culture and
outcomes is restricted, which in the following chapter works against finding
associations rather than for them.

\section{Measurement}
\label{sec:p1_measurement}

This section builds the measures in the order the design requires. It first
constructs the gold-standard scores from the visits and interviews and reports
their reliability. It then sets out how the public text is scored by
language-model instruments and how those instruments were tested. It ends
with the prediction models that carry the gold standard to schools that were
not visited, and the evidence on how well they do so.

\subsection{The gold standard}

Every observation item and interview statement is rated 1 to 5. The scores
are built in two steps. Items are first averaged into \emph{sub-scores}, each
of which covers one dimension in one setting: warmth in lessons, warmth
outside lessons, strictness in lessons, strictness outside lessons, and so
on. The sub-scores for a dimension are then averaged into that dimension's
\emph{score}, so that a school has one warmth score and one strictness
score, and each is rescaled from the 1--5 range of the items to 0--10. Two
score sets are constructed and kept apart. The \emph{enacted} scores use the
observations alone. Enacted warmth averages in-lesson warmth (names, praise,
quality of interaction, pupil participation and motivation) with
out-of-lesson warmth (relationships and interactions during transitions and
breaks, and the consistency with which rewards are used). Enacted strictness
averages in-lesson strictness (misbehaviour tolerated, disruption, response
to sanction, respect, concentration) with out-of-lesson strictness (the
consistency with which sanctions are used, corridors, arrival, dining hall,
recreational space). Enacted teaching averages the ten lesson items on
practice. The \emph{espoused} scores use the headteacher's statement ratings
alone. Espoused warmth averages the three warmth statements (rewards applied
consistently; pupils feel safe; pupils feel their teachers care); espoused
strictness averages the four strictness statements (sanctions applied
consistently; behaviour in lessons; behaviour outside lessons; teachers feel
respected). The two remaining scored statements, on staff turnover and
morale, form a staff-climate score. Item definitions and the mapping to
sub-scores are in \cref{app:2A:guide}.

Not everything recorded enters a score. On the visit, the four
outside-of-lessons items that rate the condition of wall displays and compare
teaching across the day are left out, the first because displays are not
conduct and the second because the same qualities are rated lesson by lesson.
From the interview, the ratings of how fully the headteacher answered each
open question, the checklist of named behaviour systems and the statement on
parental engagement are left out, and the interview yields no espoused
measure of teaching. The reasons are given below and in full in
\cref{app:2A:guide}.

To make the construction concrete, take the in-lesson strictness sub-score
of one school. In each of five lessons two researchers rated the level of
misbehaviour tolerated before a sanction, the frequency of disruption, the
pupils' response to correction, how respectfully they treated the teacher,
and how well they concentrated. Misbehaviour and disruption are
reverse-scored so that a higher value is stricter. One item needs a rule. If
no pupil was sanctioned during a lesson, there is no response to correction
to rate, and the researcher records a zero. That zero is not a low score, and
dropping it would discard what the lesson shows. It is therefore replaced by
the reverse of the disruption rating: a lesson with no disruption and no
sanction is scored as if pupils had responded fully, and a lesson with
constant disruption and no sanction as if they had not responded at all. The
twenty-five ratings per researcher are averaged to a lesson value, the lesson
values across lessons, and the researchers' values across researchers, giving
one in-lesson strictness figure on the 1--5 scale. That figure is averaged
with the out-of-lesson strictness figure and doubled to give the school's
enacted strictness on 0--10. Espoused strictness is simpler: the
headteacher's ratings on the four strictness statements are averaged and
doubled. Every school that received a visit has an enacted score; every
school that gave an interview has an espoused score; \NTierOne{} schools
have both. \Cref{tab:score_dist} reports the distribution of every sub-score
and score across the visited schools.

The two larger exclusions from the interview deserve a word. The ratings of
how fully the headteacher answered each open question measure articulacy
rather than culture: they load on one general factor that tracks the
interviewer's rating of the head's confidence and willingness ($r = 0.34$ to
$0.48$) and correlate with the observed scores at zero. A count of the ten
named behaviour systems correlates with observed strictness at $-0.01$,
against $0.41$ for the statement battery: what systems a school names is not
how strictly it runs. Both are retained in the data as variables in their
own right.

Agreement between researchers is moderate at the level of a single item,
and the school-level scores built from many items, lessons and raters are
reliable enough to use, as shown below. Of the 517 lessons observed,
\LessonsTwoRaters{} were rated independently by two researchers and
\LessonsThreeRaters{} by three; in \LessonsOneRater{} only one researcher
was available for that lesson and it carries one rating.
The outside-of-lessons form was completed by two or more researchers for
\DaysTwoPlusRaters{} of the \NTierOne{} visits. Where two raters saw the
same lesson, pairwise Pearson correlations across the observation items
range from $0.47$ to $0.81$ and the two are within one point of each other
close to 90 per cent of the time (\cref{tab:irr_classroom,tab:irr_outside}).
Agreement is highest on the items with the most concrete anchors, the
consistency of the reward system ($0.81$) and the frequency of disruption
($0.79$), and lowest on the teaching-practice items that ask the observer for
the most interpretation, such as the use of resources and the choice of
methods ($0.53$ and $0.55$). The interview items were rated by the
interviewer and the observer, with correlations of $0.61$ to $0.74$ and
within-one-point agreement above 90 per cent (\cref{tab:irr_interview}).

What the paper's claims rest on is not the agreement of two raters on one
lesson but the reliability of a school's score, averaged over its lessons
and raters. \Cref{tab:score_reliability} gives it by asking why any two
ratings of a lesson differ. Take every rating a researcher gave a lesson for
in-lesson strictness, one number for one lesson from one researcher. Those
ratings differ for three reasons: because the lesson was in a stricter or
less strict school, which is the difference the measure is meant to
capture; because, within the same school, that lesson was calmer or rowdier
than the school's other lessons, a different teacher, class or period of the
day; and because, looking at the same lesson, one researcher scored it a
little higher than the other. The table divides the spread of the ratings
into those three shares. For in-lesson strictness they are \ShareSchoolS{}
per cent school, \ShareLessonS{} per cent lesson and \ShareRaterS{} per cent
rater: well over half of the variation in single-lesson strictness ratings
is lessons within the same school differing from one another, only about a
fifth is schools differing from each other, and under a fifth is the two
researchers disagreeing. For in-lesson warmth the shares are
\ShareSchoolW{}, \ShareLessonW{} and \ShareRaterW{} per cent: lessons within
a school vary less in warmth than in strictness, but the researchers
disagree more about warmth, which matches the item-level pattern above.

Two things follow. The first is why averaging matters: a single lesson is a
poor guide to its school, but averaging five lessons and the raters who saw
them drives the lesson and rater noise down. Averaged over a school's own
lessons and raters, the reliability of the in-lesson warmth score is
\RelW1{} and of in-lesson strictness \RelS1{}; the outside-of-lessons
sub-scores, which summarise a whole day, are more reliable (\RelW2{} and
\RelS2{}). The in-lesson and out-of-lesson halves of each enacted score,
rated in different settings, are correlated at \HalvesW{} for warmth and
\HalvesS{} for strictness across the \NHalves{} schools, which puts the
reliability of each combined enacted score at about \RelEnactedW{} for
warmth and \RelEnactedS{} for strictness; the espoused scales, three and
four statements, have internal consistencies of \AlphaWThree{} and
\AlphaSFour{}. The lessons that carry one rating cost little here: had
every lesson been rated twice, the in-lesson reliabilities would be
\RelWOneTwoRaters{} and \RelSOneTwoRaters{} rather than \RelW1{} and
\RelS1{}, because the noise that averaging has to remove is mostly between
lessons, not between raters. They do carry one caution. Single-rated
lessons were rated lower on average than double-rated ones (by
\SingleRatedGapW{} on warmth and \SingleRatedGapS{} on strictness on the
1--5 scale), and the \SchoolsWithSingleRated{} schools with such lessons
have slightly lower scores as a result ($r = \CorrSingleShareW{}$ between
the share of a school's single-rated lessons and its in-lesson warmth), so
the following chapter checks that its estimates do not depend on them.

The second is a fact about schools rather than about measurement. Behaviour
varies more from classroom to classroom within a school than it does between
schools, so a school's enacted score is an average over quite heterogeneous
lessons, a visit of five lessons measures that average with appreciable
error, and every correlation with the enacted scores reported below is
attenuated accordingly. The attenuation is not enough to change any
conclusion: the
enacted--espoused correlations corrected for the unreliability of both
sides rise to about \DisSplitW{} for warmth and \DisSplitS{} for strictness,
still far short of two readings of one quantity, and the inspection-report
strictness correlation of \CorrOfstedStrictness{} corrected for the
criterion's unreliability is about \DisOfstedS{}. The corrections are shown
for scale; the uncorrected figures are reported throughout.
Enacted warmth and
strictness are correlated at $r = \CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$ across the
\NTierOne{} visited schools, and the espoused pair at
$\CorrWarmthStrictnessEspoused$ across the same schools. Both correlations
are inflated a little by the fact that the same people produced both scores
on the same day, so the two dimensions are, if anything, more distinct than
these figures suggest.

The gold standard is two measures rather than one because the interview and
the visit disagree about the same school. Enacted and espoused warmth are
correlated at $r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ ($p = \GoldWarmthSplitP$) and enacted and
espoused strictness at $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ ($p = \GoldStrictnessSplitP$),
on the \NTierOne{} schools with both. Two measures sharing at most a sixth of
their variance are not fallible readings of one quantity. A weighted average
is the right operation for two noisy measures of a common target, because
their errors partly cancel; it is the wrong operation for two measures of
different targets, because it produces a third quantity that is neither,
whose composition depends on the weight chosen. The two are therefore carried
separately throughout, and their divergence is itself a result reported in
Section~\ref{sec:p1_results}. Two schools show what the divergence looks like
in the ratings. At the first, the headteacher rated the school 8.0 on warmth
and 8.0 on strictness; the observers, across their lessons and the day,
recorded 7.9 and 8.5, and the in-lesson and out-of-lesson sub-scores agree
with one another to within half a point. At the second, the headteacher rated
warmth 9.3 and strictness 7.8, near the top of the scale, and the observers
recorded 4.6 and 5.9: in-lesson warmth averaged 2.2 out of 5 and out-of-lesson
warmth 2.4, while the head's ratings on the three warmth statements averaged
4.7 out of 5. Nothing in the interview distinguishes the two heads. Across the
\NTierOne{} schools, four in five have a warmth gap larger than one point on
the 0--10 scale and half have a gap larger than two. One espoused measure can
be checked against an outside record. Heads rated how low their staff
turnover was and how high morale was; the staff-climate score built from
those two statements is correlated with the school's actual teacher turnover,
as recorded by the Department for Education, at $r = -0.52$, and at $-0.48$
once the other espoused scores, disadvantage and school size are held
constant. On the one question where a head's answer can be checked, heads
report accurately.

\subsection{Scoring the public text}

For schools without a visit the gold standard must be predicted from what is
observable, and the richest observable is text. Each of the three corpora is
scored by a language-model instrument that reads one document about one
school and returns a 1--5 score for warmth and for strictness (and, for
inspection reports, teaching). All scoring uses gpt-4o-mini at zero
sampling temperature; every score records the prompt version, a hash of the
rubric text and the model version, and identical inputs are served from
cache, so that any score in the dataset can be traced to the exact instrument
that produced it.

The pipeline is the same for every corpus. A document is retrieved and
reduced to plain text; for inspection reports every stated grade and every
field that would let the model infer the grade is stripped from the body,
so that the score is read from the prose and not from the verdict. The text
is presented to the model with the instrument's rubric, and the model's answer
is converted to a band. Two architectures are used at that last step. Which
is better for a given source was not assumed; it was decided by the tests
described below. \emph{Prose} instruments give the model a written marking
scheme and have it read the whole document against it in one call. The
inspection-report instrument is of this kind and returns a band for each of
its three constructs. The behaviour-policy instrument is a variant: it gives
the model the marking scheme as a written decision procedure, has it read
the whole policy once and answer the procedure's steps, two of them with a
quotation that is checked against the policy, and the band is then computed
from the answers rather than returned by the model.
\emph{Decomposed} instruments never ask for a band: the model answers a fixed
set of factual questions, each answer supported by a verbatim quotation
verified against the document, and the band is assigned by a fixed rule in
code. The website strictness instrument, for example, asks
whether the page names a specific consequence for a specific behaviour,
whether it describes a rule at the level of a particular (a phone, a
corridor, a uniform item), whether it uses the vocabulary of expectation, and
so on; each answer must be backed by a quotation that appears in the page,
unbacked answers are discarded, and the band is assigned by a fixed ladder
over the answers that survive. The website and interview-transcript
instruments are of this kind. The operative rubric and question set of every
instrument is reproduced in \cref{app:2A:prompts}.

Instruments were tuned against reference labels and then tested three ways.
The reference labels were produced by three independent instances of a
larger language model (Claude, Opus class), each given only the written rule
and the documents, blind to one another and to every model score, with the
majority label as the target; the written rules were drafted and revised by
the author, who ruled on the boundary cases the raters could not and
labelled the development packs personally. Agreement figures below are
therefore model--model agreement, and the final arbiter of every instrument
is not agreement but the gold standard. Agreement is measured by weighted
kappa ($\kappa_w$), the standard statistic for agreement on an ordered scale:
0 is agreement no better than chance and 1 is perfect agreement, and it
counts a rating one band off as a near miss rather than a full disagreement.
No instrument can be expected to reach 1. The written rules were applied by
the author to the same development documents twice, two weeks apart, with
$\kappa_w = 0.77$ between the two passes; a rater who agrees with himself at
$0.77$ can be matched by another rater, however good, at no more than the
square root of that, about $0.88$, and the reference raters, who apply the
same rules, inherit the same ceiling. Tuning and
testing were kept apart by design: each instrument was developed on a
labelled pack of documents, every version of it was archived, and every
archived version was then scored once on a fresh pack that no version had
been tuned on.

The first test is agreement out of sample: $\kappa_w$ against the reference
labels on documents no version of the instrument was tuned on. Every
in-sample figure had read high, and the out-of-sample figures were lower for
every instrument; \cref{tab:instruments_adopted} reports them for the
instruments retained. The second test is validity against the gold standard
on the visited schools, and it is run against both halves of it: correlation
with the enacted score asks whether a source reads what the school does, and
correlation with the espoused score asks whether it reads what the school
says about itself. A source that is written by the school might be expected
to pass the second test even if it fails the first. For one construct there
is also an official register to check against. The website instrument that
reads a school's religious character agrees with the register of religious
character held by the Department for Education for 97 per cent of schools
($\kappa = 0.91$, $n = 2{,}889$). Religious character is stated plainly on a
school's website, so this shows that where the text states a thing, the
method recovers it; it does not show that the text states everything. It
matters for what follows because, where a construct fails against its
criterion, the more likely explanation is that the text does not carry the
signal than that the method missed it. Much of what schools write about
warmth is written by every school: a policy that opens with a commitment to
every child feeling safe, valued and respected, or a welcome page that
promises a caring community in which every pupil is known, describes an
intention that the warmest and the least warm schools in the visited sample
state in nearly the same words. An inspection report, by contrast, records
whether pupils moved calmly between lessons and whether low-level disruption
was seen, and those are the sentences from which a strictness score can be
read. The third test checks that no field available to
the model carries the inspection verdict: report bodies are stripped of every
stated grade before scoring, and how far the verdict can still be read from
the prose that remains is reported below. Throughout, correlations on the
visited schools are estimated on about a hundred observations and carry
intervals to match: the inspection-report strictness score's $r =
\CorrOfstedStrictness$ with observed strictness has a bootstrap 95 per cent
interval of $\CIOfstedStrictLo$ to $\CIOfstedStrictHi$, and the website
strictness score's $0.09$ an interval of $\CIWebStrictLo$ to
$\CIWebStrictHi$; the two are different instruments reading different text,
and the first is distinguishable from zero while the second is not.

\input{tables/tab_instruments_adopted}

The testing produced four results that governed which instruments were kept.
Each is a lesson about measuring with language models rather than about
schools.

First, agreement with labels and correlation with the gold standard are
unrelated across versions, and the version that agreed best with its labels
was usually the one that tracked the school worst. The inspection-report
strictness instrument that agreed best with its labels correlated with
observed strictness at $0.07$; the prose version, which agreed less well,
correlated at $0.42$ on the same grade-stripped text and the same schools
(rank correlations; the Pearson figure for the prose version is the $0.40$
of \cref{tab:instruments_adopted}). The reason is that a rubric can be tuned toward what labellers reliably
notice in a document, and what labellers reliably notice is not always what
a school does. Agreement measures the first; only the visits measure the
second. The instruments were therefore chosen by a rule that treats
agreement as a floor and never as the objective. The inspection-report and
behaviour-policy instruments are the prose rubrics the project began with;
decomposed versions of each were built to raise agreement, and a rebuild
replaced the original only if it agreed at least as well with the labels and
did no worse against the gold standard on the visited schools. For reports
and policies no rebuild met that test and the prose rubrics were kept; for
websites the decomposed rebuild did, and was kept; the interview-transcript
instruments were decomposed from the start. Because the comparison that decided retention was made against the
visited schools, the validity figures in \cref{tab:instruments_adopted} are
for instruments chosen with those schools in view, and are to that extent
optimistic; the prediction models below are evaluated with each school held
out, but the choice of instrument was not.

Second, asking the model for facts works better than asking it for
judgements. A fact can be checked: does the page name a consequence? does the
report say pupils arrive on time? The model's answers to such questions are
stable and can be verified against the text. A judgement cannot be checked:
how strict does the document sound? The answer to that depends partly on how
lenient or severe the model itself tends to be when it judges, and that
tendency is not fixed: it differs from one version of the model to the next,
so the same document can move between bands for reasons that have nothing to
do with the document.
The decomposed instruments therefore ask only for facts, count the verified
answers in code, and assign the band by a written rule. The judgement is
taken out of the model and put into a rule that anyone can read.

Third, the correlation between two text scores depends on how they were
produced, and cannot be read as a fact about schools. In the visited schools,
warmth and strictness go together: the enacted scores are correlated at
$\CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$. The inspection-report instrument, which reads
a report once and returns both scores from one reading, gives $0.54$; the
behaviour-policy instrument, which also reads once but computes its bands
from answers to a decision procedure, gives $0.22$; and the website and
interview instruments, which score warmth in one call and strictness in a
separate call from separately verified facts, give $0.08$ to $0.18$. Part of
that range is the sources: an inspector describes conduct and relationships
in one account, and a website need not. But part is the instrument. A scorer
that answers a question about one passage at a time has no sense of the
school as a whole, and returns readings of passages rather than a reading of
a school. The practical consequence is that whether the two dimensions
co-vary is a question for the gold standard, not for the text scores.

Fourth, the model is part of the instrument. An unchanged rubric moved
between models fell from $\kappa_w = 0.63$ to $0.12$, from substantial
agreement to near none, with the text and the labels held fixed. Instruments
are therefore pinned to the model they were tested with, and a change of
model is treated as a new instrument to be tested again rather than as an
upgrade.

Two of the retained instruments have a weakness that should be stated once,
here, and borne in mind wherever their scores appear. The inspection-report
warmth score moves with the school's inspection grade ($r = -0.40$, negative
because 1 is Outstanding and 4 is Inadequate) even though every grade has
been removed from the text it reads, and it does so more strongly than the
reference raters reading the same text ($-0.25$). The model is picking up the
tone in which inspectors write about a school they rate highly. The score is
therefore treated as a description of the report, never as a clean predictor
of anything. The website warmth instrument cannot tell its two lowest bands
apart reliably: the model reads the least warm websites as much the same,
so the score carries less information at the bottom of its scale than at
the top.

A reader will notice that the inspection-report warmth instrument is also the
weakest of the retained instruments on agreement ($\kappa_w = 0.18$), and
will ask whether the warmth null reported below is a failure of the
instrument rather than of the text. Two facts argue that it is the text. The
best-agreeing of the eleven warmth versions tried, which reaches
$\kappa_w = 0.45$ against the same labels, correlates with observed warmth
at $0.04$, no better than the instrument kept. And the retained instrument
does read something in the report, since its scores track the espoused
warmth measure ($0.29$) and the inspection grade ($-0.40$); what it reads is
what inspectors write about relationships, and that does not track what
observers see. The direct check, a human reading of the visited schools'
reports set against the visits, is not available: the labelled packs were
drawn from schools outside the visited sample. The inspection-report teaching score, listed in
\cref{tab:instruments_adopted} because the rubric returns it alongside the
other two, does not track observed teaching practice ($0.06$) and is not used
anywhere in what follows.

\subsection{Predicting the gold standard}

Two prediction models test how far the enacted scores can be recovered
without a visit. Model~A uses what is available for an interviewed school
and Model~B what is available for every school; the comparison between them
asks whether the interview adds anything to the public text. Each predicts
the enacted score of a dimension by ridge regression fitted on the
\RidgeTrainN{} visited schools with complete predictors; the two visited
schools missing
are those without a scored inspection report. Model~A's predictors are the espoused sub-scores, the
systems count and the three text scores for the dimension; Model~B's are the
three text scores alone. There are three text scores because there are three
corpora: the inspection report, the behaviour policy and the website each
yield one score per dimension. All three are entered so that the model
answers a question the paper cares about: whether a second or third public
source adds anything to the inspection report. Ridge is used rather than
least squares because the predictors are few and correlated, and a small
penalty on the coefficients trades a little bias for a useful reduction in
variance; the penalty is chosen by leave-one-out cross-validation, and
predictions are clipped to the 0--10 scale. Predictions are evaluated by leave-one-out
cross-validation on the same schools, which is the honest metric here: each
school's prediction comes from a model that never saw it, so the reported
correlation is what the model would achieve on a new school
(\cref{tab:interview_model_loo,tab:national_model_loo}). The fitted
coefficients are in \cref{tab:interview_model_coefs,tab:national_model_coefs}.

Strictness is predictable and warmth is not. Model~A predicts enacted
strictness at a leave-one-out $r = \RidgeAStrictness$ and Model~B, from text
alone, at $\RidgeBStrictness$ (bootstrap 95 per cent interval
$\CILooStrictLo$ to $\CILooStrictHi$). For warmth Model~A reaches $\RidgeAWarmth$ and
Model~B has no out-of-sample skill: its leave-one-out $R^2$ is $-0.02$, no
better than assigning every school the sample mean, and its predictions
collapse toward a constant with a standard deviation under a tenth of the
gold standard's. Among the predictors, the espoused strictness score and the
inspection-report strictness score do roughly equal work ($0.24$ and $0.23$
per standard deviation), which is to say that a headteacher's account of
whether the school's systems are applied consistently and an inspector's
description of conduct carry about the same information about what an
observer will find. The behaviour-policy and website scores add nothing
($-0.05$ and $0.05$ per standard deviation in Model~B), and the systems count
contributes nothing either.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:p1_results}

This section reports what the measures show. Three results stand out: the
enacted and espoused measures diverge, and they diverge more for warmth than
for strictness; no source outside the visit tracks observed warmth, and the
two that carry any warmth signal, the inspection report and the parent
survey, track what heads say instead; and behaviour policy text is unrelated
to observed practice.

\textbf{Distributions.} \Cref{tab:score_dist} reported the measures for the
visited schools; \cref{fig:score_distributions} draws the enacted and
espoused distributions side by side. Enacted warmth across the \NTierOne{}
visited schools has mean 6.4 and standard deviation 0.9 on the 0--10 scale;
enacted strictness has mean 7.0 and standard deviation 0.9. Espoused scores
across all \NInterviewed{} interviewed schools sit higher (means 8.5 and 8.0)
with standard deviations of 0.9 and 1.0; on the visited schools alone the
espoused means are 8.4 and 7.9 (\cref{tab:score_dist}). The two enacted dimensions are
correlated at $r = \CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$: warm schools tend also to
be orderly ones, which is consistent with the authoritative-school framework
and inconsistent with a single warm-to-strict axis. \Cref{fig:quadrants_ch2}
plots the two against each other. Splitting at the medians, 35 schools are
warmer and stricter than the median school and 34 are neither, but 17 are
warm without being strict and 17 strict without being warm: the four
quadrants of the joint distribution are all populated, and a third of the
visited schools sit off the diagonal. Between-school variation is
probably narrow in the English system, and score compression partly
reflects that: about two-thirds of secondary schools are graded Good and a
further sixth Outstanding, and a common inspection framework, curriculum and
training regime would be expected to compress the distribution of any
culture measure. A standard
deviation of enacted warmth is therefore about one point on the ten-point
scale, which is the unit in which the gaps below should be read.

\textbf{Enacted and espoused culture diverge.} On the \NTierOne{} schools
with both, the two sources are correlated at $\GoldWarmthSplit$ for warmth and
$\GoldStrictnessSplit$ for strictness, so the interview shares a sixth of its
variance with the visit on strictness and a sixteenth on warmth. The two
correlations are estimated on the same schools and the difference between
them, $0.16$, has a bootstrap 95 per cent interval of $\SplitDiffLo$ to
$\SplitDiffHi$ ($p = \SplitDiffP$): the point estimates say agreement is
lower for warmth, and the sample is too small to establish it. The levels
tell the same story in a different way. Heads rate their schools near the
top of the warmth statements (mean $8.4$ on the 0--10 scale) and somewhat
lower on strictness ($7.9$),
while the observers' scores sit at $6.4$ and $7.0$; the two instruments use
different items and different raters, so the difference in level is not
itself a measure of over-statement, but it does mean that a head's warmth
rating carries little information about where the school sits among its
peers on the observed measure. What a headteacher says about relationships
is a poorer guide to what a visitor finds than what they say about
behaviour.
Within the interview, the items on which heads report reliably are those about
the consistency of practice (sanctions applied consistently, $r = 0.39$ with
observed strictness; rewards applied consistently, $0.28$ with observed
warmth), not those about how people feel (pupils feel safe, $0.14$; teachers
care, $0.11$ with observed warmth, neither significant). Headteachers report
accurately on whether their systems are applied and inaccurately on
relational climate, which is the pattern one would expect if the first is
something a head can check and the second is something a head must infer.

\textbf{Where public text carries a warmth signal, it tracks talk, not
practice.} \Cref{tab:source_criterion}
sets each source's warmth and strictness score against the enacted and the
espoused criterion. For strictness, the
inspection-report score is correlated with the observed measure at
$\CorrOfstedStrictness$ and with the espoused measure at
$\CorrOfstedStrictEspoused$; behaviour policies and websites carry no
strictness signal against either. For warmth, no source is correlated with
the observed measure, and the inspection report and the parent survey are
correlated with the espoused one ($0.29$ and $0.31$). The parent survey is
the fourth independent respondent group, after observers, headteachers and
inspectors, and it shows the same crossover: parents' reports of their
children being happy and safe track what heads say about warmth ($0.31$) more
than what observers record ($0.17$), and track observed strictness ($0.28$)
better than observed warmth. \Cref{fig:crossover_warmth} draws the warmth half
of the table. What is written and reported about a school's warmth, by
inspectors, by parents and by the school itself, tracks the school's account
of itself and not the classroom. One reading is that the school's account is
the common source: a head who describes the school as warm describes it that
way to inspectors and to parents, and both repeat it. Another is that
inspectors and parents see the same public face that the interview elicits,
and that the classroom and the corridor are where warmth is enacted or not.
The data do not separate the two, and both imply that a national measure of
enacted warmth cannot be read from any of these sources.

\textbf{Behaviour policies describe aspiration.} A behaviour policy is
written by the school, so one might expect its warmth score to track what
the head says even if it does not track what observers see. It tracks
neither. The behaviour-policy warmth score is uncorrelated with observed
warmth ($0.01$) and with espoused warmth ($-0.06$), and it is uncorrelated
with the parent survey ($-0.04$); the
strictness score is uncorrelated with observed strictness ($-0.08$) and with
espoused strictness ($-0.01$). One school in ten publishes a policy authored
by its trust rather than by the school, and a template shared across a
trust's schools cannot vary with the culture of each. Written behaviour
policies are governance documents that state intent, and schools that state
the warmest intent are not, on these measures, the warmest schools. The same
result appears in the following chapter, where the behaviour-policy warmth
score is, if anything, negatively associated with pupil progress.

\textbf{What the text does and does not contain.} Two further results
qualify the inspection-report scores. The warmth score moves with the
inspection grade ($r = -0.40$; a lower grade is a better school) even though
every grade has been stripped from the text it reads, and it does so more
than the reference raters reading the same text ($-0.25$). Inspectors write
about relationships in a tone that moves with their overall verdict, and the
model reads that tone. The score is therefore a characterisation of the
report rather than a clean measure of the school. The strictness score also
moves with the verdict ($-0.49$), but unlike warmth it tracks observed
practice as well, and in the following chapter its association with pupil
progress survives conditioning on the grade. A report also predates the
visit, often by years: measured from the day of the visit, the median report was
\AgeMedian{} years old, a quarter were under \AgeQOne{} years and a quarter
over \AgeQThree{}, and \AgeShareOverThree{} per cent were more than three
years old. Stale text should attenuate any correlation with current
practice, so the strictness correlation was recomputed on the \NRecent{}
schools with the more recent reports and the \NOlder{} with the older:
$\RStrictRecent$ and $\RStrictOlder$. The correlation does not fall with the
age of the report, and the warmth null holds in both halves
($\RWarmRecent$ and $\RWarmOlder$). What an inspector wrote about conduct
some years ago still describes the school the observers saw, which says
something about the persistence of the dimension as well as about the text.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:p1_conclusion}

This paper constructs measures of warmth and strictness for English secondary
schools from direct observation, headteacher interview and public text, and
asks how far each dimension can be recovered from data that exist for every
school. Three findings summarise it.

Strictness is legible in public text and warmth is not. An inspection
report's strictness score is correlated with observed strictness at
$\CorrOfstedStrictness$, and a text-only model predicts it out of sample at
$\RidgeBStrictness$; no text source is correlated with observed warmth, and
the text-only warmth model has no out-of-sample skill. The asymmetry
appears to lie in the sources rather than the instruments: the warmth
instruments that agree best with their labels do no better against
observation than those that agree worst, and the report's warmth score
tracks what heads say and what inspectors conclude, so the instrument reads
something, just not what observers see. Inspectors record conduct and
enforcement in detail but describe relationships in a uniformly positive
register. Schools' own documents describe intent, and websites are not
written about the thing observers see. For the school-climate literature the result
is a caution: the sources that are cheap at national scale carry one of the
two dimensions and not the other, and a survey of parents carries the same
one.

Espoused and enacted culture diverge, and more for warmth. Heads' reports of
their school's warmth are only weakly related to what observers record, while
their reports of behaviour systems being applied consistently track observed
strictness. No source outside the visit
tracks observed warmth, and the two that carry a warmth signal, the
inspection report and a survey of parents, track the espoused measure
instead. The gap between what is said and what is seen is dimension-specific
and shows in three independent accounts of the school, the headteacher's, the
inspector's and the parents', which suggests it is a feature of how schools
present themselves rather than a quirk of any one instrument. For the school-effectiveness
tradition, which has measured ethos largely through leaders' and teachers'
accounts, the result says which parts of those accounts can be trusted: the
parts about whether systems are applied, and not the parts about how people
feel.

The instruments teach a lesson of their own. How closely a language-model
scorer agrees with the labels it was tuned on says nothing about whether its
scores track what happens in the school: across every version tested the two
were unrelated, and the best-agreeing version was usually the worse measure.
Instruments that ask for facts outperformed those that ask for judgements.
How strongly two text scores correlate with each other depends on how they
were produced, so the relation between the dimensions has to be read from
observation, not from the text scores. An unchanged rubric moved between models
lost most of its agreement. These results apply to any study that
scores text with a language model against a set of labels, and they argue for
testing every such instrument against something outside the labels rather
than against agreement alone.

Within its scope the paper delivers two measures that can be used with
confidence. The enacted scores are a two-dimensional measure of school
culture for the visited schools, built from direct observation, with a
reliability that is reported and adequate; and the inspection-report
strictness score extends one of the two dimensions
to the national population. The visited sample leans toward London and toward
stronger schools, and each school was seen on one day, so the following
chapter treats the measures accordingly. It asks what the two dimensions, so
measured, are worth in pupil progress.

\section{Scoring Guide and Item Definitions}
\label{app:2A:guide}

This section documents how the individual proforma items combine into the nine
sub-scores and the two score sets. The items themselves---question
wording, response format, and the anchor description attached to each rating
level---are reproduced in full in \cref{app:2A:interview_guide} for the interview
and \cref{app:2A:visit_protocol} for the two observation forms.

Every item is recorded on a 1--5 scale. Two observation items admit a zero code
with a distinct meaning: a zero on \emph{response of pupils to sanctions} records
that no sanction was issued during the lesson, and a zero on \emph{effectiveness
of pupil discussion} records that no discussion was initiated. The first is not
a genuine low score---an orderly lesson requiring no sanction should not be
counted as one where sanctions were ignored---so it is replaced by
$6 - \text{disruption}$, which assigns a calm unsanctioned lesson a high value
and a disrupted unsanctioned lesson a low one. The second is treated as a
genuine negative, since a lesson containing no discussion did not achieve
effective discussion.

Observation item scores are averaged across lessons and across researchers to
give one value per school before the sub-scores are formed.
\cref{tab:p1_subscore_items} lists the constituent items of each sub-score.

\input{snippets/subscore_items}

\paragraph{Items recorded but not scored.} Every item on the lesson
observation form enters a sub-score. Four items on the outside-of-lessons form
do not. Two---consistency of verbal feedback and quality of differentiation
across lessons---are recorded as cross-lesson summaries of the corresponding
lesson observation items, and so enter no sub-score of their own; the lesson
items already carry them. Two more---the condition of wall and corridor
displays, and the extent to which displays align with the school's stated
values---are ratings of the building rather than of conduct or relationships,
and fit neither dimension as defined; both are retained in the data.

From the interview, three components are recorded but not scored. The
ratings of how fully the headteacher answered each open question (Q5 to Q8,
Q11 and Q12) measure how articulate the head is rather than how the school
runs: they load on one general factor that tracks the interviewer's ratings
of the head's confidence and willingness, and are uncorrelated with the
observed scores. The checklist of ten named behaviour systems, counted as
S3, correlates with observed strictness at $-0.01$ against $0.41$ for the
statement battery, and enters no espoused score; of the ten systems, S3
counts the eight that represent behavioural or academic requirements and
omits the two reward systems (reward assemblies and reward points), whose
presence indicates a warmer rather than a stricter regime, and is the count
of those eight rescaled to 0--5. The tenth statement, on parental
engagement, belongs to neither dimension. Two further statements, on staff
turnover and morale, are scored but form the staff-climate score SC rather
than a culture score; there is no espoused measure of teaching. The
remaining questions (Q1 to Q4, Q9 and Q10: length of tenure, the description
of the school's culture and how it is communicated, the characteristics of
an effective school, the staff structure and accountability) were asked for
context; their ratings, where taken, enter no score.

Every other sub-score is the mean of its items, computed over the items
present so that a missing item reduces the number of contributing items
rather than the school's score.

Sub-scores combine into two score sets grouped by source, observed behaviour and
stated philosophy kept apart:
%
\begin{align}
\text{Enacted:}\quad
  W^{\text{enac}} &= \overline{(W1, W2)}, &
  S^{\text{enac}} &= \overline{(S1, S2)}, &
  T^{\text{enac}} &= T1 \label{eq:enacted} \\
\text{Espoused:}\quad
  W^{\text{esp}}  &= W3, &
  S^{\text{esp}}  &= S4, &
  \mathrm{SC} &= \overline{(\text{S3-stmt}, \text{S4-stmt})} \label{eq:espoused}
\end{align}
%
There is no espoused teaching score: the two interview statements under the
teaching heading concern staff turnover and morale, and they form the staff
climate score SC instead. The systems count $S3$ enters no espoused score.

Schools with an interview but no visit carry the espoused score set alone
(every visited school was also interviewed). No imputation is attempted
across the two, since the espoused and enacted scores are correlated at only
$r = \GoldWarmthSplit$ (warmth) and $\GoldStrictnessSplit$ (strictness), too
little for either to stand in for the other. All scores are finally rescaled
from 0--5 to 0--10.

The mapping in \cref{tab:p1_subscore_items} is generated from the scoring code
itself, so it cannot drift from what the scorer does.

\paragraph{Corpus coverage.} The three text corpora were each gathered from

counts differ: inspection reports were scored for 3,324 schools, behaviour
policies obtained for 3,363, and websites crawled for 3,370. Read against the
analysis panel of 3,332 schools, 3,272 carry a scored inspection report
(98.2 per cent; the rest had opened too recently to be inspected), 3,294 a
behaviour policy (98.9 per cent), and 3,311 a website crawl (99.4 per cent),
of which 3,284 contain scorable prose. The few schools outside the panel in
each corpus are schools that closed, merged or changed identifier between the
crawl and the construction of the panel.

\section{Prediction Model Specification}
\label{app:2A:ridge_spec}

The interview-tier model (Model~A) predicts the enacted score of each
dimension from the vector $\mathbf{f}_s$ of interview sub-scores ($W3$, $S3$,
$S4$) and the three text scores for the dimension,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ridge_interview}
  \hat{y}_s = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}' \mathbf{f}_s ,
\end{equation}
%
and the national-tier model (Model~B) from the three text scores alone,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ridge_national}
  \hat{y}_s = \hat{\alpha} + \beta_O x_{O,s} + \beta_P x_{P,s} + \beta_W x_{W,s} ,
\end{equation}
%
where $x_{O,s}$ is the inspection-report score, $x_{P,s}$ the
behaviour-policy score and $x_{W,s}$ the website score for school~$s$. Both are ridge regressions fitted on the
\RidgeTrainN{} visited schools with a complete predictor vector; the penalty
$\lambda$ is selected by leave-one-out cross-validation on the same schools,
and predictions are clipped to $[0, 10]$.
The fitted coefficient vectors are reported in the chapter
(\cref{tab:interview_model_coefs,tab:national_model_coefs}).

The national-tier model takes the language model's holistic score for each
document as a single number per source. The question that specification invites
is whether the model's reading contributes anything that a deterministic counter
could not recover from the same text.
\Cref{tab:ridge_spec_comparison} tests this directly, comparing two feature
representations under the identical estimation procedure: (A) the three
language-model scores used throughout the chapter, and (D) the keyword-rule scores computed by
the pattern matcher that runs alongside the language model on the same
documents, as a floor against which to judge whether the language model
contributes anything beyond term counting.

\input{tables/tab_ridge_spec_comparison}

The comparison rests on the identical \RidgeTrainN{}~schools in every cell, so
it is like for like. The result is dimension-specific, and the
split runs along the same seam as the rest of the chapter.

On strictness, the language model's reading contributes something the counter
cannot recover. The holistic specification reaches $r = \RidgeBStrictness$ with
a cross-validated $R^2$ of $0.099$; the keyword baseline on the same documents
reaches $r = 0.094$ and an $R^2$ of $0.008$, barely distinguishable from
predicting the sample mean. Whatever the strictness score captures, it is not
the frequency of particular words. It is located in an act of reading rather
than in any countable property of the text, which is the opposite of what one
would see if the score were merely picking up word frequencies.

On warmth, neither specification recovers anything. Both select a penalty at or
near the top of the grid and shrink almost entirely to the training mean, and
both return a negative cross-validated $R^2$ ($-0.023$ for the holistic scores
and $-0.020$ for the keyword rules), meaning each predicts the enacted warmth
score worse than the sample mean does, and in both the predictions are too
nearly constant for a correlation to be defined. This is not evidence
that the warmth score is empty---\cref{tab:source_criterion} shows it is
correlated with the espoused criterion---but it does establish that the part
of it that survives to the national tier carries no enacted-warmth signal for
either representation to find. The feature-representation test can only
discriminate between two readings of the text where there is something in the
text to read.
