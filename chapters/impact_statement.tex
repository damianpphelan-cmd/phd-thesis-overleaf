This thesis measures two features of English secondary schools that matter
for pupils but have been hard to observe: the culture a school practises day
to day, and whether it offers a subject that leads to well-paid careers.
Both sets of findings are relevant to policy.

The findings on school culture matter first for how schools are monitored.
England introduced a new school inspection framework in 2025, and one
question raised by that change is how much an inspection report tells
parents. A report does say something about how warm and how strict a school
is, but only a modest amount, and the two are found in different parts of
the report. A school's own behaviour policy and website reflect what the
headteacher believes, not what a visitor would see. The method, in which
language models score these documents and the scores are checked against
observers' ratings from school visits, could be used by researchers or
government for every school in the country at low cost.

The findings on school culture matter second for the public argument about
how schools should manage pupils' behaviour. That argument usually assumes a
choice between strict discipline and pupils' wellbeing. Warmth and
strictness are separate features of a school, and pupils make the most
progress at schools that have both. Warmth predicts pupil progress as
strongly as strictness does, but only when it is measured by observers
rather than by the headteacher. Headteachers describe their schools as
warmer than observers find them.

During the period studied, fewer than half of English state schools offered
A-level economics, the principal route into one of the highest-earning
degrees. Whether a school offers the subject usually depends on whether it
has a teacher qualified to teach it. The third paper shows that hiring an
economics teacher more than pays for itself. Pupils who take the subject go
on to earn more and pay more tax, and on the paper's main estimate that
extra tax exceeds the full cost of recruiting, training and employing the
teacher several times over. Economics receives no teacher-training bursary,
unlike other shortage subjects, and the paper provides a costed case for
one. Schools without provision are concentrated outside London and the South
East, so expanding provision would also broaden regional access. These
results are relevant to the Department for Education and to campaigns to
widen participation in economics.

The findings have been presented at seminars at the Institute for Fiscal
Studies and at UCL Economics, and the third paper at the Royal Economic
Society Conference in 2024. All three papers will be circulated as working
papers and submitted to journals. Every participating school will receive a
non-technical summary of the first two papers' findings. The first two
papers were supported by the Institute for Fiscal Studies and the Stone
Centre at UCL, and non-technical articles will appear on their websites. The
third paper was supported by the Discover Economics campaign and the
Productivity and Diversity group at the London School of Economics.
