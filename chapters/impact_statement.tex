This thesis measures two features of English secondary schools that matter
for pupils: the culture a school practises, and whether it offers a subject
that leads to well-paid careers.
Both sets of findings are relevant to policy.

The findings on school culture matter first for how schools are monitored.
England introduced a new school inspection framework in 2025, and one
question raised by that change is how much an inspection report tells
parents about a school. A report's text carries some information about how
warm and how strict a school is, but only a modest amount. A
school's own behaviour policy and website carry no such information. They
reflect what the headteacher believes, not what a visitor would see. Parents
can therefore learn something about a school's culture from its inspection
report, but not from the school's own documents. Inspection reports could
also be written to say more about culture directly. The thesis also gives
researchers a measure of culture for nearly every school in England, built
from inspection reports and checked against the school visits.

The findings on school culture matter second for the public argument about
how schools should manage pupils' behaviour. That argument usually assumes a
choice between strict discipline and pupils' wellbeing. Warmth and
strictness are separate features of a school, and pupils make the most
progress at schools that have both. Warmth predicts pupil progress as
strongly as strictness does, but only when it is measured by observers
rather than by the headteacher. Headteachers describe their schools as
warmer than observers find them. Judging a school's warmth therefore needs
an outside observer, not the school's own account.

The third paper turns to A-level economics, the principal route into one of
the highest-earning degrees. In the years the paper covers, fewer than half
of English state schools offered the subject, usually because they had no
qualified teacher. The paper shows that hiring an economics
teacher more than pays for itself. Pupils who take the subject go on to
earn more, and so pay more tax. Over their careers, that extra tax comes to
several times the full cost of recruiting, training and employing the
teacher. Economics receives no teacher-training bursary,
unlike other shortage subjects, and the paper provides a costed case for
one. Schools without provision are concentrated outside London and the South
East, so expanding provision would also broaden regional access.

All three papers have been presented at IFS and UCL seminars, and the third
also at the Royal Economic Society Conference in 2024. Each will be
submitted to a journal. Every participating school will receive a
non-technical summary of the findings. All three
papers were supported by the Institute for Fiscal Studies. The first two
were also supported by the Stone Centre at UCL, and non-technical articles
will appear on their websites. The third was also supported by the Discover
Economics campaign and the Productivity and Diversity group at the London
School of Economics.
