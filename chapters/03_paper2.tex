\begin{chapterabstract}
  This chapter estimates whether a school's organisational culture predicts
  pupil academic progress, using a measurement framework that separately
  identifies warmth (the relational quality of the school environment) and
  strictness (its behavioural structure) from researcher visits and headteacher
  interviews. Enacted culture scores derived from 102 independent school
  observations are regressed against school-level Progress~8 components and
  overall Progress~8 for the 2023--24 cohort, conditioning on prior attainment,
  pupil composition, school type, and pre-COVID Ofsted grade. Both warmth and
  strictness significantly predict overall Progress~8 ($\hat{\beta}_W = 0.127$,
  $p = 0.006$; $\hat{\beta}_S = 0.120$, $p = 0.013$), with near-equal
  magnitudes. Adding directly observed teaching quality does not attenuate
  either coefficient, suggesting culture and instruction address largely distinct
  mechanisms. Replacing visit-based scores with headteacher self-reports reduces
  the warmth coefficient by 63--106~per~cent, validating the observation-based
  measurement design. School culture is a measurable and independently
  predictive determinant of pupil progress.
\end{chapterabstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:p2_intro}

The academic progress that pupils make between Key Stage~2 and GCSE varies
enormously across English secondary schools. Using the official Progress~8
measure, the gap between the highest- and lowest-performing schools in a
typical year spans more than four grade-equivalent points across all examined
subjects---a difference that translates into thousands of pounds of additional
lifetime earnings per pupil, per grade point \citep{hodge2021}. Understanding
what drives this variation is one of the central questions in the economics
of education.

The evidence reviewed in \cref{sec:p2_lit} suggests that neither the level
of school resources nor the formal type of school can fully account for the
observed heterogeneity in pupil progress. While teacher quality is a powerful
determinant of pupil outcomes \citep{rivkin2005, chetty2014}, the observable
characteristics that predict teacher quality are limited and the scope for
scaling up improvement through teacher-focused policy is constrained. A
parallel literature on high-performing charter schools in the United States
points to specific school-level practices---structured behaviour management,
high expectations, and intensive pupil support---as the features most strongly
associated with achievement gains \citep{dobbie2013}. These practices correspond
closely to what the developmental psychology literature describes as the
authoritative style: high on both demandingness and responsiveness
\citep{maccoby1983}. The warmth and strictness dimensions introduced in
\cref{ch:paper1} operationalise this idea at the level of the school.

This chapter uses the warmth and strictness scores constructed in
\cref{ch:paper1} to estimate their association with school-level Progress~8
component scores (English, Maths, EBaC, and Open) and, as secondary outcomes,
with subject entry rates along the EBaC pathway. The primary sample comprises
the 102 gold-standard schools for which directly observed, enacted culture
scores are available from classroom and outside-of-lesson observations. This
design avoids the well-documented divergence between headteacher self-report
and independent observation documented in \cref{sec:p1_convergence}: by using
visit-only predictors, the primary estimates reflect the culture that pupils
actually experience rather than the culture that headteachers describe. An
extended specification using the 303 interview schools with espoused culture
scores is reported as a validation appendix, with estimates expected to be
attenuated in the direction predicted by the observed espoused--enacted bias.
The central empirical question is whether schools that score higher on
warmth, strictness, or both achieve systematically better pupil progress,
conditional on a rich set of observable school and pupil characteristics drawn
from the school-level administrative panel.

The identification strategy relies on cross-sectional variation in warmth and
strictness scores across schools. Because the scores are measured at a point
in time and the analysis does not exploit within-school changes in culture over
time, the estimates should be interpreted as partial correlations conditional
on observables, rather than as causal effects. The principal threats to a
causal interpretation---omitted variable bias from unobservable pupil
composition and school-level characteristics, and the non-random selection of
schools into the interview programme---are discussed in \cref{sec:p2_id}. A
further concern, arising from headteacher turnover between the time of
measurement and the outcome period, is addressed in \cref{sec:p2_continuity}.

The main finding is that enacted school culture predicts pupil progress: both
warmth and strictness are positive, statistically significant, and near-equal
in magnitude as predictors of overall Progress~8 ($\hat{\beta}_W = 0.127$,
$\hat{\beta}_S = 0.120$, each $p < 0.015$). At the component level, warmth is
most strongly associated with English and EBaC progress and strictness with
EBaC and Maths, consistent with theoretical priors about the relational demands
of breadth-of-provision and the disciplinary requirements of high-stakes
sequential subjects. Neither coefficient is attenuated by conditioning on
directly observed teaching quality, which enters insignificantly once culture is
controlled, suggesting culture and instruction predict progress through largely
distinct channels. The findings are robust to dropping the pre-COVID Ofsted
grade control, using single-year or Attainment~8 outcomes, and adding baseline
SEMH share as an additional control. A direct enacted--espoused comparison
shows that headteacher self-reports have 63--106~per~cent less predictive validity
for warmth than researcher observations, providing an external validation of the
observation-based measurement design.

The remainder of this chapter is organised as follows. \Cref{sec:p2_lit}
reviews the related literature. \Cref{sec:p2_id} describes the research question
and identification strategy. \Cref{sec:p2_outcomes} describes the outcome
variables. \Cref{sec:p2_controls} describes the control variables.
\Cref{sec:p2_continuity} addresses headteacher continuity. \Cref{sec:p2_specs}
sets out the empirical specifications. \Cref{sec:p2_results} presents results,

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:p2_lit}

This chapter draws on four principal strands of related work: the literature on
school resources and pupil achievement; research on teacher quality; evidence
from the charter school and academy programmes on the effect of school type
and specific practices; and the parenting styles literature as the theoretical
foundation for the warmth and strictness framework.

\paragraph{School resources and pupil achievement.}
The relationship between school resources and student achievement was the
subject of sustained controversy in the latter part of the twentieth century.
\citet{hanushek1997} reviewed a large body of evidence and concluded there was
no strong or consistent relationship between per-pupil expenditure and student
performance once family background was controlled for. This finding was
challenged, in part, by the class size literature. \citet{angrist1999} use an
instrumental variables design based on Israeli class size rules and find that
reducing class size generates significant test score gains for fourth and fifth
graders. \citet{krueger1999} analyses a randomised experiment in Tennessee in
which pupils were assigned to small or regular-sized classes from kindergarten
through to third grade, finding improvements of approximately four percentile
points in the first year in a small class, increasing by one point per year
thereafter. \citet{krueger2001} show that these early-years gains persist into
secondary school.

Evidence on resource effects in English schools is broadly consistent with
small positive effects, particularly for disadvantaged pupils. \citet{levacic2005}
find that additional resources improve overall GCSE attainment, with effects
concentrated in the lower half of the prior attainment distribution.
\citet{jenkins2006} find modest and subject-specific positive effects of
additional resources in English secondary schools, with reductions in the
student--teacher ratio more effective than general expenditure increases.
\citet{holmlund2010} show that the 40 per cent increase in English school
spending from 2000 to 2010 had consistently positive effects on primary school
outcomes, with larger effects for economically disadvantaged pupils.
\citet{machin2010} evaluate the Excellence in Cities programme and find a
positive impact on attendance and mathematics performance, concentrated in the
most disadvantaged schools and in the middle of the ability distribution. The
overall picture is one of small but positive resource effects that are
substantially smaller than those attributable to school culture and practice.

\paragraph{Teacher quality.}
The evidence on teacher quality is unambiguous in one respect: variation in
teacher effectiveness is large and its consequences for pupil outcomes are
substantial. \citet{rivkin2005} use matched panel data to show that a one
standard deviation improvement in teacher quality generates gains comparable
to or larger than a ten-pupil reduction in class size, with little of this
variation explained by observable teacher characteristics. \citet{chetty2014}
find that students assigned to high value-added teachers are more likely to
attend university, earn higher wages, and are less likely to have children as
teenagers; replacing a teacher in the bottom five per cent of the quality
distribution with an average teacher would raise the present value of pupils'
lifetime earnings by approximately \$250,000 per classroom. In the English
context, \citet{slater2012} show that being taught by a high-quality teacher
over a two-year GCSE course adds 0.565 of a GCSE point per subject, and
\citet{murphy2011} estimate that raising the bottom decile of teachers to
average quality over ten years would move the United Kingdom from 21st to 3rd
place in the OECD reading rankings. The teacher quality literature suggests
that the most effective schools are those that attract and retain high-quality
teachers, but it leaves open the question of what school-level features
facilitate this and whether school culture predicts pupil progress
independently.

\paragraph{School type, autonomy, and specific practices.}
The introduction of academy schools in England and charter schools in the
United States has generated a large literature on the effect of school type
and autonomy on pupil outcomes. In the English context, the evidence is broadly
positive for the first wave of academies---those introduced under the Labour
government from 2002, targeting chronically underperforming schools in
disadvantaged areas. \citet{machin2011} find that academy conversion generates
significant improvements in both pupil intake quality and performance, with
stronger effects for schools that gained the most autonomy. \citet{eyles2016}
find that the first wave boosted GCSE performance and had positive medium-term
effects on post-compulsory schooling outcomes. The second wave of academies,
converter schools from 2010 onwards, shows much weaker or null effects
\citep{eyles2019}, suggesting that autonomy per se is not the operative
channel. A comprehensive review of the US charter school literature reaches a
similar conclusion: some charter schools are substantially more effective than
traditional public schools, but the majority show no average advantage and
some are inferior \citep{epple2016}.

The most informative strand of this literature focuses not on school type but
on the specific practices that differentiate effective from ineffective schools.
\citet{abdulkadiroglu2011} find that effective Boston charters are characterised
by strict discipline and generate large test score gains; less autonomous pilot
schools with similar demographics use different practices and do not produce
comparable gains. \citet{dobbie2013} show that an index of five practices---
including high expectations, frequent feedback, and intensive tutoring---explains
approximately half of the cross-school variation in charter school
effectiveness, while class size and per-pupil expenditure are unrelated to
effectiveness. \citet{fryer2014} implements this bundle in low-performing
Houston public schools using a field experiment and finds significant
mathematics gains. \citet{chabrier2016} find that schools adopting an
intensive structured approach to behaviour are associated with large academic
gains, though the relationship is partially explained by the quality of local
alternative schools. In the English context, \citet{machin2020} show that
higher exclusion rates in early academies reflect genuine disciplinary rigour
rather than the strategic off-rolling of weaker pupils. Taken together, this
literature points to school culture---and in particular the consistent
enforcement of high behavioural expectations combined with intensive pupil
support---as the most important distinguishing feature of high-performing
schools, and directly motivates the warmth and strictness measures introduced
in \cref{ch:paper1}.

\paragraph{Parenting styles and the authoritative school.}
The theoretical framework connecting warmth and strictness to pupil outcomes
draws on the parenting styles typology of \citet{maccoby1983}, which classifies
parenting on two orthogonal axes of demandingness and responsiveness. The
authoritative style---high on both---is associated with the most positive
cognitive and non-cognitive outcomes for children. \citet{majumder2015} finds
that authoritative parenting raises the probability of completing a bachelor's
degree by 18.5 percentage points relative to uninvolved parenting.
\citet{zhang2020} find that high demandingness and high responsiveness each
have independent positive effects on children's academic outcomes. The
extension of this framework to schools---with strictness as the school
analogue of demandingness and warmth as the analogue of responsiveness---is
developed in \cref{ch:paper1}. This chapter provides the first large-scale
test of the resulting hypothesis for English secondary schools.

\paragraph{Value-added measurement.}
The use of school-level value-added measures as outcomes is subject to
well-documented limitations. \citet{gorard2010} argues that school
effectiveness estimates derived from value-added models are dominated by
relative error terms and are not reliable. \citet{gorard2013} show that
school value-added estimates are highly unstable over time: no school with
at least 99 per cent of its pupils included in value-added calculations has a
clearly positive measure for five consecutive years. These findings counsel
caution in interpreting the Progress~8 scores used in this chapter as precise
estimates of school effectiveness. Progress~8 is nevertheless used as the
primary outcome for three reasons: it is the measure used by the Department
for Education in its secondary school performance tables; it controls for
pupils' Key Stage~2 prior attainment, which is the most important observable
predictor of GCSE performance; and it enables comparison with schools outside
the primary sample through nationally standardised benchmarks.

\section{Research Question and Identification Strategy}
\label{sec:p2_id}

This chapter examines the association between the school warmth and strictness
measures constructed in \cref{ch:paper1} and school-level pupil progress across
four Progress~8 components (English, Maths, EBaC, and Open).
The central question is whether schools that score higher on warmth, strictness,
or both achieve systematically better pupil progress, conditional on the full set
of observable school and pupil characteristics available in the administrative
panel.

The identification strategy relies on cross-sectional variation in warmth and
strictness scores across schools. Because scores are measured at a point in time
rather than across multiple years, the analysis does not exploit within-school
changes in culture over time, and the estimates should be interpreted as partial
correlations conditional on observables, rather than as causal effects. Two
sources of omitted variable bias are of particular concern.

First, schools serving more disadvantaged pupil populations may score
differently on warmth and strictness for reasons unrelated to genuine cultural
differences---for example, if headteachers in high-deprivation settings are more
likely to articulate a strong relational philosophy in interview, which would
inflate warmth scores, or more likely to have invested in structured behaviour
management systems, which would inflate strictness scores. The panel controls
described in \cref{sec:p2_controls} address this in part.

Second, the recruitment of schools for the visit and interview programme was not
random, which limits external validity to the extent that the sample is
systematically selected on unobservable characteristics. The degree to which the
sample resembles the national population on key observables is documented in
\cref{app:2A:sample} of \cref{ch:paper1}.

A further concern relates to temporal alignment. Because Progress~8 is measured
in 2023--24 while culture scores are measured at the time of interview or visit ---
predominantly in 2024--25 --- the cross-sectional design requires that school
culture is sufficiently stable across adjacent years that a score measured at
time~$t$ is a reliable proxy for the culture present at time~$t - 1$. This
assumption is supported empirically. Intra-class correlation (ICC) analysis across
nine structural panel characteristics --- reported in \cref{app:3A:stability} ---
shows that in every case between-school differences substantially exceed
within-school year-to-year fluctuations. The most volatile dimension, per-pupil
expenditure, has ICC~$= 0.52$; intake characteristics including EAL share, school
roll, and mean KS2 prior attainment of the entering cohort all exceed
ICC~$= 0.80$. A school's relative standing on these structural dimensions changes
little from one year to the next, consistent with the interpretation that culture
scores measured in 2024--25 are valid proxies for the organisational conditions
that shaped the 2023--24 cohort's experience of secondary school.

The primary specification uses the 102 gold-standard schools and enacted culture
scores derived exclusively from the school visit observations ($W_1$, $W_2$ for
warmth; $S_1$, $S_2$ for strictness; $T_1$ for teaching quality). This design
avoids conflating enacted culture with the espoused culture captured by headteacher
interview, whose systematic divergence from independently observed behaviour is
documented in \cref{sec:p1_convergence}. Interview-derived (espoused) scores are
then substituted for the visit scores on the same estimation sample as a
validation check---holding the sample fixed so that the two sets of estimates
are directly comparable---with the espoused--enacted
divergence entering as an explicit prediction: estimates from interview-derived
scores should show upward attenuation for warmth as documented in
\cref{ch:paper1} (interview over-reports warmth; strictness shows near-zero
systematic bias), so the interview-based $\hat\beta_1$ should be biased upward
relative to the primary enacted estimates, with no strong directional prediction
for $\hat\beta_2$. Correspondence between the two sets of estimates would
nonetheless strengthen the causal interpretation of the culture--progress association.

\section{Outcome Variables}
\label{sec:p2_outcomes}

\subsection{Primary outcomes: Progress~8 components}
\label{sec:p2_primary_outcomes}

The primary outcomes are four school-level Progress~8 component scores, drawn
from the Department for Education's school performance tables for 2023--24.
Progress~8 measures average pupil progress between Key Stage~2 (age~11) and
GCSE (age~16) relative to nationally similar pupils; the composite score is
normalised to zero at national level with a standard deviation of approximately
one grade.

The composite score is decomposed into four components: English (two slots,
double-weighted), Maths (two slots, double-weighted), EBaC (three slots:
sciences, computer science, history, geography, and modern foreign languages),
and Open (three slots: any additional approved qualification, including arts and
vocational subjects). The four components are examined separately rather than
using the aggregate Progress~8 score because the two pairs differ in how
school-level choices shape the score. For English and Maths every pupil sits the
relevant GCSE, so the component directly reflects instructional quality; for
EBaC and Open, schools influence both which subjects pupils enter and how well
they progress in those subjects. Unfilled EBaC or Open slots contribute zero
progress to the school's average, so the EBaC and Open components are partly
determined by curriculum-pathway decisions as well as teaching quality.

This structure generates a theoretical prediction about warmth and strictness.
Schools higher on strictness---characterised by structured behaviour management,
high academic expectations, and intensive pupil steering---are more likely to
direct pupils into the EBaC pathway, potentially raising the EBaC component
through both the entry-rate and the conditional-progress channels. Schools higher
on warmth---characterised by relational support, enrichment breadth, and pupil
engagement---may generate stronger progress in the more elective Open component.
If the two culture dimensions operate through distinct curriculum channels, the
EBaC and Open components would show differential sensitivity to warmth and
strictness; if both move together, the culture effect is general rather than
pathway-specific.

The primary outcome is a two-year average of Progress~8 component scores across
2022--23 and 2023--24. Using both available post-COVID years rather than a single
year reduces outcome measurement error, smoothing idiosyncratic year-on-year
variation while preserving all usable variation in cultural predictors.
Only these two years are suitable: earlier years (2018--19 and prior) are usable
but introduce larger temporal gaps relative to the 2024--25 culture scores; 2021--22
outcomes are treated as unreliable due to ongoing grade inflation and
Covid-disrupted cohorts; 2024--25 KS4 outcomes are unavailable because the cohort
completing GCSEs that year were in Year~6 in 2019--20, when Key Stage~2
assessments were suspended due to the Covid-19 pandemic, meaning the Progress~8
baseline cannot be constructed. Where a school lacks one of the two years (due to
school type changes or performance table suppression) the available single year is
used; where neither is available the school is excluded from that outcome's
regression. The final sample sizes by outcome are reported in
\cref{tab:outcome_stats}.

The choice of 2022--24 outcomes creates a temporal gap for the majority of schools
in the primary sample: school visits took place between January~2024 and
March~2025, with 53 of 103 (51~per~cent) in 2024--25. For most schools, the
enacted culture score therefore reflects the school approximately one to two
academic years after the outcome period. This is addressed in two ways. First,
the estimates are re-run on the subsample for which headteacher continuity
between the outcome year and the point of measurement can be confirmed
(\cref{sec:p2_continuity}). Second, the
validity of using a 2024--25 culture score as a proxy for 2022--24 culture rests
on an empirical assumption of structural stability: ICC analysis across nine
structural panel characteristics (\cref{app:3A:stability}) shows that
between-school differences substantially exceed within-school year-to-year
variation, consistent with culture scores being stable proxies across adjacent
years.

\subsection{Secondary outcomes: subject entry rates}
\label{sec:p2_secondary_outcomes}

Three secondary outcomes are examined in a separate sub-section of the results:
the EBaC entry rate (the share of pupils entered for the full EBaC suite of
subjects), the humanities entry rate (history or geography), and the languages
entry rate (any modern foreign language). These are derived from pupil count
columns in the 2023--24 performance tables, divided by the total number of pupils
at Key Stage~4. The DfE ceased publishing percentage-form EBaC entry columns in
recent performance releases, so entry rates are computed from the underlying
counts.

Unlike the Progress~8 components, which measure pupil progress conditional on
entry, the entry rates capture school-level curriculum-steering decisions directly:
specifically, the degree to which schools direct pupils toward academically
intensive subject pathways. Entry rates are expected to be more sensitive to
strictness and high-expectations culture than to warmth, because the academic
pathway decisions they reflect are closer to the demandingness dimension of
school culture. Analysing entry rates alongside Progress~8 components therefore
helps to disentangle whether any observed culture--progress association operates
through changed subject-entry patterns, through conditional progress within
subjects, or both.

Note that entry rates are available only at the subject-group level in the DfE
performance tables: humanities records history or geography (combined), languages
records any modern foreign language (combined), and sciences records double-award
science or higher (combined). Individual-subject data---distinguishing history
from geography, French from Spanish, or the separate sciences---are published in
a separate DfE underlying-data file and were not included in this project's data
collection.

\section{Control Variables}
\label{sec:p2_controls}

All specifications include the following controls, drawn from the school-level
administrative panel dataset.

\textit{Pupil composition}: share eligible for the pupil premium (FSM6 measure);
share with an Education, Health and Care (EHC) plan and share receiving SEN
support (aggregate measures; the Social, Emotional and Mental Health sub-category
is not included as a contemporaneous control owing to potential endogeneity with
school behaviour and exclusion practices, but is added as a baseline measure in
the robustness specifications described in \cref{sec:p2_specs}); mean
Key Stage~2 prior attainment; share with English as an Additional Language.

\textit{School characteristics}: school type (entered as a full set of dummies:
sponsor academy, converter academy, voluntary aided, voluntary controlled,
foundation, community); phase (11--16 versus 11--18); school size (number of
pupils on roll); urban/rural classification.

\textit{Performance context}: most recent Ofsted overall effectiveness grade
(entered as a set of dummies); number of years since the last Ofsted inspection.

\textit{Workforce}: share of teachers with qualified teacher status; average
teacher salary; leadership team size.

\textit{Financial}: per-pupil total expenditure; share of expenditure on teaching
staff.

\section{Headteacher Continuity}
\label{sec:p2_continuity}

The warmth and strictness scores reflect the philosophy and leadership of the
headteacher in post at the time of measurement. If the headteacher has changed
between the time of measurement and the period to which the outcome variable
relates, the alignment between the score and the outcome is weakened. This
constitutes a potential source of attenuation bias: a change in headteacher since
measurement reduces the correlation between the measured score and the current
culture of the school, biasing estimates of the culture--progress association
towards zero.

Continuity is measured by comparing the headteacher named in the school's most
recent Ofsted inspection report against the July~2026 GIAS snapshot. This
identifies whether leadership changed between the inspection and the present,
which is the closest available proxy for continuity across the measurement
window; it is not a direct measure of continuity since the research visit or
the interview, and no such measure is available at scale. Coverage is the
binding constraint. Of the 3{,}332 schools in the national frame, headteacher
names could be recovered from both sources for 1{,}422 (43~per~cent), of which
636 (45~per~cent) show a change. Within Tier~1, continuity could be determined
for only 30 of the 102 full-data schools; 7 of those 30 (23~per~cent) changed
headteacher. Across the 303 interviewed schools, continuity is determined for
109, of which 40 (37~per~cent) changed.

Because continuity is unobserved for roughly seven in ten Tier~1 schools, a
continuity indicator cannot be entered as a control without collapsing the
estimation sample, and it is therefore not included in the main specifications.
Instead the primary specification is re-estimated on the subsample of schools
with confirmed continuity, to assess the sensitivity of the estimates to this
potential source of measurement error (\cref{tab:continuity_robustness}). The
resulting subsample is small ($n = 23$), and---because it is defined jointly by
continuity and by data availability---is not a random subset of Tier~1. The
check is accordingly a weak one, and is reported as such.

\section{Empirical Specifications}
\label{sec:p2_specs}

The analysis uses a three-stage regression design that separates the total
culture effect from the component operating via teaching quality. All three stages
are cross-sectional OLS with HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors,
estimated on the 102 gold-standard schools.

\paragraph{Stage 1: Total culture effect.}
The first stage estimates the joint effect of enacted warmth and strictness,
excluding teaching quality:
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:stage1}
  y_s = \alpha + \beta_1 W_s + \beta_2 S_s + \boldsymbol{\beta}' \mathbf{X}_s
  + \varepsilon_s
\end{equation}
%
where $y_s$ is a Progress~8 component score (English, Maths, EBaC, or Open) for
school~$s$, $W_s$ is the enacted warmth score, $S_s$ is the enacted strictness
score, and $\mathbf{X}_s$ is the vector of controls described in
\cref{sec:p2_controls}. The coefficients $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ are the primary
parameters of interest: $\beta_1 > 0$ implies that, conditional on observables,
schools scoring higher on warmth achieve better pupil progress; $\beta_2 > 0$
implies the same for strictness. Excluding teaching quality from Stage~1 avoids
conditioning on a variable that is partly a consequence of school culture (since
warmer and more structured school environments tend to produce better teaching
quality scores), which would otherwise under-estimate the total culture effect.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Teaching quality benchmark.}
The second stage estimates the total effect of the observed teaching quality
score~$T_s$ alone:
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:stage2}
  y_s = \alpha + \gamma T_s + \boldsymbol{\beta}' \mathbf{X}_s + \varepsilon_s
\end{equation}
%
This provides a benchmark for the magnitude of the teaching quality effect and
allows the culture effect to be evaluated relative to the dimension of school
practice most directly linked to instructional outcomes.

\paragraph{Stage 3: Direct culture effect net of teaching quality.}
The third stage includes all three predictors:
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:stage3}
  y_s = \alpha + \beta_1 W_s + \beta_2 S_s + \gamma T_s
  + \boldsymbol{\beta}' \mathbf{X}_s + \varepsilon_s
\end{equation}
%
Comparing the culture coefficients across Stages~1 and~3 quantifies
attenuation: the proportion of the Stage~1 culture effect that is retained in
Stage~3 measures how much of the culture--progress association is independent of
the teaching quality channel. If the culture effect operates partly by raising
teaching quality (culture as an input to instruction), Stage~3 coefficients will
be attenuated relative to Stage~1. If the culture effect is largely independent
of teaching quality---operating through, for example, pupil engagement, attendance
behaviour, or non-cognitive skill formation---the attenuation will be small.

The three-stage design provides a partial mediation framework rather than a causal
decomposition: because culture, teaching quality, and pupil progress are measured
cross-sectionally without instrument, the attenuation is informative about the
\emph{gross} vs \emph{net} culture association rather than a structural parameter.
Nonetheless, the pattern of attenuation by outcome (EBaC vs Open, English vs
Maths) is theoretically meaningful.

Robustness specifications are reported in \cref{tab:robustness_overall} and
\cref{tab:robustness_eng}, and include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Replacing enacted visit-only scores with interview-only scores
  ($W3^*$ for warmth; $\tfrac{1}{2}(S3+S4)$ for strictness) on the same
  estimation sample ($N=95$), to test the predicted attenuation from
  espoused--enacted divergence (\cref{tab:enacted_espoused}).
  \item Replacing main warmth and strictness scores with enriched scores (see
  \cref{sec:p1_enriched} of \cref{ch:paper1}).
  \item Restricting to schools where the headteacher is confirmed unchanged
  since the most recent Ofsted inspection (\cref{sec:p2_continuity}).
  \item Adding baseline SEMH share (school-level share of pupils with Social,
  Emotional and Mental Health as primary SEN need, measured in 2015--16) as
  an additional control variable.
  \item Including an interaction term $W_s \times S_s$ to test whether the
  warm-strict combination is associated with better progress than either
  dimension alone.
  \item Replacing Progress~8 component scores with overall Progress~8,
  Attainment~8, or GCSE English/Maths pass rates at grade~4+ and grade~5+.
\end{itemize}

All specifications are estimated on the sample of schools with non-missing
warmth, strictness, and outcome data.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:p2_results}

\Cref{tab:main_results_s1} reports Stage~1 estimates from the primary
specification, regressing overall Progress~8 and each of the four component
outcomes on enacted warmth~$W$ and strictness~$S$ plus the full control set,
for the $n = 95$ schools with non-missing pre-COVID Ofsted grade. Both culture
dimensions are positively and significantly associated with overall
Progress~8: $\hat{\beta}_W = 0.127$ ($p = 0.006$, HC3 robust SEs throughout)
and $\hat{\beta}_S = 0.120$ ($p = 0.013$), with near-equal magnitudes and
$R^2 = 0.595$.

At the component level, the pattern of significance varies across outcomes
in ways that partially accord with theory. Warmth is significantly associated
with English progress ($\hat{\beta}_W = 0.142$, $p = 0.002$), EBaC
($\hat{\beta}_W = 0.146$, $p = 0.017$), and Open ($\hat{\beta}_W = 0.124$,
$p = 0.020$), but not with Maths ($\hat{\beta}_W = 0.096$, $p = 0.100$).
Strictness is significantly associated with EBaC ($\hat{\beta}_S = 0.155$,
$p = 0.009$), Open ($\hat{\beta}_S = 0.123$, $p = 0.044$), and Maths
($\hat{\beta}_S = 0.107$, $p = 0.040$), but not with English
($\hat{\beta}_S = 0.079$, $p = 0.145$). The EBaC component shows the largest
and most consistent culture effects: both $W$ and $S$ are significant with
comparable magnitudes ($\hat{\beta}_W \approx \hat{\beta}_S \approx 0.15$).
Maths and English diverge from each other---strictness is the sole significant
predictor for Maths; warmth is the sole significant predictor for
English---consistent with the domain-specific theoretical predictions of
\cref{sec:p2_outcomes}.

Visit-based teaching quality is positively and significantly associated with

\subsection{National extension}
\label{sec:p2_national}

A complementary test of the strictness finding uses the Ofsted LLM strictness
score, an observer-reported measure scored on a 1--5 scale and available for
$n = 3{,}148$ English secondary schools (\cref{tab:national_strictness}).
Because the national measure derives from the same Ofsted inspection report
used to construct the pre-COVID grade control, the Ofsted grade dummy is
omitted from the headline specification (Panel~A of
\cref{tab:national_strictness}) to avoid conditioning on a downstream
confounder. That omission is itself a judgement call rather than the
conservative choice, so Panel~B reports the same specification with the grade
restored. No comparable national enacted warmth measure passes
the validity threshold established in
\cref{sec:p1_text_valid} of \cref{ch:paper1}, so warmth is omitted.

The Ofsted LLM strictness coefficient for overall Progress~8 is
$\hat{\beta}_{S}^{\text{nat}} = 0.135$ ($p < 0.001$, $R^2 = 0.506$,
$n = 3{,}148$), and is significant at the one-per-cent level across all four
Progress~8 components (English: $0.124$; Maths: $0.117$; EBaC: $0.137$;
Open: $0.158$). The Ofsted measure is on a 1--5 scale whereas the
gold-standard visit measure is on a 0--10 scale; doubling to achieve approximate
comparability yields a scale-adjusted coefficient of $0.270$, somewhat higher
than the Tier~1 visit estimate of $0.120$, though the two specifications differ
in sample and warmth control. The concordance in sign
and significance across two independent measurement approaches, two orders of
magnitude apart in sample size, substantially strengthens the inference that
strictness is associated with higher Progress~8.

The most obvious challenge to this result is that the strictness score is read
from an Ofsted report, so the coefficient might be recording little more than
Ofsted's overall approval of the school. Panel~B addresses this directly by
adding the pre-COVID (2019) overall Ofsted grade---the same control already
used in the primary Tier~1 specification---to the national regression. It is
estimated on the $n = 2{,}820$ schools with a 2019 grade on record. The
strictness coefficient is essentially untouched: overall Progress~8 moves from
$0.135$ to $0.136$, and no component estimate moves by more than $0.005$
(English: $0.124 \to 0.128$; Maths: $0.117 \to 0.117$; EBaC:
$0.137 \to 0.138$; Open: $0.158 \to 0.158$). All five remain significant at
the one-per-cent level. Conditioning on the inspection verdict therefore
absorbs almost none of the association, which is difficult to reconcile with
the view that the LLM score is a proxy for the grade: the two carry
substantially independent information about the school.

\subsection{SEMH mechanism test}
\label{sec:p2_semh_mechanism}

If strict schools engage in behavioural sorting---excluding or discouraging
enrolment of pupils with Social, Emotional and Mental Health (SEMH) needs---one
would expect them to have a lower current SEMH share than their 2015--16
baseline would predict. \Cref{tab:semh_mechanism} tests this by regressing
current SEMH prevalence (share of pupils with SEMH as primary SEN need,
2023--24) on Ofsted LLM strictness---or gold-standard visit strictness where
available---controlling for the 2015--16 baseline share and the standard
covariate set. At the national level ($n = 2{,}909$), stricter schools have
significantly lower current SEMH share conditional on their baseline:
$\hat{\beta}_{S}^{\text{nat}} = -0.132$ ($p = 0.001$). The direction is the
same in the Tier~1 subsample ($\hat{\beta}_S = -0.263$), though the estimate
is imprecise at $n = 94$ ($p = 0.397$). Warmth is not associated with SEMH
composition change in either sample. These findings are consistent with a
strictness-driven sorting mechanism, though they cannot rule out that strict
schools support SEMH pupils to exit the SEN register rather than preventing
their enrolment. Crucially, the SEMH-controlled robustness specification
(\cref{tab:robustness_overall}, column~6) adds the 2015--16 baseline SEMH
share as a covariate to the primary culture regression and finds that
$\hat{\beta}_S$ attenuates by only five per cent (from $0.120$ to $0.114$,
both significant at the one-per-cent level), indicating that whatever sorting
occurs accounts for a negligible fraction of the culture--progress association.

\subsection{Exploratory extensions}
\label{sec:p2_extensions}

Two further exploratory analyses use national datasets that fall outside the
gold-standard measurement tier.

\textit{Management discourse.}\ The interview transcript LLM management-discourse
score ($\tilde{M}$, rated 1--5 by the same LLM that scores warmth and
strictness in the extended transcript tier) is entered alongside transcript
warmth and strictness for the $n = 282$ schools with non-missing transcript

coefficient is consistently negative across all five outcome specifications

significant in any (all $p > 0.17$). Transcript warmth remains a positive

overall Progress~8), consistent with the enacted--espoused results above.

\input{tables/tab_management_discourse}

\textit{Website teaching philosophy.}\ School websites coded by the LLM as
``traditional'' or ``progressive'' in teaching philosophy are entered as dummies
against the unmarked baseline in the national sample ($n = 3{,}170$;
\cref{tab:teaching_philosophy}). The classification uses a stringent v3 rubric
requiring explicit, unambiguous pedagogical commitment---not incidental word
choice---classifying 111 schools as traditional and 34 as progressive across the
full website corpus, of which 101 and 32 respectively survive into the
estimation sample alongside 3,037 unmarked schools. Traditional schools score
$0.125$ Progress~8 points
higher than unmarked schools ($p = 0.006$), a substantially larger and more
consistent effect than the initial scoring suggested. The association is
statistically significant across all five outcome components (English: $0.128$,
$p = 0.008$; Maths: $0.113$, $p = 0.011$; EBaC: $0.146$, $p = 0.005$;
Open: $0.119$, $p = 0.019$). The progressive category contains only 32 schools
in the estimation sample, leaving the regression underpowered: the
overall progressive coefficient of $-0.085$ ($p = 0.233$) is imprecise and
only the Open component shows a marginal association ($-0.185$, $p = 0.062$).
These associations are consistent with the strictness findings but should be
treated with caution: the website LLM classification captures stated pedagogy,
not observed behaviour, and no gold-standard validation against visit-based
teaching scores is available at national scale.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:p2_conclusion}

This chapter set out to answer whether a school's organisational culture---as
measured through independent observation and structured interview with
headteachers---predicts the academic progress of its pupils, net of prior
attainment, intake composition, and school type. Using enacted warmth~$W$ and
strictness~$S$ scores derived from 102 researcher visits and 303 headteacher
interviews, the analysis estimated a three-stage OLS model on overall
Progress~8, its four component scores (English, Maths, EBaC, Open), and
Attainment~8 components for the 2023--24 cohort.

The Stage~1 regressions regress overall Progress~8 and each of its four
components on enacted warmth~$W$ and strictness~$S$ plus the full control set,
on the $n = 95$ schools with non-missing pre-COVID Ofsted grade
(\cref{tab:main_results_s1}). The headline finding is that both culture dimensions
significantly predict overall pupil progress: $\hat{\beta}_W = 0.127$
(95\%~CI [0.037, 0.216], $p = 0.006$) and $\hat{\beta}_S = 0.120$ ([0.027, 0.213],
$p = 0.013$), with $R^2 = 0.595$. The two coefficients are nearly equal in
magnitude, consistent with the theoretical prediction that warmth and strictness
make symmetric contributions to the school culture environment that supports
learning.

At the component level, warmth is positively and significantly associated with
English progress ($\hat{\beta}_W = 0.142$, [0.054, 0.229], $p = 0.002$), EBaC
progress ($\hat{\beta}_W = 0.146$, [0.027, 0.265], $p = 0.017$), and Open
progress ($\hat{\beta}_W = 0.124$, [0.020, 0.228], $p = 0.020$). Strictness is
positively and significantly associated with EBaC progress
($\hat{\beta}_S = 0.155$, [0.040, 0.270], $p = 0.009$), Open progress
($\hat{\beta}_S = 0.123$, [0.003, 0.242], $p = 0.044$), and Maths
($\hat{\beta}_S = 0.107$, [0.005, 0.208], $p = 0.040$). The EBaC component
shows the strongest response to culture, with both $W$ and $S$ significant at
the 5~per~cent level and comparable magnitude
($\hat{\beta}_W \approx \hat{\beta}_S \approx 0.15$). The Maths warmth
coefficient ($\hat{\beta}_W = 0.096$, $p = 0.100$) is the only instance where
warmth is not significant at conventional levels, consistent with the
theoretical prediction that structured disciplinary environment matters more for
the heavily assessed, sequentially cumulative Maths domain.

Stage~2 uses teaching quality~$T$ as the sole culture predictor, benchmarking
whether the $W + S$ effects could be driven by correlation with observable
instruction quality (\cref{tab:main_results_s2}). Teaching quality is
significantly positive for overall P8 ($\hat{\gamma}_T = 0.219$, $p < 0.001$)
and all four components: English ($\hat{\gamma}_T = 0.191$, $p = 0.001$),
Maths ($0.180$, $p = 0.005$), EBaC ($0.247$, $p = 0.001$), and
Open ($0.241$, $p < 0.001$). Stage~2 $R^2$ values (0.46--0.54) are somewhat lower
than Stage~1 (0.51--0.60), suggesting that the $W + S$ combination provides
stronger predictive signal for pupil progress than teaching quality alone.

Stage~3 adds teaching quality to the Stage~1 specification (\cref{tab:main_results_s3}).
The warmth and strictness coefficients are essentially unchanged. For overall P8,
both remain statistically significant: $\hat{\beta}_W = 0.119$ ($p = 0.038$) and
$\hat{\beta}_S = 0.116$ ($p = 0.016$), retaining 93~per~cent and 96~per~cent of
their Stage~1 magnitudes respectively. Across the four components, warmth retains
68--109~per~cent of its Stage~1 magnitude, and strictness retains 84--104~per~cent.
By contrast, teaching quality becomes negligible and statistically insignificant in
all five specifications once warmth and strictness are controlled
($\hat{\gamma}_T$ ranges from $-0.027$ to $+0.083$; all $p > 0.40$).
The near-zero attenuation implies that warmth and strictness do not predict pupil
progress through the teaching quality channel as captured in the current visit
protocol. An alternative reading---that $T$ is the noisiest of the three visit
dimensions, so its signal is absorbed into $W$ and $S$ through correlated
measurement error---cannot be excluded with cross-sectional data.

The validity of the enacted culture measures is further confirmed by comparing
them with interview-only (espoused) scores on the same 102 schools
(\cref{tab:enacted_espoused}). Replacing visit-based warmth ($W_{12}$) and
strictness ($S_{12}$) with their interview-based equivalents ($W_3^*$ and
$\bar{S}_{34}$) reduces warmth coefficients to near-zero and statistical
insignificance across all four components: interview-only warmth coefficients
range from $-0.007$ to $0.054$, compared with $0.096$--$0.146$ for enacted
warmth---a reduction of 63--106~per~cent, the upper bound reflecting a sign
reversal in the Open component. Espoused strictness retains somewhat more
predictive power but is less precisely estimated and insignificant in all
four components. This pattern is consistent with the systematic over-claiming of
warmth documented above: interview-based warmth scores are compressed toward the
ceiling, attenuating the coefficient toward zero. The enacted--espoused gap is
smallest for strictness, which also shows the smallest visit--interview bias,
reinforcing the interpretation that norm-governed disciplinary behaviour is more
faithfully self-reported than relational warmth.

The measurement design reveals a systematic asymmetry in how headteachers
describe their school's culture. Comparing enacted scores from school visit
observations to the corresponding interview sub-scores across the 102
full-data schools, headteachers over-claim warmth by an average of $+0.45$
score points ($W_{\text{visit}}$ mean~$= 6.62$ versus $W_{\text{interview}}$
mean~$= 7.06$, $r = 0.22$) and over-claim teaching quality by $+0.71$ points
($T_{\text{visit}}$ mean~$= 6.74$ versus $T_{\text{interview}}$
mean~$= 7.44$, $r = 0.20$). By contrast, the strictness gap is negligible
($\Delta \approx 0.01$, $r = 0.18$), consistent with strictness being a more
observable and less socially sensitive dimension of school culture that
headteachers assess accurately without recourse to self-presentation. The
modest visit--interview correlations (0.18--0.22 across all three dimensions)
confirm that enacted and espoused culture are related but meaningfully
distinct constructs: a school's culture as observed and as narrated by its
leaders are not interchangeable measures.

Three limitations bear on the interpretation of the results. First,
identification is correlational: the analysis cannot rule out that an
unmeasured third variable---community character, governing body composition,
or persistent leadership quality---drives both the culture scores and the
progress outcomes. The pre-COVID Ofsted grade control provides partial
protection against time-invariant confounders captured in prior inspection
judgements, but it is not a causal instrument. Second, for the Tier~2
(interview-only) and Tier~3 (national) schools, scores capture espoused
culture more than enacted culture. The systematic biases documented in
\cref{sec:p1_convergence} of \cref{ch:paper1}---headteachers over-claiming
warmth relative to independent observation---mean that extended- and
national-tier estimates are expected to be attenuated toward zero for warmth,
beyond what LOO-CV noise alone would predict. Researchers and policymakers
should interpret any tier-2 or tier-3 warmth estimates as lower bounds on the
true enacted-culture effect. Third, score compression limits statistical
power. Approximately 68~per cent of English state secondary schools are rated
Good and 15~per cent Outstanding by Ofsted, reflecting genuine homogeneity
driven by the shared national curriculum, inspection isomorphism, and common
training pipelines. The restricted range of the culture scores---itself partly
a substantive empirical finding rather than purely a measurement artefact---
implies that the estimated coefficient on a one-unit culture shift maps onto
a small inter-school contrast. The effect of moving from the 25th to the 75th
percentile of the warmth or strictness distribution is inherently small in a
compressed distribution; modest coefficient estimates should be read
accordingly.

The findings have implications for school improvement policy. If enacted
warmth and strictness independently predict pupil progress, then school
culture is a tractable lever for policy, not merely a background condition.
Professional development programmes that improve relational quality between
teachers and pupils, and behaviour frameworks that deliver consistent
structure without crossing into punitive exclusion, may produce progress
returns that complement curriculum and instruction interventions. The
stability of the culture effect across Stages~1 and~3---warmth and strictness
retain near-full coefficient magnitude even after conditioning on teaching
quality---suggests these dimensions predict progress through channels that
are largely distinct from instruction quality as measured in the current visit
protocol, so programmes targeting culture and instruction simultaneously may
generate complementary rather than overlapping effects.

Several extensions would strengthen the causal architecture of these
findings. First, exploiting within-school variation in culture over time---
once a longer series of interview and visit data is available---would remove
the confounding by fixed school characteristics that cross-sectional OLS
cannot purge. Second, the gap between espoused and enacted culture identified
above is itself a measure of cultural coherence: schools where headteacher
self-assessment closely matches independent observation may achieve more
consistent implementation of their stated values, generating better outcomes
through coherence rather than through the level of warmth or strictness per
se. Third, heterogeneity by school type, intake deprivation, and prior Ofsted
grade warrants exploration: the theoretical prediction is that warmth effects
are largest in schools serving high proportions of pupils with Social,
Emotional and Mental Health needs, where relational pastoral support generates
the greatest marginal value. Fourth, testing whether strictness scores predict
changes in school-level SEMH composition over time would provide evidence on
a sorting mechanism---a school whose behaviour culture is strongly punitive
would, under selective exclusion, show declining SEMH prevalence as affected
pupils leave, indicating that culture shapes school composition as well as
pupil progress within a given composition. Fifth, the enacted--espoused
divergence documented here and in \cref{ch:paper1} motivates further work
on the optimal weighting of visit and interview components, and on whether
the predictive validity of the composite score can be improved by
down-weighting espoused components for dimensions---particularly warmth---
where systematic positive bias is largest.

\section{Structural Stability of School Characteristics}
\label{app:3A:stability}

The intra-class correlation (ICC) decomposes total variance in a panel variable
into a between-school component and a within-school over-time component. An ICC
close to one indicates that schools are much more different from each other than
they are from their own past selves: year-on-year within-school fluctuation is
small relative to persistent inter-school heterogeneity. ICC is estimated by
one-way random-effects ANOVA on the school-year panel, retaining only schools
with at least three years of non-missing data for the variable in question.

\Cref{tab:structural_stability} reports ICC estimates and mean absolute
year-on-year changes for nine panel variables covering pupil intake composition,
special educational needs prevalence, prior attainment, pupil progress, and
per-pupil expenditure. Six of nine variables exceed ICC~$= 0.69$; all
exceed~$0.49$. Intake characteristics---the share of pupils with English as an
Additional Language, school roll, and mean KS2 prior attainment of the entering
cohort---are especially stable, with ICC values between 0.81 and 0.92 and mean
absolute year-on-year changes of less than four per cent of the variable's mean.
Special educational needs prevalence (EHC plan share, ICC~$= 0.67$; SEN support
share, ICC~$= 0.65$) and school-level Progress~8 (ICC~$= 0.73$) show moderate to
high stability. Per-pupil expenditure, at ICC~$= 0.52$, is the most volatile,
reflecting sensitivity to national funding policy changes; even so, the majority
of variation in expenditure is between schools rather than within schools over
time.

These patterns are consistent with the view that schools occupy relatively
persistent positions in the distribution of structural characteristics, and
support the use of culture scores measured in 2024--25 as proxies for the
organisational conditions present during the 2023--24 outcome period
(\cref{sec:p2_outcomes}).

\Cref{tab:sen_category_stability} extends the analysis to the thirteen
primary-need categories within the SEN population. ICC values range from
0.320 (Severe Learning Difficulty) to 0.643 (Moderate Learning Difficulty);
the educationally important Social, Emotional and Mental Health (SEMH)
category has ICC~$= 0.545$. The lower stability of SEMH relative to the
aggregate SEN support share (ICC~$= 0.65$) is consistent with SEMH composition
being partly responsive to school behaviour and exclusion practices, rather than
being determined solely by fixed intake characteristics. Low ICCs for rare
categories (Severe Learning Difficulty, Multi-Sensory Impairment, Visual
Impairment) partly reflect small-count measurement noise rather than genuine
volatility. For the main analysis, aggregate SEN controls are preferred to
category-level decompositions; the moderate stability of baseline SEMH share
supports its inclusion as an additional control in the robustness specification
described in \cref{sec:p2_specs}.

\input{tables/tab_structural_stability}

\input{tables/tab_sen_category_stability}

\Cref{tab:p8_component_stability} reports ICC estimates separately for the four
Progress~8 components that serve as the primary outcomes of this chapter,
together with the corresponding Attainment~8 components used in the
contemporaneous robustness specification. Among the Progress~8 components,
stability varies from ICC~$= 0.587$ for the Open component to ICC~$= 0.748$
for EBaC, with English and Maths sitting between these at ICC~$\approx 0.67$.
The higher stability of EBaC relative to Open reflects the fact that
curriculum-pathway decisions---which subjects pupils are entered for---are
governed by stable school policy and change little from year to year, while
the more eclectic Open bucket (arts, vocational, and additional GCSEs) is
subject to greater annual variation in subject mix. All four P8 components
exceed the per-pupil expenditure ICC of~$0.52$, which is the weakest result
in the main stability table; the cross-sectional design is therefore at least
as defensible for each component individually as for the structural
characteristics already discussed.

The theoretical prediction of this chapter is that warmth is most strongly
associated with the Open component---the dimension most sensitive to breadth
of provision and pupil engagement---and that strictness is most strongly
associated with the EBaC component---the dimension most sensitive to academic
pathway steering. The lower stability of Open P8 relative to EBaC P8
(ICC~$= 0.587$ versus $0.748$) implies that the warmth coefficient will be
estimated against a noisier outcome, which may attenuate the estimated effect.
This is a limitation to acknowledge in interpreting any differential finding
between components.

Attainment~8 component ICCs substantially exceed their Progress~8 counterparts
across all four components, ranging from ICC~$= 0.750$ (Open) to
ICC~$= 0.887$ (EBaC). This pattern is expected: Attainment~8 measures absolute
performance without subtracting KS2 prior attainment, so the stable intake
signal---itself at ICC~$= 0.91$ for the KS2 scaled score---is retained in the
dependent variable and inflates between-school variance relative to
within-school variance. The higher Attainment~8 ICCs therefore do not indicate
that value-added is more stable than raw attainment; they reflect the
composition of the variance rather than the reliability of the school-level
signal.

\Cref{tab:score_controls_corr} reports pairwise Pearson correlations between
the three visit-based culture scores ($W_{\text{visit}}$,
$S_{\text{visit}}$, $T_{\text{visit}}$) and the composite scores
($W_{\text{comp}}$, $S_{\text{comp}}$) against each control variable,
estimated on the Tier~1 sample of 102 visited schools. No pairwise
correlation exceeds $|r| = 0.26$. The largest entries are between
$T_{\text{visit}}$ and KS2 prior attainment ($r = +0.26$), and between
$W_{\text{visit}}$ and EAL share ($r = -0.25$) and selective school status
($r = -0.23$). The absence of high correlations indicates that the culture
scores are not collinear with the observable school characteristics included
in the regression, and that selective and grammar schools are modestly but
not substantially lower-scoring on warmth even after accounting for intake
characteristics in the main specification.

\Cref{tab:continuity_robustness} reports the primary Stage~1 specification
side-by-side for the full Tier~1 sample ($n = 95$) and for the subset of
schools where the headteacher named in the most recent Ofsted inspection report
is confirmed still in post in the July~2026 GIAS snapshot ($n = 23$). The
restriction is driven as much by data coverage as by turnover: of the 102
full-data schools, continuity could be determined for only 30, of which 23 were
unchanged and 7 had changed. Given the
small $n$, coefficients in the continuity subsample are imprecisely estimated
(SEs approximately four times larger than in the full sample), and neither
warmth nor strictness achieves statistical significance in any component.
Strictness point estimates are directionally consistent with---and in several
cases larger than---the full-sample estimates ($\hat{\beta}_S$ ranging from
0.207 to 0.333), while warmth estimates are noisy and should not be
interpreted directionally. This check does not provide evidence against the
primary results; the subsample is insufficiently powered to detect effects of
the magnitude identified in the full specification.
