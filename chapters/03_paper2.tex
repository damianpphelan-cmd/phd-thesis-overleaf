\begin{chapterabstract}
This paper asks whether the culture of a school affects how much progress its pupils make. I measure the warmth and strictness of \NTierOne{} English secondary schools by direct observation, and relate those scores to Progress~8, the Department for Education's measure of pupil progress. The estimates are associations, with intake, school characteristics and the pre-pandemic inspection grade held constant. There are four main results. First, both dimensions are associated with progress: a standard deviation of warmth goes with 0.15 of a grade more progress, and a standard deviation of strictness with 0.11. Second, the quality of the teaching observed does not account for either association. Third, the two dimensions add rather than amplify each other, and the schools in the top half on both dimensions outperform the rest by two-fifths of a GCSE grade per subject. The warmth association also depends on the observation: substituting the headteacher's own account of the school for the scores removes it. Fourth, the results extend nationally: culture scores read from inspection reports predict progress across effectively all English state secondary schools. A standard deviation of the strictness reading goes with 0.13 of a grade, and of the warmth reading with 0.08.
\end{chapterabstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:p2_intro}

This paper asks whether the culture of a school affects how much progress
its pupils make. I estimate the association between the warmth and strictness of
English secondary schools, measured by direct observation, and the progress their pupils make between the end of primary school and their GCSEs. Warmth is the quality of the relationships between adults and pupils, in lessons and around the school. Strictness is the orderliness of the school and how consistently its behaviour rules are enforced. These are the two dimensions of culture throughout the chapter. I use the enacted culture scores that were constructed in the previous chapter for the \NTierOne{} visited schools, enacted meaning built from what the observers saw rather than from what the school says about itself, together with the Progress~8 measure
published by the Department for Education, which records each school's
average pupil progress relative to pupils nationally with the same prior
attainment.

Whether the school a child attends makes a difference beyond the ability and family circumstances the child arrives with, and if so through what mechanism, is a long-standing question. The early answers were negative.
\citet{coleman1966} found that school resources explained little of the
variation in attainment once family background was taken into account, and
\citet{hanushek1997}, reviewing the evidence three decades later, found no consistent relationship between expenditure and performance.

The class-size experiments and natural experiments that followed
\citep{krueger1999,krueger2001,angrist1999} showed that resources can
matter, and English evidence finds small positive effects of spending that
are concentrated among disadvantaged pupils
\citep{levacic2005,jenkins2006,holmlund2010,machin2010}. However, these
effects are modest when set against the size of the school-to-school
variation that they leave unexplained.

Two lines of work have looked for the source of those school-to-school differences in something other than resources. \citet{rutter1979}, studying twelve inner-London
secondary schools, placed it in what they called ethos, meaning the
expectations and routine practice that a school sustains, and the
effective-schools syntheses that followed listed an orderly environment and
a supportive climate among its correlates
\citep{edmonds1979,sammons1995,teddlie2000}. The teacher-quality literature
placed it in the classroom: \citet{rivkin2005} and \citet{chetty2014} show
that variation in teacher effectiveness is large and consequential, and
English estimates \citep{slater2012,murphy2011} are of the same order. What
neither line of work settles is whether a school's culture, as distinct from
the quality of its individual teachers, predicts how much progress its pupils
make, and that is the question here.

Two strands of the literature are closest to this paper. The first is the
authoritative-school literature \citep{pellerin2005,gregory2010}, which
builds on the parenting typology of \citet{maccoby1983}, in which the combination of responsiveness and demandingness predicts the best child outcomes \citep{majumder2015,zhang2017}. Warmth and strictness are the school-level counterparts of that pair, and the literatures that follow each have their own names for the same two things: responsiveness, support and warmth on one side; demandingness, structure and discipline on the other. This chapter uses warmth and strictness throughout. The literature finds that schools which combine the two produce better behavioural and engagement outcomes, but it has rarely had an academic-progress outcome or a direct measure of both dimensions.

The second strand is the literature on high-expectation, high-structure charter and academy models, which finds
large effects of a bundle of practices. In the United States, effective
charter schools are marked by strict discipline and high expectations
\citep{abdulkadiroglu2011,angrist2010}; an index of five such practices
explains about half of the variation in charter effectiveness, while class
size and spending explain none of it \citep{dobbie2013}; and the bundle
transplanted into ordinary public schools raises mathematics attainment
\citep{fryer2014}. \citet{chabrier2016} and \citet{epple2016} review the wider evidence. Most charter schools do no better than the ordinary public schools their pupils would otherwise have attended, and the effective ones are again those with strict discipline and high expectations.

In England, the first wave of academies, which targeted under-performing schools and gave them autonomy,
raised attainment \citep{machin2011,eyles2016}. The later converter academies show much weaker effects \citep{eyles2018}. This suggests that
autonomy itself is not the operative channel. \citet{machin2020} show that the early academies' higher exclusion rates reflected a change in discipline rather than the removal of weak pupils. What the improving academies demonstrably changed, then, was behaviour, which is the strictness dimension this paper measures directly.

In all of this work, warmth and strictness are never observed separately. An effective charter school of this kind is strict and warm at once: tight behaviour rules, and adults who know every pupil individually. The studies compare schools that run the whole package with schools that do not, so they can say that the package works, but not which part of it does. This paper separates the parts. It measures warmth and strictness as two scores, in ordinary schools rather than in schools built around the package, and asks what each contributes on its own.

The estimates are associations, not causal effects, and every one is presented as such. What the design does is narrow the list of other explanations. The outcome is value-added, so the largest confound in any school comparison, that stronger schools take stronger pupils, is removed at the start. The regressions then hold constant intake composition, prior attainment, school type, size, location and the school's pre-pandemic inspection grade, so a culture coefficient cannot be a disguised effect of who attends the school or of how it was regarded before the outcome cohorts arrived. The sample excludes schools that admit pupils only at 13 or 14, whose published progress partly belongs to the schools their pupils came from. And the associations reported below appear again across the national population, on text-derived scores, so they are not a property of the hundred schools that agreed to be visited. The one thing the design cannot rule out is a persistent characteristic of a school that produces both its culture and its results.

There are four main results. First, both dimensions are associated with progress. A standard deviation of enacted warmth goes with Progress~8 that is $0.15$ of a grade higher; a standard deviation of enacted strictness goes with $0.11$ of a grade. The two estimates are close in size, and the difference between them is not statistically significant, so the data do not rank the two dimensions. Both hold however the inspection-grade control is handled.

Behind those similar sizes the two associations behave differently. The strictness association is visible with no controls at all: stricter schools simply make more progress. The warmth association is not. At first sight warmth appears unrelated to progress, and the reason is an intake pattern that hides it. Schools with many pupils who speak English as an additional language tend to make more progress, and the observers scored those schools lower on warmth. Comparing all schools together therefore mixes the warmth association with an intake pattern that pulls the opposite way, and the two roughly cancel. Compare schools with similar intakes and the warmth association emerges. Once it does, an unmeasured confounder would have to be stronger to explain it away than to explain away the strictness association.

The two dimensions also carry separate information. Entered on its own, each has a coefficient of about $0.2$. Entered together, each falls, but neither falls to zero, so each is associated with progress in a way the other does not capture.

Second, conditioning on enacted teaching quality does not account for the association. Adding the visit's teaching score leaves both culture coefficients essentially unchanged, and the teaching coefficient itself is close to zero. This matters because teaching quality is the most obvious rival explanation: a warm and orderly school might simply be a school whose teachers teach well. On this evidence, the culture association is not teaching quality under another name.

Third, the schools in the top half on both warmth and strictness outperform the rest by two-fifths of a GCSE grade per subject for every pupil, while the interaction between the two dimensions is indistinguishable from
zero in the visited sample. In other words, the dimensions add to one
another rather than amplifying each other. The warmth that predicts progress is the warmth the observers saw, not the warmth the head described. Use the headteacher's own account of the school instead of the enacted scores and the association disappears.

The fourth result is the national extension. Through the previous chapter's text-derived scores, predicted warmth, predicted teaching and mark-scheme strictness each predict progress across the national population. The associations hold with the inspection grade held constant, and they hold on reports written before either outcome cohort's GCSE results existed, so they are neither the grade restated nor inspectors describing results they already knew.

The next section describes the data. The paper then sets out the
specification and what it can and cannot identify, reports the results and
their robustness, and concludes.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:p2_data}

\textbf{Outcomes.} The outcome is Progress~8, which is the Department for
Education's school-level value-added measure. For each pupil, attainment
across eight GCSE slots, weighted towards English and Maths, is compared with
the national average attainment of pupils who left primary school with the
same Key Stage~2 result, and the differences are then averaged across the
school's cohort. A school at zero is exactly average for its intake. A score
of $+0.5$ means that its pupils achieved half a grade more per subject than
pupils elsewhere with the same starting point, and $-0.5$ means half a grade
less. Nationally the measure has a standard deviation across schools of
about $0.5$ and an interquartile range of about two-thirds of a grade. This
means that the difference between a school at the first quartile and one at
the third is roughly two-thirds of a grade in every subject for every pupil.

The outcome is a value-added measure, and value-added measures have their
critics. \citet{gorard2010,gorard2013} argue that school-level estimates are
dominated by error and unstable from year to year. I use Progress~8
nonetheless, for three reasons. It is the measure that the Department for
Education publishes and by which schools are judged; it conditions on the
single strongest predictor of GCSE performance, which is prior attainment at
Key Stage~2; and it allows the visited schools to be placed against every
school in the country on the same footing. I meet the concern about
instability by averaging two cohorts and by showing, in the appendix, that
a school's Progress~8 is stable across years (\cref{app:3A:stability})
relative to the differences between schools.

I report overall Progress~8 and its English and Maths components, which are
the cleanest measures the tables offer because every pupil sits both of
those examinations. The remaining components, EBacc and Open, are partly
determined by which subjects a school enters its pupils for, and they are
reported in \cref{app:3A:tables} together with a direct analysis of subject
entry rates; \cref{app:3A:p8mechanics} sets out how the components are
constructed.

The primary outcome is the two-year average of the 2022/23 and
2023/24 cohorts, which are the two post-pandemic years for which a Key
Stage~2 baseline exists. Averaging the two reduces the year-to-year noise
in a single cohort's figure, and it also dilutes any cohort-specific
disruption: the Department advised caution over the 2022/23 measures
because of the pandemic's effect on that cohort. The 2024/25 cohort has no baseline, because its
Key Stage~2 tests were cancelled in 2020, and \cref{app:3A:tables} reports a
pseudo-progress measure for it that correlates $+0.81$ with real
Progress~8.

\textbf{Culture measures.} The explanatory variables of interest are the enacted warmth and strictness scores of the previous chapter, measured on a 0--10 scale for the \NTierOne{} visited schools. They are
entered per standard deviation so that the two coefficients are comparable.
Across the visited schools enacted warmth has a mean of $6.4$ and a standard
deviation of $0.9$, and enacted strictness a mean of $7.0$ and a standard
deviation of $0.9$, so a standard deviation is about one point on the
ten-point scale.

Two properties of these scores, which were established in
the previous chapter, bear on how the estimates below should be read. The
first is that a school's score is an average over lessons that differ from
one another more than schools differ from each other. Within-school lesson
variation is about three times the between-school variation for in-lesson
strictness. This means that ``a strict school'' here is a school whose
lessons are orderly on average, and not one in which every lesson is.

The second is that the scores carry measurement error, with a reliability
of about 0.7. For a score entered on its own, error of this kind biases the coefficient towards zero, so those estimates are conservative. Entered together, the two scores behave less simply.
 Warmth and strictness are correlated, so the regression's job is to decide how much of the joint association belongs to each. Noise interferes with exactly that decision: when one score is measured with more error than the other, some of its association is credited to the other score instead. So when the two scores enter together, the error makes the division between the two coefficients uncertain: part of the warmth association can appear as strictness, and the reverse. The error cannot produce the overall association. It moves estimates towards zero, so if anything the association between culture and progress is understated. About a third
of the observed lessons were rated by one researcher rather than two, and the
robustness section checks that the estimates do not depend on those schools.

The visit's teaching-practice score, built from the ten lesson items on questioning, feedback, explanation and structure, enters as the rival explanation to be tested. It
is the most obvious rival explanation for any association between culture
and progress, since a school whose teachers are warm and orderly may also be
a school whose teachers teach well. The espoused scores, from the headteacher
interview, enter one robustness specification.

Three text-derived scores
enter the national extension, all built and validated in the previous
chapter and all reading the school's most recent inspection report with
its stated grades removed. Warmth and teaching come from prediction
models: each learns, from the visited schools, which words and phrases of
a report go with higher enacted scores, and every national school's
prediction comes from a model that never saw it. Both models are trained
on the part of the enacted score that intake statistics cannot explain,
so they earn no credit for recognising demographics. Their held-out
correlations with the enacted scores are $\PredWOfsted$ for warmth and
$\PredTOfsted$ for teaching. Strictness is read differently: a one-page mark scheme applied once per report by a language model, frozen before any scoring. Its bands correlate with
enacted strictness at $\RubricSOfstedRaw$.

One further website-derived measure enters an exploratory section only: an indicator of a traditional pedagogy.

The previous chapter explains why the two dimensions are read differently: warmth shows in a report's everyday wording, which counting can pick up, while strictness sits in specific claims that a reader has to be told to look for. \Cref{fig:national_bands}, reported with the national extension, shows the national distribution of the strictness bands.

\textbf{Controls.} All specifications condition on the same set of
controls. The first is the mean Key Stage~2 score of the cohort. Progress~8 already compares each pupil with pupils who left primary school at the same level, so intake should not matter to it, but the adjustment may be imperfect. The control protects the estimates if it is. The next are the shares of pupils eligible for free school meals, with English as an additional language (EAL) and with special educational needs, which are the composition measures most strongly associated with progress.

Then comes school size, logged so that the control compares proportional rather than absolute differences in size; academy status, urban location and selective admissions; and
the number of years since the school's last inspection, since a school that
was inspected long ago is described by an older report.

Finally, the school's overall inspection grade as at August 2019 enters as indicators for grades below Good,
pooling Outstanding with Good. Outstanding schools were exempt from
routine inspection before 2020, so the Outstanding label carries the
oldest information in the variable. I use the 2019 grade rather than the current one because a
contemporaneous grade is partly an outcome of the culture being measured. I
enter it at all because it is the best available summary of how the school
was regarded before the outcome
cohorts arrived.

Seven of the 103 visited schools carry no 2019 grade under their current identity in the Department's records, mostly because a school that converts to an academy is registered afresh under a new reference number, and the old grade stays with the old registration. In these cases the school itself usually continues, with the same site and pupils, and its earlier registration is recorded as its predecessor. One of the seven is excluded from the outcome regressions anyway as a late-entry school (schools admitting only from age 13; defined below), and one lacks the years-since-inspection control, so five remain in the estimation sample. For four of the five, the predecessor's 2019 grade fills the gap. The fifth has no predecessor recorded in the Department's register, so no 2019 grade exists to inherit, and it is dropped where the grade control is required.

\textbf{Sample.} The estimation sample starts from the 103 visited schools, and three exclusions reduce it. The first is schools whose statutory entry age is 13 or above (university technical colleges, studio schools and
similar; 98 schools nationally, two of them among those visited), which are
excluded from every outcome regression. Progress~8 measures progress from
age 11, and these schools enrol their pupils at 13 or 14, so their figure
attributes to them progress that was made elsewhere. The second is one visited school that has never been inspected under its current registration, so the years-since-inspection control cannot be computed for it. The third is the school with no recorded predecessor, described above. The
primary estimation sample is therefore $n = \PrimarySpecN$: 103 schools,
less the two late-entry schools, one missing control and one unfillable
grade.

\Cref{tab:outcome_stats}
and \cref{tab:control_stats} report descriptive statistics for the outcomes
and the controls. The visited schools' mean Progress~8 is about a quarter of
a grade above zero, with a standard deviation a little below the national
one, which is the restricted range noted in the previous chapter.

The visited schools are stronger on average than the national population, so the estimates apply most directly to a population somewhat above the national average. The sample also contains few very weak schools, so both the culture scores and Progress~8 cover a narrower range than they do nationally. An association is estimated from differences between schools, so when the schools differ less there is less for the regression to work with, and a real association becomes harder to detect. If anything, then, this feature of the sample understates the associations; it cannot have created them. Two checks on the sample follow. The first asks whether the visited schools look like other English secondary schools. The second asks whether the culture scores simply track the schools' intakes.

On the first, the visited schools are close on the controls to the interviewed schools, the larger pool of 303 from which they were recruited (the descriptive tables report both groups), and to the country as a whole: 29 per cent of pupils eligible for free school meals, 16 per cent with special educational needs, three-quarters academies, nine in ten urban, one in twenty selective, and a 2019 grade distribution of roughly a quarter Outstanding, five-eighths Good and one in nine below Good. The one visible difference is the share of pupils with English as an additional language: 28 per cent, against 21 per cent in the interviewed sample. The difference reflects the London weighting, and the share is controlled for throughout.

On the second, the correlations between the culture scores and the controls, reported in \cref{tab:score_controls_corr}, are all below $|0.3|$. The culture measures are not close proxies for intake or school type, so the coefficients below cannot simply be reflecting differences in pupil composition.

\section{Empirical Specification}
\label{sec:p2_spec}

The primary specification is
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:main}
  Y_s = \alpha + \beta_W W_s + \beta_S S_s + \mathbf{X}_s'\gamma
        + \sum_g \delta_g \,\mathbb{1}[G_s = g] + \varepsilon_s,
\end{equation}
%
where $Y_s$ is the two-year average Progress~8 (overall, English or Maths)
of school $s$, $W_s$ and $S_s$ are its enacted warmth and strictness scores
in standard-deviation units, $\mathbf{X}_s$ is the set of controls listed
above and $G_s$ is its 2019 inspection grade. Standard errors are
heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC3), which is the small-sample variant that
is appropriate when $n$ is near 100.

I estimate three stages:
equation~(\ref{eq:main}); the same equation with the teaching-practice score
$T_s$ in place of $W_s$ and $S_s$; and the equation with all three. Each dimension is also entered on its own: warmth without strictness, and strictness without warmth, in separate regressions.

The national extension uses the same equation on the national sample, with a text-derived score in place of $W_s$ and $S_s$. There are three such scores from the previous chapter: predicted warmth, predicted teaching, and the mark-scheme strictness bands. Each score is divided by its standard deviation, so its coefficient reads as grades of Progress~8 per standard deviation, like the visited coefficients. Each score then enters a separate regression of Progress~8 on that score and the full control set, run on every school with an outcome. Each of the three regressions is estimated twice: once with the 2019 grade dummies among the controls and once without them.

The estimates are associations rather than causal effects, and what the design does and does not achieve can be stated exactly. Because the outcome is value-added, the
comparison is between schools whose pupils started from the same place. This
means that the first and largest confound in any school-effects estimate,
namely that stronger schools take stronger pupils, is removed by
construction, and the Key Stage~2 control guards against what remains.
Half of the visits took place during 2023/24, the second outcome cohort's
own year, and half in the year after it. The timing therefore does not
rule out ordinary reverse causation: good results may produce a warm and
orderly school.

What the design relies on instead is that culture is persistent, so that
what was observed in 2023/24 and 2024/25 is a reasonable measure of what
the 2022/23 and 2023/24 cohorts experienced. This is an assumption, and it is not tested directly. No school was visited twice, so the persistence of culture itself is never measured. The evidence that can be offered is indirect, and it comes in three parts.

The first is stability. A school's
Progress~8, and the structural characteristics that shape it, are stable
across years relative to the differences between schools, which is consistent with stable culture without establishing it.

The second is the contemporaneous cohort. The 2024/25 cohort, the one measured at the same time as the fieldwork, gives coefficients close to the primary ones on its pseudo-progress measure. That check is reported with the robustness results.

The third comes from the national extension: old inspection reports predict progress almost as well as recent ones. Some schools were last inspected many years ago, so their reports describe an older version of the school; if culture drifted quickly, those reports should predict today's progress much worse than fresh ones do. They predict only slightly worse: each additional year since a report was written weakens the association between strictness and progress by about $0.003$ ($p = 0.09$). The weakening is treated as real rather than dismissed, but it is small: at that rate, ten years of report age reduce the association by about $0.03$.

Two features of the design also limit what the estimates could be picking up besides culture. Because the 2019 grade is held constant, the comparison is between schools that the inspectorate regarded alike before the outcome cohorts arrived. This matters because reputation is itself a route to results: a school with a good name attracts committed staff and motivated families, and those arrivals could raise results whatever the school's culture is like. The grade is the most visible summary of a school's standing, so holding it constant absorbs the part of that route the grade captures, though not any part the grade misses. Finally, because the sample excludes schools that enrol at 13 or 14, no
school is credited with progress that was made at another school.

What the design cannot do is rule out the possibility that some persistent
characteristic of a school produces both its culture and its results. This might be a headteacher who is effective in ways that the visit does not measure, or the part of a school's reputation that the grade does not capture.

The data cannot rule this out, but they can test parts of it. The teaching-practice score is observed alongside the culture scores, so the most obvious such characteristic, that warm and orderly schools are simply schools whose teachers teach well, can be tested directly rather than assumed away. And the associations are not confined to the visited schools. They appear again across the national population, where culture is read from inspection reports rather than visits, and they hold with the inspection grade held constant. The schools that agreed to be visited cannot therefore be the source of the result.

One sorting concern remains. Pupils with social, emotional and mental-health needs tend to make less measured progress. If strict schools retain fewer of them, whether through exclusion or through families leaving, their measured progress would rise without their culture improving anything. A measure of each school's share of such pupils taken before the outcome years tests this route, in the robustness section.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:p2_results}

\subsection{Estimates on the visited schools}

\Cref{tab:spec_ladder} reports the primary estimates, which show that culture is associated with progress. In the primary specification enacted warmth has a
coefficient of $\hat{\beta}_W = \PrimarySpecW$ ($p = 0.001$) and enacted strictness a coefficient of $\hat{\beta}_S = \PrimarySpecS$ ($p = 0.012$) on overall Progress~8. Warmth is the larger of the two
coefficients, although the difference between them is not itself
significant ($p = \WaldWSP$). The table shows the same equation
under four treatments of the grade control, from requiring an original 2019
grade ($n = 95$) to dropping the control altogether ($n = 100$), and the
coefficients move by no more than $0.02$ across the four. Both dimensions
contribute, as the authoritative-school framework predicts.

\input{tables/tab_spec_ladder}

To give these magnitudes scale: a standard deviation of enacted warmth is about one point on the ten-point scale, and the associated $0.15$ of a grade is about a third of a standard deviation of school Progress~8 nationally. Two standard deviations on both dimensions together go with about half a grade per subject per pupil, the size of difference that separates a well-above-average school from an average one in the Department for Education's published performance tables.

Each dimension, entered on its own, has a coefficient of about $0.2$: \cref{tab:univariate_ws} reports $0.219$ for warmth and $0.199$ for strictness.
When the two enter jointly, warmth keeps two-thirds of its solo coefficient
and strictness about half. The two scores overlap, as their correlation of $\CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$ implies, but each keeps a substantial association of its own. If the two scores measured one underlying quality rather than two, then entered together one of them would do all the work and the other's coefficient would fall to zero. Neither falls to zero, so the two behave as separate dimensions. That correlation, though, partly reflects how the scores were made: the same observers scored both dimensions at the same school on the same day, which pushes the two scores together. The previous chapter's estimate of the correlation between the dimensions themselves, with that pushed-together part stripped out, is about \CorrWSTrue{}, so the case for treating them as two separate things is stronger still.

\input{tables/tab_univariate_ws}

The controls play different roles for the two dimensions. With no controls at all, warmth has a coefficient of
\SensShortW{} on the same schools ($p = \SensShortWP$) and strictness
\SensShortS{} ($p = \SensShortSP$): unconditionally, the strict
schools show the clearer progress advantage. Adding the share of
pupils with English as an additional language, and nothing else, moves the
warmth coefficient to \SensShortEalW{} and the strictness coefficient to
\SensShortEalS{}, which is close to the full-specification split
(the no-control estimates are tabulated with the sensitivity analysis, \cref{tab:sensitivity_bounds}).

The reason is that schools with many EAL pupils make more progress on average
and are scored somewhat lower on enacted warmth, so a comparison that
ignores intake composition hides the warmth association. The warmth result
is therefore a conditional one. It appears when schools are compared with
others of similar intake, which is the comparison the specification is built to make.

This conditionality matters when reading the sensitivity analysis below. The lower warmth
scores of high-EAL schools could partly reflect how observers read warmth
in different contexts rather than the schools themselves. The regression cannot tell these two cases apart. In the first, the scores are accurate: high-EAL schools really are, on average, less warm as the items measure it, and comparing schools of similar intake then reveals a true association between warmth and progress. In the second, the observers read warmth differently in high-EAL contexts, so those schools' low scores partly reflect the scoring rather than the schools. The adjusted association then mixes a fact about the schools with a correction for how the observers scored.

At the level of the components, warmth is significant for both subjects
(English $0.162$, $p < 0.001$; Maths $0.135$, $p = 0.011$), while
strictness falls short of significance in each taken alone (English
$0.071$, $p = 0.11$; Maths $0.076$, $p = 0.09$). Overall Progress~8 is not
the sum of these two components: it also counts the EBacc and Open slots,
and it is there that the strictness association is clearest (EBacc $0.138$,
$p = 0.008$; Open $0.119$, $p = 0.035$). Those two components mix progress with the school's entry decisions, so part of the overall strictness association could come from stricter schools entering pupils for different subjects rather than from their pupils progressing more. Testing that route means first showing that stricter schools do enter pupils differently, and the visited sample is too small to show it: there, strictness does not significantly predict EBacc entry. The national sample introduced below is large enough, and the entry analysis reported with the national extension finds the route to be small. The full component
estimates and the entry analysis are in \cref{app:3A:tables}.

\Cref{tab:stages23_trio} reports the second and third stages, which show that teaching quality does not account for the association. On its own, the visit's
teaching-practice score predicts overall Progress~8 at $0.206$ ($p < 0.001$), but it explains less of the variation between schools than the two culture scores do ($R^2$ of $0.55$ against $0.62$). When it
is added to the primary specification it enters at $0.015$ ($p = 0.82$), and
the culture coefficients are essentially unchanged ($0.137$ and $0.103$).

There are two possible interpretations of this result, and the data cannot distinguish between them. The first is that culture and teaching are genuinely different predictors: what a school is like between and around lessons matters over and above how well its lessons are taught. The second is that the three scores overlap too much for the regression to separate them. Teaching is highly correlated with the two culture scores, so the regression may have assigned its association to them, in which case the null on teaching understates what instruction contributes. This second interpretation cannot be put down to a noisier teaching measure: although the observers agree least on the teaching items lesson by lesson, the averaged school score is about as reliable as the culture scores ($0.67$, against $0.60$ and $0.58$ for the in-lesson components).

Two facts favour the first interpretation without settling the matter. Firstly, the teaching score on its own predicts progress well, so it is not noise. Secondly, part of the culture association runs through warmth outside lessons, and no measure of classroom teaching observes what happens there. Settling the question fully would need a measure of teaching quality independent of the visit, and none exists for these schools. The claim made here is therefore the narrower one: conditioning on enacted teaching quality does not account for the culture association.

\subsection{The national extension}

The estimates so far rest on a hundred schools that agreed to be visited. The national test asks whether the associations survive outside that sample, on schools that no one visited. \Cref{tab:national_legs} reports these regressions. For \NNationalStrictness{}
schools with an outcome, some thirty times the visited sample,%
\footnote{The national sample starts from the 3,332 schools of the national population: 60 schools have no scored inspection report, mostly because they opened too recently to have been inspected; a further 80 have no published Progress~8, mostly because their first cohort has not yet reached GCSEs; 95 are excluded as late-entry schools, the same exclusion the visited sample applies; and 45 lack a control variable. That leaves 3,052. \Cref{app:3A:robust} checks whether these omissions could bias the estimates.} warmth
and teaching are predicted from each school's inspection report by the
models of the previous chapter, and strictness is read by the one-page
mark scheme. Every warmth and teaching prediction comes from a model
that never saw the school.

All three coefficients are positive and precise:
$\NatW$ per standard deviation for warmth, $\NatT$ for teaching and
$\NatS$ for strictness, each with $p < 0.001$.

The three coefficients cannot be compared with one another, because the three scores are not equally good measures. Each score is aimed at one of the previous chapter's three measures, warmth, teaching or strictness, but captures only a share of what it is aimed at, and each also picks up a different amount of the rest of what a report says about a school. A larger coefficient can therefore reflect a stronger association, or just a score that carries more of what the report says. What the scores' imperfection cannot do is create the associations. The part that a score misses acts as noise, and noise pushes a coefficient towards zero rather than away from it, so with perfect scores the coefficients would if anything be larger.

The imperfection raises a different concern. Besides culture, each score picks up other things a report says about a school, and those other things might predict progress on their own. The grade and timing checks below are aimed at exactly that possibility.

Four checks address particular doubts about these estimates. The first concerns the inspection grade.
A score read from a report might merely restate the inspectorate's
grade, in which case its association would be the grade's association.
Adding the 2019 grade dummies moves the coefficients almost nowhere
(warmth $\NatWGrade$, teaching $\NatTGrade$, strictness $\NatSGrade$): what the text says predicts
progress over and above what the grade says.

The second concerns timing. On reports
published before September 2022, before either outcome cohort's results
existed, the coefficients are $\NatWPre$, $\NatTPre$
and $\NatSPre$, essentially their full-sample values, so reports that
could have been written in knowledge of the outcomes are not driving the
associations.

The third concerns the models' own estimation error. The warmth and teaching scores are themselves the output of a fitted model, so they carry that model's uncertainty as well as the regression's. Refitting the model many times on resampled training schools and rerunning the regression each time gives 95 per cent intervals of [$\NatWBootLo$, $\NatWBootHi$] for warmth and [$\NatTBootLo$, $\NatTBootHi$] for teaching, both away from zero.

The fourth concerns sample dependence: excluding the visited schools from the national sample raises the warmth coefficient (to $+0.136$) and the teaching coefficient (to $+0.122$), so neither association depends on the schools that agreed to take part.

The size of each national coefficient can be checked against the visited results. Each national score captures only a share of the score the visit produced, so its coefficient should be smaller than the visited coefficient in roughly that proportion. For warmth and teaching this is what the data show: the national coefficients sit close to the visited coefficients scaled down by the share each score captures. The strictness coefficient is about twice that size. The likely reason lies in how the strictness score is produced. The mark scheme assigns its band by reading the whole report, and a report describes more than behaviour: it also gives a general picture of how good the school is. Some of that general picture ends up in the strictness band, and schools whose reports read well tend to have good results. The strictness coefficient therefore picks up some of that link as well as strictness itself. Because of this, comparing warmth with strictness is best done in the visited sample, where both were measured directly. \Cref{app:3A:benchmarks} works through the arithmetic for each coefficient.

There is one further concern about how the strictness score is produced. Inspectors know a school's exam results when they write, and good results may lead an inspector to describe the school's behaviour more favourably than they otherwise would. If that happens, the strictness score partly measures results, and part of the strictness coefficient is results predicting results. The pre-2022 check rules out the direct version of this: reports written before the outcome years still show the association, and those inspectors could not have known the outcomes. It cannot rule out the indirect version. An inspector who saw the school's earlier results may still have described its behaviour more favourably, and earlier results resemble current ones, so some of the association may still come from results rather than strictness.

The national data can also be used to test whether schools that are both warm and strict do better than the two separate associations imply. Schools in the top half on both predicted warmth and strictness make $0.36$ of a grade more progress than other schools with similar intakes, or $0.26$ when the inspection grade is also held constant (\cref{tab:typology}). The interaction term is again close to zero ($0.004$, $p = 0.56$), so here too the advantage is the two separate associations added together rather than anything extra from the combination.

The national version of this split is rougher than the visited one, in two ways. The strictness bands are whole numbers and their median is four, so the higher-strictness half is in practice the schools in the top band. And few national schools are low on warmth while high on strictness, so the group that would show what strictness does without warmth is small.

Progress~8 counts eight subject slots, and a slot that a school does not enter a pupil for scores zero, so a school can raise its score by entering more pupils for the academic subjects of the English Baccalaureate rather than by the progress its pupils make. This is the question left open earlier: whether the strictness association comes from these entry decisions rather than from progress within subjects. Stricter schools do enter more pupils for the academic subjects nationally, but adding the school's EBacc entry rate to the regression removes only 10 to 14~per~cent of the strictness association nationally, and about 11~per~cent in the visited sample. Most of the association therefore reflects the progress pupils make within the subjects they take. \Cref{app:3A:tables} reports the entry regressions.

The extension does not make these associations causal. A score built from an inspection report is open to the same doubt as a score built from a visit: some feature of a school that the control variables do not capture could produce both the score and the results. How strong such a feature would have to be to overturn the visited estimates is the subject of the sensitivity analysis below.

The same associations do not appear in what schools say about themselves. Neither the behaviour-policy scores nor the website culture scores are positively associated with progress in any specification (\cref{tab:llm_p8_matrix}), which matches the previous chapter's finding that these documents carry little of what the visit measured.

\subsection{Robustness}

\Cref{tab:robustness_overall} reports checks on the warmth and strictness estimates for the visited schools. Each check is aimed at a particular doubt about those estimates.

Two doubts concern how the regression is set up. The inspection grade is the strongest control in the specification, so the first check drops it, in case the grade rather than culture is doing the work. The outcome averages two years of results, which could hide a result that holds in one of those years only, so the second check uses 2023--24 alone.

Three doubts concern which pupils and which schools produce the association. A strict school might raise its measured progress by losing pupils with social, emotional and mental-health needs rather than by teaching them better, so the share of those pupils is added as a control. London schools make more progress than schools elsewhere and differ from them in other ways, so London is controlled for separately. A single unusual school could be producing the association on its own, so each school is dropped in turn, with those results in the appendix.

The remaining doubts concern the measures. The association might be a property of Progress~8 rather than of what pupils actually achieve, so the estimates are repeated on Attainment~8 for the 2024/25 cohort, both in total and for English alone. Attainment~8 is a points total over ten weighted subject slots rather than a grades-per-subject measure, so its coefficients in \cref{tab:robustness_overall} look much larger; converted to the same footing, 1.40 points is roughly a seventh of a grade per subject, close to the primary warmth estimate. The culture scores rest on the researchers' ratings, so two checks ask whether the number of raters matters: one adds the share of a school's lessons that were rated by a single researcher, and the other keeps only the schools where every lesson was rated by at least two.

Every check but two leaves both coefficients significant at the five per cent level or better. The exceptions are both for strictness: the Attainment~8 English component, and the subset of schools where every lesson was double-rated. \Cref{app:3A:robust} walks through the checks one by one.

One further check concerns the standard errors rather than the coefficients. Schools in the same academy trust share policies and often leadership, so their results may not be independent of one another, and treating them as independent would make the standard errors too small. Clustering the standard errors by academy trust in the national sample changes them by at most ten per cent. Each national coefficient is at least nine times its standard error, so a change of that size leaves all three significant at the one per cent level (\cref{app:3A:robust}).

\input{tables/tab_robustness_overall}

The enacted warmth and strictness scores come from the visits, while the outcomes average the results of two groups of pupils: those who sat their GCSEs in the summer of 2023, and those who sat them in the summer of 2024. Half the visits took place during the 2023/24 school year, while the second group was still in the school. The other half took place the following year, after both groups had left. In the schools visited during 2023/24, the observers saw the school while the second group was still attending it, though the first group had already left. In the schools visited in 2024/25, both groups had gone: the second up to a year before the visit, and the first up to two years before. The four checks below ask whether the culture the observers scored can stand for the culture those pupils experienced.

The first check looks at how much a school's results move from year to year. The average within-school standard deviation of Progress~8 is $0.20$, against a standard deviation across schools of about $0.5$ (\cref{tab:stability_p8}). Results are therefore a slow-moving feature of a school rather than something that swings from cohort to cohort. Whether culture changes as slowly is a separate question, and it is the one that matters here. The three checks that follow are about culture itself.

The second check uses the one cohort measured at the same time as the fieldwork. That cohort, 2024/25, has no Progress~8, because the Key Stage~2 tests it would be measured from were cancelled in the first year of the pandemic. A stand-in is built instead by adjusting the cohort's Attainment~8 for the school's Key Stage~2 intake, averaged over nearby years. Built the same way for an earlier year, where Progress~8 does exist, this measure correlates $+0.81$ with it. The primary regression is then run again with this stand-in as the outcome in place of Progress~8. The coefficients that come back are close to the primary ones: for warmth and for strictness alike, the stand-in coefficient is within $0.04$ of a grade of its Progress~8 counterpart (\cref{tab:p8_proxy}). The strictness coefficient is, if anything, larger on the stand-in than on Progress~8.

The value of this check is that it removes the timing gap, because the culture and the results now describe the same year. Its weakness is that closing that gap does not settle which way the relation runs. The 2024/25 results were published after every visit, so the culture the observers saw cannot have been a response to them. But a school's results are similar from year to year, so a school that did well in 2024/25 was in most cases already doing well when it was visited, and those earlier results were public. What the check shows is that the association is still there when culture and results belong to the same year. What it cannot show is whether the culture came before the results or followed from them, and that is why it is not treated as evidence on its own.

The third check uses results from long before the fieldwork. The question is still the same one: does culture change slowly enough that the 2023--25 scores also describe the school in earlier years? If culture is a lasting feature of a school, the culture observed in 2023--25 should relate to results from earlier years as well; if the score mostly reflects the school's recent results, it should not. Regressing pre-pandemic Progress~8, from 2018/19, on the culture scores observed in 2023--25, with that era's control variables where they exist, gives $+0.09$ for warmth and $+0.08$ for strictness on the 93 schools with both outcomes. Neither is significant, and both are smaller than the $+0.16$ and $+0.10$ that the same schools give for current results.

The result shows us two things. Firstly, the culture observed in 2023--25 is related to results from six years earlier. That is what would be expected if culture lasts, and it is not what would be expected if the observers were simply scoring schools by their recent results. Secondly, the older association is weaker than the current one. That is also what would be expected if culture changes slowly: the further back the results, the less that school resembles the school the observers saw. The second point is only suggestive, because a formal test cannot reject that the older and current associations are the same size ($p = 0.17$ for warmth and $0.62$ for strictness with the era's control variables, $0.09$ and $0.57$ with current ones).

The fourth check uses a change of headteacher as a sign that a school's culture may have changed. The concern throughout this subsection is that the culture observed at the visit may not be the culture of the outcome years, and a new headteacher is the clearest recorded event that could break the link between the two, since the head shapes a school's routines and expectations. The schools are therefore split by whether the headteacher changed between the first outcome cohort's final year and the end of the visit window, which can be determined for all 99 schools: 58 kept the same headteacher and 41 changed.

In the 58 schools with the same head, a standard deviation of warmth is associated with $+0.16$ of a grade and a standard deviation of strictness with $+0.13$, both significant and close to the full-sample estimates of $0.15$ and $0.11$. In the 41 schools whose head changed, the warmth association is close to zero and neither association is significant.

What the results show is that the main estimates do not depend on the schools where culture is most likely to have changed. The 58 same-head schools are the ones where the culture the observers scored is most likely to be the culture the outcome cohorts experienced, and the associations there are as strong as in the full sample. The weaker associations in the changed-head schools are also consistent with the reasoning behind the check: if a new head changed a school's culture, the scores describe a culture that the outcome pupils did not experience, and the association should weaken. This second observation is weak evidence on its own, because the changed-head group is small, its estimates are noisy, and a formal test cannot reject that the two groups' associations are the same size ($p = 0.27$ for warmth).

The number of schools that changed headteacher also has an implication for the main estimates. If the scores in the 41 changed-head schools are noisier measures of the culture the outcome cohorts experienced, that noise pushes coefficients towards zero, so including those schools would if anything make the full-sample estimates smaller, not larger. \Cref{tab:timing_checks} reports the estimates from the third and fourth checks with their standard errors.

\input{tables/tab_timing_checks}

The last check concerns the possibility that some characteristic of a school that the control variables do not capture produces both its culture scores and its results. This is the doubt the design cannot remove, and no test can rule it out. What two standard sensitivity measures can do is calculate how strong such a characteristic would have to be to explain the estimates away (\cref{tab:sensitivity_bounds}). For strictness, an unmeasured characteristic about as influential as the full control set would be enough to remove the association. For warmth, the same amount of confounding would leave about three-quarters of the association in place. Two limits apply. Firstly, the measures say nothing about reverse causation, only about omitted variables. Secondly, the measures start from the estimated coefficients. The culture scores contain measurement error, and error of that kind pulls an estimated coefficient towards zero, so the coefficients the measures start from are likely smaller than the true associations. A larger association takes more confounding to remove, so the measures likelier understate than overstate how much confounding the associations could withstand. \Cref{app:3A:robust} sets out both measures in full.

\subsection{Exploratory results on traditional pedagogy}

The last results concern what schools say about how they teach. In the public debate about school improvement, the schools known for combining warmth with strictness often also describe their teaching as traditional: knowledge is taught directly by subject-expert teachers and practised until it is remembered, and creative work is treated as something that builds on knowledge rather than something that develops alongside it. Whether a school takes this approach cannot be read from its inspection report, because reports assess the quality of a school's teaching but rarely describe its approach. The one public source in which schools state their approach is their own website, so the previous chapter measured traditional pedagogy from each school's site: a binary indicator that marks a school when its website presents knowledge as the organising principle of the curriculum or describes teacher-led instruction. The indicator marks 125 of the 3,048 national schools with an outcome.

Schools whose websites espouse traditional pedagogy make \IdPed{} of a grade per subject more progress than otherwise similar schools ($p < 0.01$, $n = 3{,}075$). The association changes little when the school's pre-pandemic inspection grade is added to the control variables ($+0.135$).

The indicator can also be put together with the two national culture measures. Of the 299 schools in the top half on both predicted warmth and the strictness bands, 16 also espouse traditional pedagogy, so the full combination is rare, and the warm-and-strict schools are only a little more likely to espouse it than the rest (5 per cent against 4).

In raw terms, the 16 schools average $+1.02$ on Progress~8, so their pupils make about a full grade more progress per subject than the national average. Part of that gap may reflect who attends these schools rather than what the schools do. A school with a public identity of this kind is chosen: parents who want a strict, knowledge-led education for their children may differ from other parents in how much they support their children's schooling, and pupils with such parents might make more progress in any school. The controls can address the measurable part of this, comparing the 16 with otherwise similar schools on intake, size, type and location. Entering an indicator for the 16 into the same regression as the national estimates, with the full control set and the pre-pandemic inspection grade held constant, gives $0.72$ of a grade ($p < 0.001$).

The 16 can also be compared only with the other warm-and-strict schools, to ask whether traditional pedagogy adds anything beyond the two culture dimensions. Among the 299 schools in that group, the ones that also espouse traditional pedagogy make $0.38$ of a grade more progress than the ones that do not ($p = 0.03$).

The last comparison is the fragile one: one of the 16 appears to be misclassified by the indicator, and removing that school alone reduces the within-group difference to $0.30$ of a grade ($p = 0.06$). The first comparison is steadier: with the misclassified school removed, the group's advantage over otherwise similar schools is $0.60$ of a grade rather than $0.72$, and it remains clearly significant ($p = 0.001$).

These results are exploratory, meaning they were produced to see whether a pattern exists rather than to test a hypothesis fixed in advance, and they carry four limits because of it. The measures come from what schools choose to say about themselves. The traditional-pedagogy indicator misses schools that follow the approach without writing about it, and marks some schools that do not follow it. The 16-school group is small enough that a few unusual schools can move its estimates substantially, as the removal of a single school above showed. And a significance level assumes that the comparison was chosen before the data were seen; these comparisons were chosen partly because the data looked interesting, and comparisons chosen that way will sometimes look strong by chance alone, so the significance levels here overstate the evidence in a way that those elsewhere in the chapter do not. The associations are therefore reported as descriptions rather than tests.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:p2_conclusion}

Observed school culture is associated with pupil progress. Across the
\NTierOne{}
English secondary schools that were visited, a standard deviation of enacted warmth is associated with Progress~8 that is $0.15$ of a grade higher, and a standard deviation of enacted strictness with $0.11$ of a grade. The coefficients barely move whether the pre-pandemic inspection grade is included among the control variables, excluded, or filled in from a predecessor school where it is missing. The two coefficients are close in size, and a formal test cannot distinguish them. 

The two associations simply add together: a school that is high on both dimensions gains no more and no less than the sum of the two. Schools in the top half on both outperform the rest by two-fifths of a grade. Conditioning on enacted teaching quality
does not account for the association. Substituting the headteacher's own description of the school for the observed scores removes the warmth association, so the association belongs to what the observers saw rather than to what the school says about itself. And the associations are not a property of the hundred schools that agreed to be visited: all three scores predict progress across the national population, with warmth and teaching read from inspection reports by the prediction models and strictness by the one-page mark scheme. Each of the three national associations survives two checks. It holds when the pre-pandemic inspection grade is added to the control variables, so the scores carry information beyond the report's overall verdict. And it holds on the reports written before the outcome cohorts sat their GCSEs, whose inspectors could not have known the results the scores now predict.

There are two implications for the current policy debate. Both depend on the association being at least partly causal, which the design cannot prove, though the checks above have removed many of the other explanations.

The first implication concerns the behaviour debate, which treats discipline as the thing that improves schools and relational culture as secondary. The estimates here do not rank the two that way: warmth matters about as much as strictness, and the two add rather than multiply. On these estimates, a policy that strengthened behaviour while relationships weakened would gain about as much on one dimension as it lost on the other. The schools two-fifths of a grade ahead are the ones high on both. That figure comes from the two coefficients added together rather than from comparing groups of schools directly: sorting the visited schools by whether they sit in the top half on warmth and on strictness gives four groups, and some of the four contain too few schools for a direct comparison.

The second implication concerns where in the school the warmth that matters is found. The sub-score results place the association in warmth outside lessons and in orderliness inside them. The warmth that goes with progress is therefore not a way of teaching; it is how adults and pupils treat each other in corridors, at break and at the gate.

The estimates are associations, not causal effects. Several features of the design strengthen them: the outcome is value-added, the controls are extensive, the coefficients barely move across specifications, and school characteristics persist across years. What the design cannot rule out is that something about a school which the controls do not capture, present throughout the period, produces both its culture and its results, because every school is observed only once. There are three other limitations. The visited sample is small, and its schools are stronger than the national average. Culture was measured during or after the final outcome year rather than before it. And a single visit measures a school with considerable error.

Two designs would go further than this one. Observing culture before an outcome cohort arrived and again after it left would show which comes first, the culture or the results. And following schools whose culture changed, because the headteacher changed or a new behaviour policy was introduced, would show whether results change with it.

Within those limits, the results pick out the relational and disciplinary features of a school that go together with its pupils' progress. They also show that these features, and teaching besides, can be measured for every school in the country from the public record: warmth and teaching by a model that counts words in inspection reports, and strictness by a mark scheme that tells the reader what to look for.

\section{How the Progress 8 Measures Are Calculated}
\label{app:3A:p8mechanics}

The eight slots of Progress~8 (P8 below) are structured, and this
structure is why the thesis treats the components differently. The measure
itself is as the body defines it: attainment across eight GCSE slots,
compared with the national average for pupils with the same Key Stage~2
starting point, and averaged across the school. English and Maths each fill two slots and
are double-weighted. Every pupil sits both, so these components measure
progress and nothing else. The EBacc component covers three slots that must be filled
from a restricted academic list: sciences, computer science, history,
geography, and modern foreign languages. The Open component covers the final
three slots, which may be filled by any approved qualification, including
arts and vocational subjects. For these last six slots the school makes
entry decisions, and an unfilled slot contributes zero progress to the
school's average. Consequently, a school's EBacc and Open components mix two
different things: how much progress pupils make in the subjects they enter,
and which subjects the school enters them for. A school that enters few
pupils for languages will carry empty EBacc slots and a depressed EBacc
component even if the pupils it does enter progress well.

This structure generates a theoretical prediction about warmth and
strictness. Schools higher on strictness, characterised by structured
behaviour management, high academic expectations, and intensive pupil
steering, are more likely to direct pupils into the EBacc pathway, and could therefore raise their EBacc component through entry as well as through progress itself. Schools higher on warmth might generate stronger
progress in the more elective Open component. The five-outcome versions of the main results tables in this appendix
provide evidence on that prediction, and the entry-rate analysis below
addresses the pathway channel directly.

The entry rates themselves are calculated from the pupil count columns of
the 2023--24 performance tables, because the Department for Education
stopped publishing entry rates in percentage form in recent releases. The
EBacc entry rate is the number of pupils entered for the full EBacc suite
divided by the number of pupils at the end of Key Stage~4, and the
humanities and languages rates are constructed in the same way from their
respective counts. Entry rates are available only at the subject-group
level. Individual subjects within a group cannot be distinguished in the
data collected for this project.

\section{Structural Stability of School Characteristics}
\label{app:3A:stability}

The intra-class correlation (ICC) decomposes total variance in a panel variable
into a between-school component and a within-school over-time component. An ICC
close to one indicates that schools are much more different from each other
than they are from their own past selves. In other words, year-on-year
fluctuation within a school is small relative to the persistent differences
between schools. ICC is estimated by
one-way random-effects ANOVA on the school-year panel, retaining only schools
with at least three years of non-missing data for the variable in question.

\Cref{tab:structural_stability} reports ICC estimates and mean absolute
year-on-year changes for nine panel variables covering pupil intake composition,
special educational needs prevalence, prior attainment, pupil progress, and
per-pupil expenditure. Six of nine variables exceed ICC~$= 0.69$, and all
exceed~$0.49$. Intake characteristics (the share of pupils with English as
an Additional Language, school roll, and the mean KS2 prior attainment of the
entering cohort) are especially stable, with ICC values between 0.81 and 0.92
and small mean absolute year-on-year changes relative to their between-school
spread.
Special educational needs prevalence (EHC plan share, ICC~$= 0.67$; SEN support
share, ICC~$= 0.65$) and school-level Progress~8 (ICC~$= 0.73$) show moderate to
high stability. Per-pupil expenditure, at ICC~$= 0.52$, is the most volatile, which
reflects its sensitivity to changes in national funding policy. Even so, the
majority of the variation in expenditure is between schools rather than
within schools over time.

The ICC evidence quantifies the stability that the design assumes: schools
occupy relatively persistent positions in the distribution of structural
characteristics, which supports using culture scores measured in 2023--24
and 2024--25 as proxies for the organisational conditions present during
the outcome period.

The same analysis at the level of the thirteen primary-need categories
within the SEN population shows moderate stability for the Social, Emotional
and Mental Health (SEMH) category (ICC~$= 0.545$), which supports the
baseline SEMH share entering as an additional control in the robustness
specification of \cref{tab:robustness_overall}. The lower stability of SEMH
relative to the aggregate SEN support share (ICC~$= 0.65$) is consistent
with SEMH composition being partly responsive to school behaviour and
exclusion practices, rather than being determined solely by fixed intake
characteristics.

\input{tables/tab_structural_stability}

\Cref{tab:p8_component_stability} reports ICC estimates separately for the four
Progress~8 components reported in this chapter.
Among the Progress~8 components,
stability varies from ICC~$= 0.587$ for the Open component to ICC~$= 0.748$
for EBacc, with English and Maths sitting between these at ICC~$\approx 0.67$.
EBacc is more stable than Open because curriculum-pathway decisions, meaning which subjects pupils are entered for, are governed by stable school policy and change little from year to year,
while the more varied Open component (arts, vocational and additional GCSEs) is subject to greater annual variation in subject mix. All four P8 components
exceed the per-pupil expenditure ICC of~$0.52$, which is the weakest result
in the main stability table. The cross-sectional design is therefore at
least as defensible for each component individually as for the structural
characteristics already discussed.

The Open component's lower stability means that the warmth coefficient is
estimated against a noisier outcome. The chapter's differential
prediction, set out in \cref{app:3A:p8mechanics}, expects warmth to show
most strongly in the Open component and strictness in the EBacc component.
With Open at ICC~$= 0.587$ against $0.748$ for EBacc, the warmth side of that prediction is tested against more noise and may be understated. Any
differential finding between components must be read against this
difference in outcome noise.

\section{Robustness in Detail}
\label{app:3A:robust}

This section walks through the checks summarised with the results: the alternative specifications of \cref{tab:robustness_overall}, the headteacher-change split, the two sensitivity measures of \cref{tab:sensitivity_bounds}, the clustering of the national standard errors, and a check on the schools the national sample omits.

\Cref{tab:robustness_overall} reports the primary
estimate under alternative specifications, all on the primary sample, and
each of them addresses a particular doubt. The grade control might be doing
the work, or absorbing part of it. The specification ladder
(\cref{tab:spec_ladder}) already varies its treatment. Dropping the control
altogether leaves $0.153$ and $0.097$ ($n = 100$).

Averaging two cohorts might smooth over a result that holds in
only one of them. The single 2023/24 outcome year gives $0.155$ and $0.105$.

Strict schools might have fewer pupils with social, emotional and
mental-health needs, and gain in measured progress by that route. Adding the
2015/16 baseline share of such pupils, which was measured before any of the
culture was observed, gives $0.149$ and $0.090$ ($n = 94$), and the sorting
channel accounts for under a tenth of the strictness association
(\cref{tab:semh_mechanism}).

Progress~8 might be peculiar: on the 2024/25 cohort's Attainment~8, which does not condition on intake, both dimensions remain significant at the 5 per cent level on the total, at $1.40$ and $1.32$ points per standard deviation of the score. Attainment~8 sums grades over ten weighted subject slots, so these coefficients are roughly a seventh of a grade per subject. On the English component alone warmth remains significant ($0.35$) while strictness does not ($0.16$, $p = 0.12$). The Key Stage~2 control in
these regressions is the school-level mean for the Progress~8 cohorts,
since the 2024/25 cohort has no Key Stage~2 results of its own.

The enacted scores might reflect something about the visits themselves. About
a third of the observed lessons were rated by one researcher rather than
two, those lessons were scored a little lower, and the share of such lessons
in a school's visit correlates $-0.25$ with its warmth score. Adding that
share as a control moves nothing ($0.150$ and $0.105$; the share itself
has a coefficient of $0.025$, $p = 0.76$).

The warmth result might depend on London, since
the sample over-represents London and the EAL share, through which the
warmth association works, is concentrated there. Adding a London indicator built from the schools register moves warmth only to $0.152$ ($p = 0.001$) and strictness to $0.096$, and the indicator itself is not significant ($0.113$, $p = 0.33$), so the association does not depend on London.

Restricting the sample to the 64 schools in which every lesson was rated by at least two researchers gives $0.189$ for warmth
($p = 0.001$) and $0.071$ for strictness ($p = 0.15$). The warmth estimate is
larger on that subset and the strictness estimate smaller, with a standard
error a quarter larger on two-thirds of the sample, so the strictness result
is the one that depends on the full sample.

No single school carries the visited estimates: dropping each school in turn moves the warmth coefficient by at most $0.019$ and the strictness coefficient by at most $0.017$, and no omission moves either across conventional significance.

In the headteacher-change split reported with the timing checks, the 58 schools with the same head in the September 2022 and July 2025 registers give estimates close to the full-sample values for both dimensions, while the 41 schools whose head changed give a near-zero warmth estimate with standard errors more than twice as large; a formal test cannot reject that the two groups' associations are equal.

Finally, two standard measures ask how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to overturn the visited estimates (\cref{tab:sensitivity_bounds}). Both concern the same doubt: some characteristic of a school that the control variables do not capture might produce both its culture scores and its results. Neither can rule such a characteristic out; each calculates how strong it would have to be to explain the estimates away.

The first, Oster's $\delta$, compares the unmeasured characteristics with the controls that can be measured. Adding the full control set moves each coefficient by a known amount, and the measure extrapolates from that movement: it asks how many times as influential as the observed controls the unmeasured ones would have to be to drive the coefficient to zero. For strictness the answer is \OsterDeltaS{}, just above the conventional benchmark of one, so unmeasured characteristics about as influential as the full control set would be enough to remove the association. For warmth the measure cannot be read the usual way, because adding the controls raises the warmth coefficient rather than lowering it, and the extrapolation then almost never reaches zero; warmth's large $\delta$ of \OsterDeltaW{} is therefore not by itself evidence of robustness. The informative number for warmth is instead the coefficient that remains if the unmeasured characteristics are assumed exactly as influential as the controls, which is \OsterBetaEqualW{}, about three-quarters of the estimate. What no measure of this kind can rule out is a confounder unlike the observed controls, working in the opposite direction to them.

The second, the E-value, asks the question without reference to the controls. It considers a single unmeasured characteristic and asks how strongly it would have to be associated with both the culture score and progress to account for the whole estimate. For warmth the answer is an association of at least \EValW{} in risk-ratio terms with each, and for strictness \EValS{}. A confounder of that strength would be more strongly related to the score, and to progress, than the score and progress are related to each other.

Both measures speak only to omitted variables; neither touches reverse causation. Both also start from the estimated coefficients. The culture scores contain measurement error, and error of that kind pulls an estimated coefficient towards zero, so the coefficients the measures start from are likely smaller than the true associations. A larger association takes more confounding to remove, so the measures likelier understate than overstate how much confounding the associations could withstand.

Schools in the same academy trust share practices, so the standard errors were also computed with clustering on trust membership from the schools register (1,595 clusters; three-fifths of schools in multi-school trusts). Clustering moves the standard errors by at most ten per cent and changes no inference. The visited sample has too few multi-school trusts (25 schools across nine shared trusts) for cluster-robust inference to be informative, so I claim no reassurance from it.

The strictness result does not depend on treating the five bands as equally spaced steps. Comparing each band directly with band 3, progress rises band by band ($-0.27$, $-0.05$, $+0.15$, $+0.43$); \cref{tab:band_dummies} reports the four estimates.

One further doubt concerns the national sample itself. The national regressions run on 3,052 of the 3,332 panel schools, and the footnote with the results gives the arithmetic: 60 schools have no scored inspection report, 80 no published Progress~8, 95 are excluded as late-entry schools, and 45 lack a control variable. The doubt is whether the missing 280 differ from the rest in a way that could bias the estimated associations.

They differ in how well they are doing. The omitted schools are more disadvantaged and weaker than the estimation sample: the uninspected group averages a free-school-meals share of 37 per cent against the sample's 30, and where an outcome is published the omitted groups' mean Progress~8 sits well below zero ($-0.45$ for the uninspected schools, $-0.44$ for the late-entry schools and $-0.27$ for those missing a control, against $+0.01$ in the sample). That is the profile of newly opened schools, schools in transition and university technical colleges, and it says who the estimates describe: established mainstream schools.

Being weaker is not, by itself, a source of bias. The regression estimates how progress moves with strictness among the schools it includes, so leaving out a group of weak schools changes who is described, not the answer for those included. The estimates would only mislead if the omitted schools' own strictness--progress relationship were different, so that putting them back would change it. For one group that can be tested directly. The 45 schools dropped only for a missing control have both a strictness band and an outcome. Among them, a standard deviation of the band score goes with $0.29$ of a grade (standard error $0.07$); in the estimation sample the same figure is $0.23$ ($0.01$). The two are indistinguishable, and the omitted group's is if anything the steeper. For the omissions to inflate the national strictness association, the missing schools would need a weaker relationship than the sample. The one direct test finds the same or a slightly stronger one, so the exclusions read as mildly conservative rather than favourable.

\section{Benchmarks for the National Coefficients}
\label{app:3A:benchmarks}

Each national coefficient has a benchmark. The national score is an imperfect stand-in for the enacted one, so its coefficient should be a scaled-down copy of the visited coefficient, shrunk by the share of the enacted score it captures. For warmth that benchmark is a third of $0.219$, roughly $0.07$, and the national estimate of $\NatW$ sits on it. For teaching it is $0.28 \times 0.206 \approx 0.06$, and the estimate of $\NatT$ sits about a quarter above. For strictness it is $0.30 \times 0.199 \approx 0.06$, and the estimate of $\NatS$ is roughly double. The benchmarks are rough: the shares they rest on come from about a hundred schools of restricted range, so each carries a sampling error of about $0.1$, and the strictness share is lowered by the coarseness of a five-band score, which sets that benchmark too low.

The strictness overshoot has an explanation in how the two readings were built. The warmth and teaching models were trained only on the part of the enacted score that demographics cannot explain, with names and other shortcuts stripped from the text, so their scores carry warmth and teaching and little else. The mark scheme reads the report as it stands. Its bands pick up conduct, and with it some of the general character of an orderly, effective school, and that general character is itself correlated with results. Part of the strictness coefficient is that extra content rather than strictness alone. For the ranking of the two dimensions, the visited sample is the one to trust, because there the dimensions were measured directly, and warmth carries at least as much weight as strictness.
