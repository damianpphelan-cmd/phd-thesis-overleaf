\begin{chapterabstract}
Does the culture of a school affect how much its pupils learn? This paper
estimates the association between the warmth and strictness of English
secondary schools, measured by direct observation at \NTierOne{} visited
schools, and Progress~8, the Department for Education's value-added measure,
conditioning on prior attainment, intake composition, school type, size,
location and the school's pre-pandemic inspection grade. Three results follow.
Both dimensions predict progress with about equal weight: a standard deviation
of observed warmth is associated with $\hat{\beta}_W = \PrimarySpecW$ of a grade
higher Progress~8 and a standard deviation of observed strictness with
$\hat{\beta}_S = \PrimarySpecS$ ($n = \PrimarySpecN$), both significant at the
one per cent level and stable across specifications. The association does not
run through observed teaching quality, which enters at $0.024$ and leaves both
culture coefficients unchanged. And the dimensions add rather than amplify one
another: the warm-and-strict schools sit $0.37$ of a grade above the rest and
the interaction is null. Substituting the headteacher's own account for the
observed scores removes the warmth association, and the strictness score read
from inspection reports reproduces the strictness result across
\NNationalStrictness{} schools with the inspection grade held constant
($\BetaNationalStrictness$).
\end{chapterabstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:p2_intro}

Does the culture of a school affect how much its pupils learn? This paper
estimates the association between the warmth and strictness of English
secondary schools, measured by direct observation, and the progress their
pupils make between the end of primary school and GCSE. It uses the enacted
culture scores constructed in the previous chapter for \NTierOne{} visited
schools, and the Progress~8 measure published by the Department for
Education, which records each school's average pupil progress relative to
pupils nationally with the same prior attainment.

The question belongs to the tradition that asks whether the school a child
attends makes a difference beyond what the child brings to it, and if so
through what. The tradition began with a negative answer: \citet{coleman1966}
found that school resources explained little of the variation in attainment
once family background was accounted for, and \citet{hanushek1997}, reviewing
the evidence three decades later, found no consistent relationship between
expenditure and performance. The class-size experiments and natural
experiments that followed \citep{krueger1999,krueger2001,angrist1999} showed
that resources can matter, and English evidence finds small positive effects
of spending, concentrated among disadvantaged pupils
\citep{levacic2005,jenkins2006,holmlund2010,machin2010}; but the effects are
modest against the size of the school-to-school variation they leave
unexplained. Two lines of work have located that variation elsewhere.
\citet{rutter1979}, studying twelve inner-London secondary schools, placed it
in what they called ethos, the expectations and routine practice a school
sustains, and the effective-schools syntheses that followed listed an orderly
environment and a supportive climate among its correlates
\citep{edmonds1979,sammons1995,teddlie2000}. The teacher-quality literature
placed it in the classroom: \citet{rivkin2005} and \citet{chetty2014} show
that variation in teacher effectiveness is large and consequential, and
English estimates \citep{slater2012,murphy2011} are of the same order. What
neither line settles is whether a school's culture, as distinct from the
quality of its individual teachers, predicts how much its pupils learn, and
that is the question here.

Two strands are closest to the paper. The authoritative-school literature
\citep{pellerin2005,gregory2010}, building on the parenting typology of
\citet{maccoby1983} in which responsiveness and demandingness each carry an
independent effect on children's outcomes \citep{majumder2015,zhang2020},
finds that schools combining support with structure produce better
behavioural and engagement outcomes; but it has rarely had an
academic-progress outcome or a direct measure of both dimensions. And the
literature on high-expectation, high-structure charter and academy models
finds large effects of a bundle of practices. In the United States, effective
charter schools are marked by strict discipline and high expectations
\citep{abdulkadiroglu2011,angrist2010}, an index of five such practices
explains about half of the variation in charter effectiveness while class size
and spending explain none \citep{dobbie2013}, and the bundle transplanted into
ordinary public schools raises mathematics attainment \citep{fryer2014};
\citet{chabrier2016} and \citet{epple2016} review the wider evidence, in which
most charter schools show no average advantage and the effective ones share
this profile. In England, the first wave of academies, which targeted
under-performing schools and gave them autonomy, raised attainment
\citep{machin2011,eyles2016}, while the later converter academies show much
weaker effects \citep{eyles2019}, which suggests that autonomy is not the
operative channel; \citet{machin2020} show that the early academies' higher
exclusion rates reflected genuine disciplinary change rather than the removal
of weak pupils. In all of this work strict behaviour norms and warm,
individual relationships travel together inside one model, and the studies
compare that model with its absence. This paper measures the two dimensions
separately, at ordinary schools rather than a selected model, and asks what
each contributes.

The outcome is a value-added measure, and value-added measures have their
critics: \citet{gorard2010,gorard2013} argue that school-level estimates are
dominated by error and unstable from year to year. Progress~8 is used here
nonetheless, because it is the measure the Department for Education
publishes and schools are judged by, because it conditions on the single
strongest predictor of GCSE performance, prior attainment at Key Stage~2, and
because it allows the visited schools to be placed against every school in
the country on the same footing; the instability concern is met by averaging
two cohorts and by showing, in the appendix, that a school's Progress~8 is in
fact stable across years relative to the differences between schools.

The design is an association with several features that strengthen it short
of identification, set out in full in the empirical specification below. The
outcome is value-added, so it already conditions on where pupils started; the
regressions condition on intake composition, prior attainment, school type,
size, location and the school's pre-pandemic inspection grade; the sample
excludes schools that admit pupils only at 13 or 14; and the strictness result
is reproduced across the national population with a text-based measure. What
the design cannot do is rule out that some persistent characteristic of a
school produces both its culture and its results, and every estimate is
presented as an association.

Three results follow. First, both dimensions predict progress, with about
equal weight: a one standard deviation increase in observed warmth is
associated with $0.14$ of a grade higher Progress~8, and in observed
strictness with $0.13$, both significant at the one per cent level, and both
stable across every treatment of the inspection-grade control. Entered alone
each dimension carries a coefficient of about $0.22$, so the two share some
variance but each carries independent signal. Second, the association does not
run through observed teaching quality: adding the visit's teaching score
leaves both culture coefficients unchanged and the teaching coefficient
itself is close to zero. Third, the schools that combine high warmth with high
strictness outperform the rest by a third of a grade, while the interaction
between the two dimensions is null; the dimensions add rather than amplify
one another. Substituting the headteacher's own account of the school for the
observed scores removes the warmth association entirely, and the strictness
score read from inspection reports reproduces the strictness result across
the national population with the inspection grade held constant.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:p2_data} describes the
outcome, the culture measures, the controls and the sample.
Section~\ref{sec:p2_spec} sets out the specification and what it can and
cannot identify. Section~\ref{sec:p2_results} reports the results and their
robustness. Section~\ref{sec:p2_conclusion} concludes.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:p2_data}

\textbf{Outcomes.} The outcome is Progress~8, the Department for Education's
school-level value-added measure. For each pupil, attainment across eight GCSE
slots, weighted toward English and Maths, is compared with the national
average attainment of pupils who left primary school with the same Key
Stage~2 result; the differences are averaged across the school's cohort. A
school at zero is exactly average for its intake; $+0.5$ means its pupils
achieved half a grade more per subject than pupils elsewhere with the same
starting point, and $-0.5$ half a grade less. Nationally the measure has a
standard deviation across schools of about $0.5$ and an interquartile range
of about two-thirds of a grade, so the difference between a school at the
first quartile and one at the third is roughly two-thirds of a grade in every
subject for every pupil. The paper reports overall Progress~8 and its English
and Maths components, which are the cleanest measures the tables offer
because every pupil sits both examinations. The remaining components, EBacc
and Open, are partly determined by which subjects a school enters pupils for,
and are reported in \cref{app:3A:tables} together with a direct analysis of
subject entry rates; \cref{app:3A:p8mechanics} sets out how the components are
constructed. The primary outcome is the two-year average of the 2022/23 and
2023/24 cohorts, the two post-pandemic years for which a Key Stage~2 baseline
exists; averaging reduces the year-to-year noise in a single cohort's figure.
The 2024/25 cohort has no baseline, because its Key Stage~2 tests were
cancelled in 2020, and \cref{app:3A:tables} reports a validated
pseudo-progress measure for it.

\textbf{Culture measures.} The explanatory variables are the enacted warmth
and strictness scores of the previous chapter: the visit-based measures, on a
0--10 scale, for the \NTierOne{} visited schools, entered per standard
deviation so that the two coefficients are comparable. Across the visited
schools enacted warmth has mean $6.6$ and standard deviation $1.0$ and enacted
strictness mean $7.0$ and standard deviation $0.9$, so a standard deviation is
about one point on the ten-point scale. The visit's teaching-practice score,
built from the nine lesson items on questioning, feedback, explanation and
structure, enters as a benchmark: it is the most obvious rival account of any
culture--progress association, since a school whose teachers are warm and
orderly may also be a school whose teachers teach well. The espoused scores,
from the headteacher interview, enter one robustness specification, and the
inspection-report strictness score enters the national extension.

\textbf{Controls.} All specifications condition on the mean Key Stage~2
score of the cohort, which guards against the possibility that Progress~8 is
not fully neutral to intake; the shares of pupils eligible for free school
meals, with English as an additional language and with special educational
needs, which are the composition measures most strongly associated with
progress; log school size; academy status, urban location and selective
admissions; years since the last inspection, since a school inspected long
ago is described by an older report; and the school's overall inspection
grade as at August 2019, entered as dummies. The 2019 grade is used rather
than the current one because a contemporaneous grade is partly an outcome of
the culture being measured, and it is entered at all because it is the best
available summary of how the school was regarded before the outcome cohorts
arrived. Six visited schools converted to academies after 2019 and carry no
grade of their own; for five, the predecessor school's 2019 grade is used,
and the sixth is dropped where the control is required.

\textbf{Sample.} Two exclusions apply. Schools whose statutory entry age is 13
or above (university technical colleges, studio schools and similar; 98
schools nationally, two among those visited) are excluded from every outcome
regression: Progress~8 measures progress from age 11, and these schools enrol
their pupils at 13 or 14, so their figure attributes to them progress made
elsewhere. One visited school lacks the years-since-inspection control. The
primary estimation sample is therefore $n = \PrimarySpecN$.
\Cref{tab:outcome_stats} and \cref{tab:control_stats} report descriptive
statistics for outcomes and controls. The visited schools' mean Progress~8 is
about a quarter of a grade above zero with a standard deviation a little
below the national one, which is the restricted range noted in the previous
chapter. On the controls the visited schools are close to the interviewed
ones and to the country: 29 per cent of pupils eligible for free school
meals, 16 per cent with special educational needs, three-quarters academies,
nine in ten urban, one in twenty selective, and a 2019 grade distribution of
roughly a quarter Outstanding, five-eighths Good and one in nine below Good.
The one visible difference is the share of pupils with English as an
additional language, 28 per cent against 21 in the interviewed sample, which
is the London weighting; it is controlled throughout. The correlations
between the culture scores and the controls are all below $|0.3|$
(\cref{tab:score_controls_corr}), so the culture measures are not close
proxies for intake or school type, and the coefficients below are not
composition effects in disguise.

\section{Empirical Specification}
\label{sec:p2_spec}

The primary specification is
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:main}
  Y_s = \alpha + \beta_W W_s + \beta_S S_s + \mathbf{X}_s'\gamma
        + \sum_g \delta_g \,\mathbb{1}[G_s = g] + \varepsilon_s ,
\end{equation}
%
where $Y_s$ is the two-year average Progress~8 (overall, English or Maths) of
school $s$, $W_s$ and $S_s$ are its enacted warmth and strictness scores in
standard-deviation units, $\mathbf{X}_s$ the controls listed above and $G_s$
its 2019 inspection grade. Standard errors are heteroskedasticity-consistent
(HC3), the small-sample variant appropriate to $n$ near 100. Three stages are
estimated: equation~(\ref{eq:main}); the same equation with the
teaching-practice score $T_s$ in place of $W_s$ and $S_s$; and the equation
with all three. Each dimension is also entered alone. The national extension
replaces $W_s$ and $S_s$ with the inspection-report strictness score,
standardised, on all schools with an outcome, and is estimated with and
without the grade dummies.

The estimates are associations, and it is worth being exact about what the
design does and does not buy. Because the outcome is value-added, the
comparison is between schools whose pupils started from the same place, so
the first and largest confound in any school-effects estimate, that stronger
schools take stronger pupils, is removed by construction, and the Key
Stage~2 control guards the residual. Because the culture measures come from
visits that were mostly made in the year after the outcome cohorts sat their
examinations, reverse causation in the ordinary sense, that good results
produce a warm and orderly school, is not excluded by timing; what the design
relies on instead is that culture is persistent, so that what was observed
in 2024/25 is a fair reading of what the 2022/23 and 2023/24 cohorts
experienced. The appendix supports that reading two ways: a school's
Progress~8, and the structural characteristics that shape it, are stable
across years relative to the differences between schools, and the one cohort
measured contemporaneously with the fieldwork gives coefficients within
$0.05$ of the primary ones. Because the 2019 grade is held constant, the
comparison is between schools that the inspectorate regarded alike before the
outcome cohorts arrived, so a school's reputation, and whatever it draws in
staff and families, is partly absorbed. And because the sample excludes
schools that enrol at 13 or 14, no school is credited with progress it did
not house.

What the design cannot do is rule out that some persistent characteristic of
a school produces both its culture and its results: a headteacher who is
effective in ways the visit does not measure, or a stable school reputation
that draws both committed staff and motivated families. Two features of the
data bear on this without resolving it. The teaching-practice score is
observed alongside the culture scores, so the most obvious such
characteristic, that warm and orderly schools are simply schools whose
teachers teach well, can be tested directly rather than assumed away. And the
strictness result reproduces across the national population using a
text-based measure with the inspection grade held constant, so it is not an
artefact of which schools agreed to be visited. The sorting concern that
remains, that strict schools may retain fewer pupils with social, emotional
and mental-health needs and gain in measured progress by that route, is
tested by adding a pre-period measure of that share. The estimates are
nonetheless presented as associations throughout, and no coefficient is
described as an effect.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:p2_results}

\textbf{Culture predicts progress.} \Cref{tab:spec_ladder} reports the
primary estimates. In the primary specification observed warmth carries a
coefficient of $\hat{\beta}_W = \PrimarySpecW$ ($p = 0.002$) and observed
strictness $\hat{\beta}_S = \PrimarySpecS$ ($p = 0.007$) on overall
Progress~8, with $R^2 = 0.62$. The table shows the same equation under four
treatments of the grade control, from requiring an original 2019 grade
($n = 95$) to dropping the control ($n = 100$); the coefficients move by less
than $0.02$ across the four. The two dimensions contribute with near-equal
weight, as the authoritative-school framework predicts.

\input{tables/tab_spec_ladder}

The magnitudes are worth placing. A standard deviation of enacted warmth is
about one point on the ten-point scale, or half a point on the five-point
scale of every warmth item in every lesson observed. The associated $0.14$ of a grade is about a quarter of a
standard deviation of school Progress~8 nationally, about a quarter of the
gap in mean Progress~8 between schools graded Outstanding and Good in 2019
($0.53$), and about a quarter of the gap between the least and most
disadvantaged quarter of schools by free-school-meal share ($0.62$). Two
standard deviations on both dimensions together, the distance from a cold
and disorderly school to a warm and orderly one within the visited range,
is associated with about half a grade per subject per pupil, which is the
size of the difference the performance tables treat as separating a
well-above-average school from an average one.

Entered alone, each dimension carries a coefficient of about $0.22$
(\cref{tab:univariate_ws}): $0.218$ for warmth and $0.228$ for strictness.
Jointly each retains roughly three-fifths to two-thirds of its solo coefficient. The two
dimensions therefore share variance, as their correlation of
$\CorrWarmthStrictnessEnacted$ implies, but each carries substantial signal
the other does not, which is the empirical basis for treating them as two
constructs; a single warm-to-strict axis would show one coefficient
absorbing the other.

\input{tables/tab_univariate_ws}

At the component level English is warmth-led (warmth $0.167$, $p < 0.001$;
strictness $0.087$, $p = 0.083$) and Maths balanced (warmth $0.112$,
$p = 0.041$; strictness $0.110$, $p = 0.036$). One reading is that English,
which rewards sustained written work and discussion, is the subject in which
relationships between teacher and pupil bear most directly on effort, while
Maths rewards the concentration that an orderly classroom protects; the
component estimates are consistent with that reading without establishing
it. Estimates for the two remaining components, and the analysis of subject
entry that they require, are in \cref{app:3A:tables}.

\textbf{Teaching quality does not account for it.} \Cref{tab:stages23_trio}
reports the second and third stages. The visit's teaching-practice score alone
predicts overall Progress~8 at $0.245$ ($p < 0.001$) but with a lower $R^2$
($0.55$) than the two culture scores. Added to the primary specification it
enters at $0.024$ ($p = 0.76$), and the culture coefficients are unchanged
($0.131$ and $0.126$). Warmth for Maths slips past the five per cent threshold
once teaching enters ($p = 0.115$), which is unsurprising given how much
variance the three visit scores share.

Two readings of that result are available and the data do not choose between
them. The first is that culture and instruction are distinct predictors of
progress: what a school is like between and around lessons, and how orderly
its lessons are, matter over and above how well the lessons are taught, so
that the culture association is not the teaching-quality association under
another name. The second is that the teaching score is the noisiest of the
three visit measures, being read from nine items on which observers agree
least, and that its signal is absorbed by the two culture scores with which
it is correlated, so that the null on teaching understates what instruction
contributes. Three facts favour the first reading without settling it: the
teaching score alone predicts progress well, so it is not merely noise; the
culture coefficients do not move at all when it enters, where absorption
would show as movement in both directions; and the culture association runs
partly through out-of-lesson warmth, which no measure of classroom teaching
could carry. What would separate the two readings is a measure of teaching
quality independent of the visit, and none exists for these schools;
conditioning on observed teaching quality does not account for the culture
association, and that is the claim made.

\input{tables/tab_stages23_trio}

\textbf{The dimensions add.} \Cref{fig:quadrant_ws} plots the visited schools
by their two culture scores, shaded by progress. Schools in the warm and
strict quadrant are predominantly above-average performers, and the other
three quadrants are all populated, which is what allows the combination to
be tested. Formally, the warm-and-strict quadrant sits $0.37$ of a grade
above the rest of the sample after controls ($p < 0.001$), while the
interaction between the two standardised scores is null ($0.045$, $p = 0.25$;
\cref{tab:robustness_overall}). Warmth and strictness add rather than amplify
one another; the best schools have both because both help, not because of a
combination effect, and a school that is strong on one dimension gains from
the other regardless. Decomposing the visit scores into their five
observational blocks (\cref{tab:subscores}), the association runs through
warmth outside lessons and orderly conduct within them: out-of-lesson warmth
($0.108$, $p = 0.023$) and in-lesson strictness ($0.120$, $p = 0.009$)
survive joint entry, and their counterparts do not. The relational quality
that travels with progress is the one visible in corridors and at break, and
the order that travels with it is the one visible in lessons.

\textbf{Espoused culture does not predict progress.} Substituting the
headteacher's espoused scores for the observed ones on the same schools
reduces the warmth coefficient to $0.055$ ($p = 0.29$); espoused strictness
retains a smaller association ($0.115$, $p = 0.018$). The measurement
asymmetry of the previous chapter reappears as a predictive one: what a
headteacher says about warmth carries no information about progress, and what
they say about behaviour systems carries some. This is the pattern the
previous chapter's finding predicts, since espoused warmth was there shown to
be only weakly related to observed warmth and espoused strictness more
closely related to observed strictness, and it is a check on the observed
measures as much as on the espoused ones: had the espoused scores predicted
progress as well as the observed ones, the visits would have added nothing
that an hour with the headteacher could not supply. Two related results are
in \cref{app:3A:tables}. The gap between a school's espoused and enacted
scores predicts nothing beyond the enacted level; and the association is not
confined to the visited schools' own report on themselves, since parents'
survey responses on their children's happiness and safety are positively
associated with progress ($0.14$ per standard deviation) with the caveat that
satisfaction and results are jointly determined.

\textbf{National extension.} The inspection-report strictness score, which
the previous chapter showed to track observed strictness at
$r = \CorrOfstedStrictness$, is available for \NNationalStrictness{} schools
with an outcome, some thirty times the visited sample. On the full control set
without the grade it carries $\BetaNationalStrictness$ ($p < 0.001$,
$R^2 = 0.52$; \cref{tab:national_strictness}); with the 2019 grade added
($n = 2{,}746$) it carries $0.203$, and no component estimate moves by more
than $0.01$. Two things follow. The first concerns the score itself. A score
read from an inspection report might be thought to proxy for the
inspectorate's overall approval, so that the association is the grade's
association under another name; but the coefficient is untouched by
conditioning on the grade, and a proxy for the grade would lose most of its
coefficient when the grade entered. What the score carries is what the report
says about conduct and enforcement, over and above the verdict, and that is
what predicts progress. The second concerns the visited sample. The visited
schools agreed to be visited, and the previous chapter showed they lean
toward stronger schools; the national coefficient is estimated on every
school with a report and an outcome, and it is of the same order as the
visited strictness coefficient in standard-deviation units, if anything
larger. The strictness result is
therefore not a property of the schools that said yes. What the extension
does not buy is the same reassurance for warmth, since no text source carries
observed warmth, nor identification, since a text-derived measure inherits
the same omitted-variable concern as an observed one. Two further
text-derived results are consistent with the previous chapter's measurement
findings and are reported in \cref{tab:llm_p8_matrix}: the behaviour-policy
warmth score is significantly \emph{negatively} associated with progress, and
the website warmth and strictness scores are not associated with it at all.

\textbf{Robustness.} \Cref{tab:robustness_overall} reports the primary
estimate under alternative specifications, all on the primary sample, and
each addresses a particular doubt. The grade control might be doing the work,
or absorbing part of it: dropping it leaves $0.140$ and $0.127$ ($n = 100$).
Averaging two cohorts might smooth over a result that holds in only one:
the single 2023/24 outcome year gives $0.158$ and $0.126$. Strict schools
might have fewer pupils with social, emotional and mental-health needs, and
gain in measured progress by that route: adding the 2015/16 baseline share
of such pupils, measured before any of the culture was observed, gives
$0.156$ and $0.105$ ($n = 94$), and the sorting channel accounts for under a
tenth of the strictness association (\cref{tab:semh_mechanism}). Progress~8
might be peculiar: on the 2024/25 cohort's Attainment~8, which does not
condition on intake, both dimensions remain significant, on the total of the
four buckets ($1.17$, $1.73$ points) and on the English bucket alone ($0.31$,
$0.22$). All of these leave both coefficients significant at the five per
cent level or better.

Two further checks concern timing and the headteacher. Within-school
Progress~8 is stable across years (mean within-school standard deviation
$0.20$, against a between-school standard deviation of about $0.5$), which
supports using culture measured after the outcome window as a proxy for
culture during it (\cref{tab:stability_p8}), and the 2024/25 pseudo-progress
measure, the one cohort measured contemporaneously with the fieldwork, gives
coefficients within $0.05$ of their real-Progress~8 counterparts
(\cref{tab:p8_proxy}). Restricting to the 22 schools whose headteacher is
confirmed unchanged since the last inspection, so that the culture observed
is the culture the outcome cohorts had, leaves the strictness point estimates
at or above the full-sample values and the warmth estimates imprecise; the
subsample is too small to be informative either way
(\cref{tab:continuity_robustness}).

\textbf{Implications.} Two implications bear on current policy debate and
follow from the pattern of results rather than any single coefficient. The
behaviour debate treats discipline as the active ingredient of school
improvement and relational culture as secondary; these estimates give the two
equal weight, show no trade-off between them, and place the warm-and-strict
schools a third of a grade ahead. A school that tightens its behaviour
systems and lets relationships go is, on these estimates, giving back what it
gains. And the association runs through relational quality outside lessons
and orderly conduct within them, which locates the relational work that
matters outside the classroom, in corridors, at break and at the gate, where
observation and training effort are least concentrated. Both implications
are conditional on an association being at least partly causal, which the
design supports but does not establish.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:p2_conclusion}

Observed school culture predicts pupil progress. Across \NTierOne{} visited
English secondary schools, a standard deviation of observed warmth is
associated with $0.14$ of a grade higher Progress~8 and a standard deviation
of observed strictness with $0.13$, both significant at the one per cent
level and both stable across every reasonable specification. The two
dimensions contribute equally and additively; the schools that combine them
outperform the rest by a third of a grade. Observed teaching quality does not
account for the association. The headteacher's own account of the school
predicts nothing on warmth, and the strictness score read from inspection
reports reproduces the strictness result nationally with the inspection grade
held constant.

Against the literature the paper sits in, the results say three things. To
the school-effects tradition, they give a quantitative reading of two of the
correlates of ethos that Rutter and the effective-schools syntheses named,
measured by observation across a national population rather than a case-study
sample. To the charter and academy literature, they say that the two halves
of the practice bundle, structure and support, each carry weight when
measured apart, and that neither is the whole of the effect. And to the
authoritative-school literature, they supply the academic-progress outcome it
has mostly lacked, and find what the typology predicts: both dimensions
matter, and the schools with both do best.

The estimates are associations, strengthened by a value-added outcome,
temporal ordering, a rich control set and stability across specifications, but
not identified: a persistent characteristic that produces both a school's
culture and its results cannot be excluded with cross-sectional data. The
visited sample is small and leans toward stronger schools; culture was
measured after the outcome window for most schools; and one visit is a noisy
reading of a school. A longitudinal design, with culture observed before the
outcome cohort arrived and again after, would speak to the direction of the
association; a design that observed culture change within schools, as
headteachers changed or behaviour systems were introduced, would speak to
its cause. Within the present limits, the results locate the relational and
disciplinary features of a school that travel with its pupils' progress, and
they show that one of the two, strictness, is legible at national scale in
the public record.

\section{How the Progress 8 Measures Are Calculated}
\label{app:3A:p8mechanics}

Progress~8 measures the progress a pupil makes between the end of primary
school and GCSE, relative to pupils nationally who started from the same
point. Each pupil's GCSE results are counted across eight slots, their
attainment is compared with the national average attainment of pupils with
the same Key Stage~2 starting point, and the difference is averaged across
the school. A school at zero is exactly average; a school at $+0.5$ achieves
half a grade more per subject per pupil than would be expected from its
intake.

The eight slots are structured, and the structure is why this thesis treats
the components differently. English and Maths each fill two slots and are
double-weighted; every pupil sits both, so these components measure progress
and nothing else. The EBaC component covers three slots that must be filled
from a restricted academic list: sciences, computer science, history,
geography, and modern foreign languages. The Open component covers the final
three slots, which may be filled by any approved qualification, including
arts and vocational subjects. For these last six slots the school makes
entry decisions, and an unfilled slot contributes zero progress to the
school's average. Consequently, a school's EBaC and Open components mix two
different things: how much progress pupils make in the subjects they enter,
and which subjects the school enters them for. A school that enters few
pupils for languages will carry empty EBaC slots and a depressed EBaC
component even if the pupils it does enter progress well.

This structure generates a theoretical prediction about warmth and
strictness. Schools higher on strictness, characterised by structured
behaviour management, high academic expectations, and intensive pupil
steering, are more likely to direct pupils into the EBaC pathway, and could
therefore raise their EBaC component through the entry-rate channel as well
as through progress itself. Schools higher on warmth might generate stronger
progress in the more elective Open component. The five-outcome versions of
the main results tables in this appendix speak to that prediction, and the
entry-rate analysis below addresses the pathway channel directly.

The entry rates themselves are calculated from the pupil count columns of
the 2023--24 performance tables, because the Department for Education
stopped publishing percentage-form entry columns in recent releases: the
EBaC entry rate is the number of pupils entered for the full EBaC suite
divided by the number of pupils at the end of Key Stage~4, and the
humanities and languages rates are constructed in the same way from their
respective counts. Entry rates are available only at the subject-group
level; individual subjects within a group cannot be distinguished in the
data collected for this project.

\section{Structural Stability of School Characteristics}
\label{app:3A:stability}

The intra-class correlation (ICC) decomposes total variance in a panel variable
into a between-school component and a within-school over-time component. An ICC
close to one indicates that schools are much more different from each other than
they are from their own past selves: year-on-year within-school fluctuation is
small relative to persistent inter-school heterogeneity. ICC is estimated by
one-way random-effects ANOVA on the school-year panel, retaining only schools
with at least three years of non-missing data for the variable in question.

\Cref{tab:structural_stability} reports ICC estimates and mean absolute
year-on-year changes for nine panel variables covering pupil intake composition,
special educational needs prevalence, prior attainment, pupil progress, and
per-pupil expenditure. Six of nine variables exceed ICC~$= 0.69$; all
exceed~$0.49$. Intake characteristics---the share of pupils with English as an
Additional Language, school roll, and mean KS2 prior attainment of the entering
cohort---are especially stable, with ICC values between 0.81 and 0.92 and mean
small mean absolute year-on-year changes relative to their between-school spread.
Special educational needs prevalence (EHC plan share, ICC~$= 0.67$; SEN support
share, ICC~$= 0.65$) and school-level Progress~8 (ICC~$= 0.73$) show moderate to
high stability. Per-pupil expenditure, at ICC~$= 0.52$, is the most volatile,
reflecting sensitivity to national funding policy changes; even so, the majority
of variation in expenditure is between schools rather than within schools over
time.

These patterns are consistent with the view that schools occupy relatively
persistent positions in the distribution of structural characteristics, and
support the use of culture scores measured in 2024--25 as proxies for the
organisational conditions present during the 2023--24 outcome period.

\Cref{tab:sen_category_stability} extends the analysis to the thirteen
primary-need categories within the SEN population. ICC values range from
0.320 (Severe Learning Difficulty) to 0.643 (Moderate Learning Difficulty);
the educationally important Social, Emotional and Mental Health (SEMH)
category has ICC~$= 0.545$. The lower stability of SEMH relative to the
aggregate SEN support share (ICC~$= 0.65$) is consistent with SEMH composition
being partly responsive to school behaviour and exclusion practices, rather than
being determined solely by fixed intake characteristics. Low ICCs for rare
categories (Severe Learning Difficulty, Multi-Sensory Impairment, Visual
Impairment) partly reflect small-count measurement noise rather than genuine
volatility. For the main analysis, aggregate SEN controls are preferred to
category-level decompositions; the moderate stability of baseline SEMH share
supports its inclusion as an additional control in the robustness specification
of \cref{tab:robustness_overall}.

\Cref{tab:p8_component_stability} reports ICC estimates separately for the four
Progress~8 components that serve as the primary outcomes of this chapter,
together with the corresponding Attainment~8 components used in the
contemporaneous robustness specification. Among the Progress~8 components,
stability varies from ICC~$= 0.587$ for the Open component to ICC~$= 0.748$
for EBaC, with English and Maths sitting between these at ICC~$\approx 0.67$.
The higher stability of EBaC relative to Open reflects the fact that
curriculum-pathway decisions---which subjects pupils are entered for---are
governed by stable school policy and change little from year to year, while
the more eclectic Open bucket (arts, vocational, and additional GCSEs) is
subject to greater annual variation in subject mix. All four P8 components
exceed the per-pupil expenditure ICC of~$0.52$, which is the weakest result
in the main stability table; the cross-sectional design is therefore at least
as defensible for each component individually as for the structural
characteristics already discussed.

The theoretical prediction of this chapter is that warmth is most strongly
associated with the Open component---the dimension most sensitive to breadth
of provision and pupil engagement---and that strictness is most strongly
associated with the EBaC component---the dimension most sensitive to academic
pathway steering. The lower stability of Open P8 relative to EBaC P8
(ICC~$= 0.587$ versus $0.748$) implies that the warmth coefficient will be
estimated against a noisier outcome, which may attenuate the estimated effect.
This is a limitation to acknowledge in interpreting any differential finding
between components.

Attainment~8 component ICCs substantially exceed their Progress~8 counterparts
across all four components, ranging from ICC~$= 0.750$ (Open) to
ICC~$= 0.887$ (EBaC). This pattern is expected: Attainment~8 measures absolute
performance without subtracting KS2 prior attainment, so the stable intake
signal---itself at ICC~$= 0.91$ for the KS2 scaled score---is retained in the
dependent variable and inflates between-school variance relative to
within-school variance. The higher Attainment~8 ICCs therefore do not indicate
that value-added is more stable than raw attainment; they reflect the
composition of the variance rather than the reliability of the school-level
signal.
