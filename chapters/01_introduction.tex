\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro_motivation}

Schools with similar pupils and similar budgets produce very different
results. Some of that difference reflects what schools do rather than who
they enrol, and the features of a school that are most often credited ---
its ethos, its discipline, the opportunities it puts in front of pupils ---
are precisely the ones that are hardest to measure. This thesis measures two
of them for English secondary schools, and asks what each one means for
pupils: the culture of the school, and whether it offers a high-return
subject.

Culture comes first because it is the feature schools are most confident
they have and least able to evidence. Nearly every school describes itself
as warm, supportive and well ordered. The measures available to researchers
and to policy do not settle whether that is true: inspection judgements
bundle culture with attainment, exclusion rates conflate strictness with
disorder, and surveys record what people say rather than what a visitor
would see. The first two papers take a different route. I visited
\NTierOne{} schools, spent a structured day observing each one, and scored
two dimensions of its culture: warmth, the relational quality of the school,
and strictness, its behavioural structure. The two dimensions come from the
parenting literature, where their combination --- demanding and responsive
at once --- is the style that produces the best child outcomes. Separating
them matters because the public debate about schools tends to treat them as
opposites: the case for strict schools is often put as a case against soft
ones. Whether warmth and strictness are substitutes, complements or simply
independent is an empirical question, and answering it requires measuring
each on its own.

Direct observation produces credible scores but cannot scale: no researcher
can visit 3,332 schools. Every school, however, produces text --- an
inspection report written about it, a behaviour policy written by it, a
website written for its parents --- and modern language models can read all
of it. The first paper asks how much of what an observer sees in a school
survives into the text that exists for every school, and the second asks
what the observed scores predict. The answers discipline each other: the
text is only useful where it tracks what schools actually do, and the
visits reveal where it does and does not.

The subject a school offers comes second because it is a sharper object: a
constraint rather than a climate. Whether a pupil can take A-level
economics --- the principal gateway to one of the highest-earning degrees
--- depends on whether their school employs a teacher qualified to teach
it, and fewer than half of English schools do. A pupil at the wrong school
cannot choose what is not offered, whatever their ability or ambition. The
third paper measures what that constraint costs, in earnings foregone and
in who the constraint falls on, and what removing it would return to the
public purse.

The three papers share a method as well as a subject. Each measures
something about schools that was previously observed only in small samples
or not at all --- enacted culture, the legibility of culture in public
text, the availability of a subject --- and each does so at or near the
population of English schools, by combining direct measurement with
administrative data and, in the first two papers, with language models
validated against a gold standard. The thesis is, in that sense, an
argument that features of schools long thought unmeasurable at scale can
now be measured, and that measuring them changes what we know.

\section{Contribution to the Literature}
\label{sec:intro_contribution}

The first paper contributes to two literatures. The school-climate
literature has long used the parenting typology of demandingness and
responsiveness to classify schools, and finds that pupils do best in
schools that are both demanding and responsive; but it measures climate
through surveys of pupils, parents or staff, which record perceptions
rather than practice. The paper's first contribution is a gold standard
built instead from direct observation, with an explicit distinction between
what a school enacts and what its headteacher espouses --- a distinction
the survey literature cannot draw, and which turns out to be large: heads
describe their schools as warmer than observers find them, and the gap is
wider for warmth than for strictness. The second contribution is to the
text-as-data literature: the paper treats the country's stock of school
documents as measurement material, scores it with a prediction model and
with a language model working to a mark scheme, and validates both against
the gold standard. The result is a map of which parts of school culture
are, and are not, written down --- warmth lives in an inspection report's
everyday wording, strictness in specific claims --- which is itself a
finding about what the public record of schooling contains.

The second paper contributes to the literature on school effectiveness. The
best-identified evidence on school culture comes from studies of
high-expectation charter schools in the United States, which estimate the
effect of a bundle: strict discipline, high expectations, extended time,
intensive tutoring. The paper's contribution is to unbundle the two
cultural dimensions and estimate each one's association with progress in
the population of ordinary English state schools, where both dimensions
vary freely. Both matter, with near-equal strength; they add rather than
interact; and observed teaching quality accounts for neither. The paper is
explicit that these are associations, not causal effects --- schools are
not randomly warm --- and its contribution is correspondingly one of
measurement and description: it shows that the dimension policy debates
neglect, warmth, predicts progress as strongly as the dimension they
emphasise, and that this is only visible when warmth is measured by
observation rather than self-report.

The third paper, joint with Arun Advani and Elaine Drayton, contributes to
the literature on field-of-study returns. That literature identifies the
value of a subject from the margin of competitive admission --- pupils just
above and below an entry cut-off. Our contribution is the extensive margin
that precedes any application: whether the subject is available at the
pupil's school at all. Using within-school changes in availability in
linked administrative data covering the population of English pupils, we
show that availability constrains take-up, that the constrained choice has
large earnings consequences, and that the constraint binds hardest outside
London and the South East. The paper then prices the policy lever directly:
because one teacher converts a school from non-offering to offering, the
fiscal return to hiring an economics teacher can be computed and compared
with its cost, in the framework of the marginal value of public funds. The
return exceeds the cost several times over.

Together the papers make one argument in two settings. What a school does
--- the culture it enacts day to day, the subjects it puts on its timetable
--- is measurable, varies widely between otherwise similar schools, and
predicts what its pupils go on to achieve. The tools that make the
measurement possible at scale, language models reading the public record
and population administrative data, are new; the questions are old ones
that could not previously be answered at this scale.

\section{Overview of the Thesis}
\label{sec:intro_overview}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.
\Cref{ch:paper1} develops the measurement framework. It constructs
gold-standard warmth and strictness scores for \NTierOne{} schools from
structured researcher visits and headteacher interviews, and then asks how
far those scores can be recovered from text that exists for every school
--- Ofsted inspection reports, behaviour policies and school websites ---
using a prediction model that learns which words and phrases go with warmer
or stricter schools, and a large language model that scores each document
against a mark scheme. Inspection reports carry a modest signal of both
dimensions, but through different routes: warmth shows in a report's
everyday wording, strictness in specific claims a reader must be told to
look for. Schools' own documents track what headteachers say about their
schools rather than what observers record there.

\Cref{ch:paper2} uses the observed scores as explanatory variables. It
estimates the association between school culture and pupil progress between
Key Stage~2 and Key Stage~4, and shows that warmth and strictness predict
Progress~8 with near-equal magnitude, that observed teaching quality does
not account for either association, and that warmth predicts progress only
when it is measured by observation: replacing the observers' scores with
the headteachers' own ratings removes the warmth association, while
strictness is associated with progress under either measure. Culture scores
read from inspection reports extend both associations to effectively all
English state secondary schools.

\Cref{ch:paper3}, joint work with Arun Advani and Elaine Drayton, turns
from culture to curriculum. It shows that whether an English school offers
A-level economics depends on whether it employs a suitably qualified
teacher, uses within-school changes in provision to estimate the effect of
taking the subject on degree choice and earnings, and computes the fiscal
return to expanding the supply of economics teachers, which exceeds the
cost of a teacher several times over.
